# Resources' open questions

In order of what each would settle for the whole Open Moon. Each names what decides it and how it could be answered
on this laptop. The findings behind them are in the [README](README.md).

1. **The nitrogen's extraction plan.** On 9 October the author asked for the extraction to be planned in detail:
   feasible, sustainable, and protecting the major celestial bodies as the conservation study's principle 5 plans.
   The air needs 2.5×10¹⁸ kg of N2. Ordinary icy bodies hold their nitrogen bound, at a few tenths of a percent to a
   few percent of their mass, and by a rough count the Kuiper belt's small bodies alone hold 80–800 times the need.
   - **What decides it:**
     - which populations supply it (Kuiper-belt bodies, Centaurs, Jupiter-family and long-period comets, Jupiter's
       trojans, carbonaceous asteroids), with their nitrogen content, mass, reach and delivery energy;
     - the share of each population taken, small enough that no population and no unique body is used up;
     - whether the bodies mined for the water bring the nitrogen with them;
     - the chemistry at the source: ammonia cracked to N2, ammonium salts and organics broken down, the hydrogen kept
       there;
     - the source industry's size, power and heat.
   - **How:** a census of the candidate populations from the literature, with nitrogen content, mass and the velocity
     change to reach the Moon; a joint balance of nitrogen, hydrogen, oxygen and carbon across the import streams
     with the water (question 2); then a plan by population and century over the 500-year build, with its pipeline,
     processing and the traffic rules. The choice among the populations, and any change to the principles, is the
     author's.
2. **The array's population and its habitats' shielding.** At the Stanford torus's 990 t a person, the habitats
   are the largest material stream after the build: 99–990 Gt for 0.1–1 billion residents, 2,000–14,000 Gt for
   2–14 billion ([industry](industry.md)).
   - **What decides it:** the author's placement of people; passive shield mass per area against water or magnetic
     shields; the floor area each person has.
   - **How:** shield designs from the literature by mass per person, with the provisioning domain's population
     scenarios.
3. **The redox account of the imports.** Each import stream carries oxidizing or reducing power:
   - oxygen as water leaves hydrogen that would burn all of it back;
   - nitrogen as ammonia carries hydrogen needing seven times the air's oxygen;
   - organic carbon draws oxygen.

   **How:** a ledger of O2 equivalents per stream beside its mass. Decide where the hydrogen stays (split at the
   source, cracked to N2 there, bound as water in the seas) and in what form the oxygen arrives. This is the
   conservation study's open question on the form of imported oxygen and the use of its hydrogen.
4. **Where deliveries put their energy.** The fall into the Moon's well is 1.2–2.7 MJ/kg, which over a 500-year build
   is 35–349 W/m² if it ends as heat on the Moon. The upper air's exobase is sensitive to deposited energy.

   **How:** delivery schemes compared by where they dissipate: aerobraking low, regenerative lowering on tethers or
   an elevator, delivery as ice to chosen places. Add an arrival rule in the spirit of S7, and size the build's
   duration against the climate during construction. Arithmetic first, then the atmosphere domain's loss response
   for the energy that reaches the upper air.
5. **The shield's material life cycle.** The film plant remakes 2.3–4.6 Gt a year. Its recovery fraction sets a fresh
   feed of 73–1,470 kg/s, and nothing yet sets it. Also open:
   - the film's life, against 26–53 lunar cross-sections of new film a year;
   - the plant's site;
   - where failed tiles go;
   - where fresh feed comes from: lunar rock while the Moon is airless, then recycling or small bodies.

   **How:** with the planned `research/array-industry` branch, a stock-and-flow model of the fleet's materials with
   damage rates (protons, meteoroids) and recovery steps.
6. **The carbon cycle as a box model.** The parts are:
   - air, plants, soils with their carbonates, and the seas' dissolved carbon;
   - burial in lakes and seas;
   - the kilns and where their lime goes.

   It needs the corrected runoff and climate, weathering that slows as soils form, and the magnesite route. The CO2
   study names it as next work.

   **How:** a box model through the first thousands of years, run in seconds.
7. **The oxygen budget against the ground.** Ferrous and metallic iron and sulfides take oxygen as the surface
   weathers, and organic burial returns it. The industrial architecture's step "establish the final oxygen regime"
   needs its size and timing.

   **How:** the same box model, with an oxidation front in the soils and the regolith's iron by terrane.
8. **The seas' composition through time.** The delivered water's salts are a design lever: fresh seas for millennia,
   or salted at delivery. Weathering, evaporation in closed lakes and salt works follow.

   **How:** a chemical model of the seas and lakes with the delivered composition as scenarios, beside the ecology
   work's living waters.
9. **A geochemical map of the Moon's rock.** Elements by region on the atlas, separating dry ground from the flooded
   high-titanium basalts and KREEP. That sets what can be mined before flooding, and where the summit's steel and the
   shield's titania come from.

   **How:** published orbital maps (Lunar Prospector, Kaguya) resampled to the atlas grid, with their data rights
   reviewed.
10. **How much water the crust takes.** Saturating the pores to 1–20 km moves the import from 1.4 to 7.3×10¹⁹ kg.

   **How:** with geography, the crust's permeability and the time to saturate.
11. **Micronutrients and critical elements, element by element.**
    - For the biomes: molybdenum and vanadium first, then boron, cobalt, copper, zinc, selenium and iodine.
    - For the array's hardware: germanium, indium, copper and rare earths.

    **How:** a table of each element's need against lunar rock and asteroid supply.
12. **Lift gas and its hydrogen.** Hydrogen's safety at scale, or a helium supply; regolith helium as a by-product of
    soil processing; and helium's escape, a loss term the escape budget does not yet list.
13. **What weathering releases.** Mercury, chromium and nickel from fresh rock, and acid waters where iron-rich
    drainage oxidizes.
14. **Earth's share.** The conservation principle weighs the project's effects on Earth as part of its costs. The
    ledger should carry them.
