# Aerophytes in agriculture, living area and the economy

31 October 2026. **Lesser aerophytes and colonies of small sky-reef modules are the strongest
candidates for deliberate cultivation.** Their mature hosts have more carbon left for a new
crop per projected area after upkeep and replacement than round giants do, and a modular
colony offers repeated harvests without rebuilding an entire lifting organism. Large colonies
and round giants also provide space: living surfaces and conditional load
capacity for other biomass, gardens, homes and small communities. That service can be
valuable even when the host's own harvested output is modest. Materials can be recovered
during renewal or at the end of life while the host continues supporting its inhabitants.

These are provisioning proposals based on the ecology models, rather than selected crops,
proven edible organisms or a change to the planned food blend. Agricultural value includes
propagation, feedstocks and materials as well as food. Economic value also includes the
services a living community provides while it remains intact. The accompanying
[calculation](aerophytes.py) and [product](results/aerophytes.json) separate recurring host
expenditure from a possible new product allocation; [inputs](aerophytes_inputs.json) and
[sources](aerophytes_sources.json) record their basis.

## Which forms suit which work

| Form | Agricultural opportunities | Other economic opportunities | Principal qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lesser aerophytes, G02; representative 100-m round body | Purpose-grown storage organs or secretions; nursery stock; small managed crop units | Membrane collection, separately grown fibre or other material organs | Product-bearing forms need their own wet mass, gas, nutrient and harvest budgets; food chemistry is unestablished |
| Sky reefs, G03; colonies of 60-m modules | Farm colonies with separate green, nursery and service modules; attached epiphytes and phototrophic films | Shed membrane, material cultivation, propagule production, gardens, dwellings and community space | The colony's modules share carbon and loads; no ordinary farm deck or heavy soil bed is already provided |
| Round giants, G03; representative 1-km strong-fibre body | Limited specialty products and cultivated tenants on suitable surfaces | Living area for gardens and dwellings; habitat stewardship, observation and tourism; renewed or retired structure | Long establishment and substantial tendon renewal reduce the freely allocatable annual carbon |
| Small 8–15 m bodies in the food-web study | Experimental specialist forage or whole-body crops | Propagules and ecological restoration stock | Rebuilding gas, envelopes and reserves consumes most construction carbon; smallness does not make them efficient annual crops |
| G50 epiphytes and G51 underside films, associated with the hosts | Candidate herbs, ornamentals, leafy/algal crops, pigments or other specialty compounds | Nursery services, biological collections and material cultures | Their independent fixation is additional to the host account only after their own light, nutrient, water and shading effects are calculated |

The proposed products do not follow automatically from the taxonomic names. A storage pod,
exudate organ or cultivated film is a design to investigate. Suitable composition,
digestibility, processing and harvest response would distinguish a food cultivar from a
material cultivar. A microbial or fungal culture supplied with host sugars converts the
host's production; it does not add independent primary production.

## How much carbon could a crop receive?

The sky-ecology product already pays for maintenance, photolytic replacement of lost hydrogen,
night gas substrate, tissue and structural renewal, and 0.1 kg C/m²/year of sugar for tenants.
Population replacement costs the adult mortality rate times whole-body construction, including
the new gas and reserves. The remainder can be allocated among growth, sailing, additional
repair, tenants and a cultivated product.

For the three representative bodies, the screen uses the inherited gross fixation of
2 kg C/m²/year and photolytic gas, with an adult mortality rate of 0.003/year. The trial crop
receives 25–50% of the remaining assimilate. New tissue retains about 81% of its construction
carbon, using the host model's construction cost, and contains 45% carbon by dry mass. The
result is **generic dry organic tissue before processing**, with no assumed edible fraction.
This is an allocation ceiling before extra crop-maintenance respiration, night storage,
decay, supporting tissue, gas for the wet load and harvest-wound repair. A designed crop
must pay those costs from the allocation, as well as collection and processing losses.
All areas below are horizontal projected hectares.

| Mature representative | Carbon available before new allocation, kg C/m²/year | New dry tissue at 25–50% allocation, t/ha/year | With gross fixation reduced by 10%, t/ha/year |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Lesser, 100 m | 0.927 | 4.2–8.3 | 3.3–6.5 |
| Reef module, 60 m | 0.853 | 3.8–7.7 | 2.9–5.9 |
| Round giant, 1 km | 0.267 | 1.2–2.4 | 0.3–0.6 |

This identifies a useful allocation margin in the lesser bodies and small reef modules.
Across the source's adult mortality range of 0.001–0.005/year, their unallocated carbon is
0.903–0.951 and 0.832–0.874 kg C/m²/year respectively; the giant's is 0.150–0.383. The giant
makes considerable biomass, but much of it renews the structure that keeps it airborne.
Annual structural renewal is about 0.33 kg dry/m² in the lesser body, 0.53 in the reef module
and 1.27 in the giant. All three renew 0.50 kg dry/m²/year of host green tissue.

The light reductions are prescribed sensitivities, with gross fixation scaled linearly and
fixed upkeep retained. They are not predictions for a particular coverage, altitude or the
final 301 K climate. At a 20% reduction the representative giant cannot pay its full fixed
programme at the central mortality rate; the calculation credits no crop in that case.
Lesser bodies and reef modules retain allocation margins. Real shade acclimation, directional
light and changing tissue investment could change these responses.

The ecology branch's richer-sky estimate remains provisional because its new mutual-shading
column does not resolve the Moon's strong multiple scattering. This provisioning account
therefore avoids multiplying a per-area crop by an assumed global cover. Existing tenant-light
calculations do support photosynthesis on flanks and undersides: at 10 km an isolated underside
receives 19–30% of top horizontal illumination, and a reef underside 11–22%, over the stated
ground-albedo range. A separate crop model must establish how much production those surfaces
can supply without impairing their host or the community below.

## Ways to cultivate rather than exhaust the host

**Recurring product organs are the first agricultural candidate.** A cultivated lesser body
or reef module could devote a defined share of its residual carbon to detachable storage
organs, a collectable secretion or sacrificial material growth. Harvest would preserve the
gas envelope, loaded tendons, photosynthetic tissue and night stores. The generic tissue
calculation supplies a starting carbon budget, while each product needs its own composition
and synthesis cost. The existing tenant sugar is already committed to the food web; tapping
it would change that food web rather than recover an unused output.

**Attached crops offer another route, especially for specialty horticulture.** Lightweight
supports on illuminated flanks or fringes could hold cultivated epiphytes or phototrophic
films with managed water and nutrient return. Some crops might be more valuable for their
propagules, aromas, pigments or appearance than for bulk calories. Earth experiments on
orchid propagation show that managing compatible fungi can materially affect successful
seedling production; that is a precedent for nursery expertise, not a lunar yield estimate
([Yang, Li and Gao, 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2023.1114105/full)).

The crop also needs its own response to the long light and dark intervals. An attached herb
or algal culture does not acquire the host's night tolerance merely by living on it. Storage,
metabolic slowing, supplementary supply or a suitably timed harvest must be assessed for
the crop's actual drifting day; the fixed reference host budget does not establish those traits.

**Nurseries can sell living capacity.** The older growth screen brings suitable juveniles
through canopy nurseries in roughly 1–7 years; founding reef modules take roughly 2–8 years.
At the reference light with photolytic gas and all surplus invested in growth, a 2-km colony
takes about 83 years to grow from its founding module, against 226 years for a 1-km round
giant from its juvenile. Cultivar maintenance, juvenile production, replacement modules and
restoration could therefore be useful activities. A farm selling crops and propagules must
split its allocation between them; the same surplus cannot fund both at their full rates.

**Wild harvesting is a different account.** The earlier resource screen collected 5–20% of
annual living-tissue renewal and described the result as a food garnish. That calculation
used 0.55 kg dry/m²/year including its older living-community convention; the current host
food-web ledger uses 0.50 kg dry of green host tissue with tenants separate. Neither proves
edibility or a sustainable harvesting rate. Cutting active foliage can remove future carbon
income and nutrients, while collecting senescent tissue may yield a poor food. The wild-tissue
illustration does not determine what a purpose-bred perennial crop could allocate.

Whole-body cropping is less attractive in the existing designs. In the small 8–15 m bodies,
only 5–8% of photolytic construction carbon becomes the living tissue counted as edible in
the source scenario. Repeatedly rebuilding the lifting machinery is a large cost. Cultivation
of selected organs would preserve that machinery between harvests.

## Materials and services

**Shed membrane is the most developed material opportunity in the existing work.** A 2-km
colony's old resource scenario yields about 38,000–570,000 m²/year of collected film, assuming
renewal every 10–30 years and recovery of 10–50%. Those are design assumptions, and the result
is membrane area rather than certified gas-cell fabric. Permeability, strength, fatigue,
storage and joining after collection remain open. Repair patches, packaging or composite
feedstock may accept material that fails the much stricter demands of lifting-gas containment.
Active gas barriers must remain continuous; a harvest method needs to establish that a new
barrier is functioning before removing the old material.

**Fibres and other grown materials are plausible, with product qualification deciding the
use.** Retired tendon material and deliberately grown sacrificial fibres could supply textiles,
cordage or composite feedstocks. Loaded tendons are essential structure, so the giant's large
standing stock cannot be counted as a recurring harvest. Biological cellulose already has
experimental processing routes into spun fibres and knitted fabric
([Silva et al., 2024](https://acris.aalto.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/150975091/CHEM_Silva_et_al_Upcycling_of_cellulosic_2024_Int_J_Biol_Macromol.pdf));
this supports investigating the material pathway while leaving aerophyte quality and cost
unestablished. Fermentation-grown cellulose and manufactured films are relevant competitors.

**Living services may exceed the value of harvested mass, particularly for giants.** Wet
pools, litter capture, roosts and guard communities help keep the host supplied and functioning.
Earth's seabird–reef studies demonstrate productive links maintained by animal nutrient
transport ([Graham et al., 2018](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/128983/1/Graham_et_al._Nature_2018_accepted.pdf)).
Returning flyers could serve an analogous function here, subject to the sky's own nutrient
account. Removing guano, litter or guards can reduce that service. Nutrient capture redistributes
the Moon's existing inventory; selling it does not create new phosphorus.

Research, viewing and carefully managed visits offer direct human uses of the habitat. A
visitor ship can carry its own weight while observing a giant, avoiding the assumption that
the living surface is already a passenger platform. Lightweight instruments and specialised
nursery attachments may be suitable tenants. Tourism revenue, visitor capacity and operating
costs have not been estimated; ecological and cultural value need not depend on a commercial
visitor operation.

## Living area, homes and small communities

Providing space for other life is a major use in its own right. The existing geometry gives
a round body four square metres of physical surface per projected square metre and a reef
about 3.63. A 2-km reef therefore has roughly **11.4 km² of physical surface over a 3.14 km²
projected footprint**, divided among tops, flanks, contacts and undersides. Those surfaces
offer different light and moisture conditions for gardens, nurseries, pools, films and other
communities. Physical surface is not automatically walkable floor or productive cropland;
built living areas would occupy selected portions while the host keeps its productive tissue
and the other organisms keep their habitat.

The existing carrying allowances make a small home or community worth pursuing quantitatively.
For a deliberately simple illustration, combine three green 60-m modules with each specialised
60-m tenant module, or domatium. Use the domatium with its existing tenant-food allocation:
it has a conditional steady additional payload of 34.1 tonnes and a routine carbon deficit
of 0.535 kg C/m²/year. The green modules' residual carbon, after their own upkeep and adult
replacement at the central mortality rate, pays that deficit with about **21%** of their
combined residual. This expenditure competes with the crop allocations discussed above.

| Illustrative cluster | Sum of projected module area | Conditional tenant payload, domatia only | Routine domatia support |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 domatia and 9 green modules | 3.74 ha | 102 t | 5.0 t C/year |
| 30 domatia and 90 green modules | 37.4 ha | 1,023 t | 50.0 t C/year |

For comparison, the [inhabited-volume study](../research/studies/inhabited_volume/README.md)
uses whole-district inventories of 20, 50 and 100 tonnes per resident. These include buildings,
people and furnishings, water and food stocks, and local utilities; the buoyant carrier is
separate. A four-person home at the light inventory requires 80 tonnes, within the smaller
cluster's conditional payload. A community of sixteen at the central inventory requires
800 tonnes, within the larger cluster's allowance. These are plausible mass comparisons,
not resident capacities or completed dwellings. The calculation deliberately adds none of
the green modules' own tenant payload, and it does not infer usable floor area from the
colony's footprint.

The 21% carbon figure pays routine domatia operation. Establishment and replacement of the
domatia and additional hydrogen to access their payload margins still require accounts. The
residential inventories already include generic building and floor mass; detailed floor,
connection and control designs must fit those allowances, or their excess must be deducted
from the remaining payload. A home must distribute its weight
through engineered attachments or grown supporting tissues; local loads cannot be justified
solely by the colony's total buoyancy. Construction, supplies and harvest also change trim.
Human food, water, energy, sanitation, movement and access must be supplied even when the
host's own metabolism closes. Returning treated nutrients could make the settlement a useful
partner to its host, with the transfer and treatment designed explicitly.

Round giants also deserve residential study. The 1-km strong-fibre reference has a theoretical
steady tenant allowance near 335,000 tonnes; accessing the whole allowance would require a
large hydrogen investment over centuries in the source's allocation cases. A small home
would use a tiny part. The relevant questions for that home concern local support, the gas
needed for its actual mass, pressure control and access, rather than treating a giant as
categorically suitable only for instruments or visitors.

This suggests two distinct agricultural enterprises: cultivating the aerophyte for a recurring
product, and cultivating other organisms in the space it maintains. An inhabited reef could
combine homes, gardens, nurseries and managed material collection. The proportions should be
designed around its light, carbon, nutrient and load accounts rather than assuming every
surface must become a crop or every nominal payload margin must become housing.

## What determines whether these uses pay

The principal economic comparison is with a ground farm, a farm in an engineered sky town,
a fermentation plant or manufactured material. A cultivated aerophyte could gather sunlight
and maintain its own lifting structure close to aerial customers. Its costs include years of
establishment, water and nutrient delivery, weather-dependent access, collection machinery,
processing, loss and continued care of a living host. Modularity may help isolate harvests
and replace individual crop units, but no managed farm operation has yet been sized.

Buoyancy makes the harvest schedule consequential. A 60-m green module's conditional steady
tenant allowance is about 10.5 tonnes, whereas its static trim mass equivalent is only about
0.14 tonnes. The larger allowance requires extra hydrogen and construction expenditure; it
is not immediately free payload. The existing dynamic calculation permits only about 41–50%
of the static equivalent for a passive sudden load under its specified assumptions. Removing
crop also changes buoyancy. Gradual, distributed transfers to independently buoyant collection
craft and explicit trim control are candidates to test. Annual dry yield alone gives neither
peak wet crop mass nor a safe transfer size.

Specialised tenant modules demonstrate the tradeoff. A 60-m domatium has a conditional steady
tenant allowance near 34 tonnes but consumes about 0.43–0.53 kg C/m²/year, supplied under the
source's allocation cases by about 1.6–6 green modules. Water-retaining beds, irrigation and
service installations therefore spend part of the same carbon surplus used in the crop table.
Their costs must be deducted before quoting a realised farm yield.

Harvest also establishes a return route for nutrients. For an illustrative leaflike product
containing the source's 1–3 g P/kg dry, a harvest of 4–8 t/ha/year exports **4–24 kg P/ha/year**.
At the existing 99% food-phosphorus return assumption, approximately 3.96–23.76 kg must travel
back upward, with 0.04–0.24 kg unrecovered. The small final loss does not remove the need to
circulate the larger flow, and this crop loop is additional to the host's ordinary needs.
A sugar product may export less phosphorus; a protein-rich product needs an explicit nitrogen
budget. Neither can be assigned the leaflike nutrient content without checking its composition.

A useful economic test is annualised establishment, care, collection, processing and nutrient
return cost divided by delivered saleable output, compared with the alternative supplier.
The present model supplies only a carbon-allocation input to that calculation. Processing
recovery, edible fraction, prices, demand and service revenue remain unspecified. Specialty
products and local supply to aerial settlements are sensible first comparisons because they
can carry more collection cost per unit mass; this is a research priority, not a profitability
finding. Bulk fuel and removal of operating lifting hydrogen are weak starting candidates:
they spend the same valuable carbon or lift needed to maintain the producer. A dedicated
gas-producing cultivar would be a separate energy-conversion proposal, compared with
electrolysis and other gas supplies; the present operating inventory is not its annual yield.

The first useful trials are therefore a managed lesser-body crop, a reef module growing a
removable product, and a shed-membrane qualification programme. Each would test a repeatable
harvest cycle with its water, nutrient return, wet load and host recovery measured together.
A small-home cluster and a larger inhabited colony are equally useful design cases for the
space the organisms maintain, with gardens and ecological habitat included from the start.
They would establish how to inhabit that space while preserving the living systems that
support it.

## Reproduction and evidence

Run `python -m provisioning.aerophytes` for an ignored run product, or pass
`--output provisioning/results/aerophytes.json` to regenerate the recorded product. Run
`python -m pytest provisioning/tests/test_aerophytes.py` for its accounting checks.
The [check record](aerophytes_checks.json) distinguishes numerical tests, source access and
substantive review from biological or economic validation. No crop, price, wild harvest quota,
food-blend allocation or global sky coverage is adopted by this assessment.
