# Local and beamed power for the enclosed programme

*13 November 2026. Engineering alternatives for a 2041 industrial start and subsequent expansion. Conditional equipment and delivery costs; no selected utility design or revised financial returns.*

## Power choice before Open Moon

Local generation deserves a primary place in developing the enclosed programme. Solar electricity and direct solar process heat can support early productive facilities. Continuous industrial and inhabited service warrants a competition among fission, solar with storage, combinations of those systems, and power transmitted from orbit. **A ten-station beaming network is not a prerequisite for first production.** Its use must follow from the requirements and economics of an actual deployment.

The existing [energy comparison](../energy_options.md) and [power study](../power.md) address Open Moon, where atmospheric heating, reduced sunlight under a shield and shared infrastructure change the choice. Those conditions do not establish a preferred power supply for communities and factories in vacuum. The comparison here gives beaming no atmospheric cooling or shield credit. It also preserves the [enclosed feasibility assessment](../../research/studies/enclosed_feasibility/README.md) as a calculation of the inherited architecture, while reopening that architecture's surface power choice.

Under the middle engineering assumptions, fission costs less than the inherited beam supply. Beaming nevertheless competes well against imported equipment that must store electricity through an ordinary lunar night. Solar thermal storage becomes a serious alternative when local material can provide the bulk store and sufficiently light containment works. These results justify developing local alternatives; they do not establish that any proposed plant is qualified.

The boundary is lunar surface electricity within the planned off-Earth economic zone served by **U**, the established general economic unit, potentially a currency or basket of U-related currencies. Remote asteroid-source facilities and depots retain their separately modeled supplies. Open Moon, the habitat array and outer-system construction remain outside the comparison. This report supports engineering selection and subsequent economic integration; it assumes familiarity with the staged industrial programme, but defines the electricity and cost measures needed to compare its alternatives.

## Equal electrical service and distinct costs

The principal comparison supplies **1,000 MW of mean delivered electricity with 1,500 MW of specified capacity**. Results are divided by mean output to make them readable. Thus a cost expressed per MW of mean service describes a large installation; it is not a quotation for a standalone 1 MW plant. The specified capacity allowance is retained across alternatives, without adding another generic reserve multiplier.

An ordinary site receives tracking sunlight for half the 708.734-hour synodic cycle, followed by **354.367 hours of darkness**. The model retains photovoltaic output derating and local electrical losses. Terrain, seasonal shadows, equipment failures and correlated outages require a later site simulation. A separate synthetic polar case has about 85% illumination and a 100-hour principal shadow; it illustrates the value of shorter storage duration without claiming that a surveyed site meets those conditions or moving the existing programme to the poles.

The alternatives share the occupied facilities' electricity demand, ordinary local distribution, use-side heat rejection and the existing **72-hour critical reserve at 3.5 kW per resident**. These common costs remain outside the incremental comparison. Night-cycle storage is additional: an emergency reserve cannot also be fully committed to routine darkness. Specified plant capacity and a balanced energy cycle alone do not establish the probability of uninterrupted service.

Equipment costs are in **constant USD2026**. The reference delivery surcharge is **1,000 USD2026/kg to the surface** and **500 USD2026/kg to the relevant orbit**. Low and high delivery cases use 500/250 and 4,000/2,000 USD2026/kg, respectively. These are comparative assumptions, not transport quotations or replacements for the programme's coupled freight account. Local equipment delivery costs 20 USD2026/kg on the surface or 200 USD2026/kg to orbit. Hardware and fluid inventories are compared at 100%, 50% and 10% imported mass; the thermal storage medium is always procured locally, at its own bulk-material rate.

Factory prices remain the same when equipment is made locally. This deliberately separates avoided import freight from uncertain manufacturing improvements. It does not assume that a lunar factory can already manufacture 90% of a reactor, fuel cell or transmitter. Each design needs its own qualified local products, imported components and delivered assembly cost.

Initial costs include recurring equipment production and delivery. Levelized costs divide discounted purchases, replacements, upkeep and fuel allowances by discounted delivered electricity, using a **3% real discount rate** over 20, 100 or 200 years. Generators have a common accounting split of 30% active equipment renewed after 20 years and 70% structure after 40 years. Batteries and active regenerative fuel-cell equipment last 10 years; tanks last 30 years. These are comparison assumptions, not demonstrated service lives. An inventory of water or thermal medium survives equipment renewal, with explicit annual makeup of 0.1% and 0.5%, respectively.

Option-specific development, detailed installation, large transmission works, reactor decommissioning and a complete fuel supply chain remain unpriced. A fission fuel/operations allowance of 30 USD2026/MWh and annual upkeep of 2% of equipment price do not settle those obligations. The resulting levelized costs are partial engineering estimates rather than all-inclusive utility tariffs.

## Six supply alternatives

| Supply | Treatment in the comparison | Principal development requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Beamed solar | Inherited transmitter, capture and receiver efficiencies; ten stations with 20% useful capacity; receiver hardware; complete orbital assembly mass | Qualified apertures, pointing, thermal control, useful orbital coverage and safe receiver sites |
| Solar and batteries | Tracking photovoltaic panels, usable battery energy, converters and storage thermal control | Affordable long-duration storage, durable cells and replacement logistics |
| Solar and regenerative fuel cells | Panels, electrolysers, fuel cells, balance of plant, hydrogen/oxygen/water tanks, conserved fluid inventory and radiators | Closed-cycle operation through the lunar night, low leakage and maintainable tanks and machinery |
| Solar thermal and local heat storage | Concentrators, local bulk medium, containment and insulation allowance, heat engine and radiator | Recoverable high-temperature heat after a full night, repeated cycling and practical containment |
| Half fission and half solar with fuel cells | Fission supplies half the mean load; solar and regenerative storage supply the remainder | Qualification of both supplies and their control system; the 50% share is an example, not an optimized mix |
| Fission | Complete generating-system mass and factory price, electrical delivery loss and fuel/operations allowance | A deployable reactor at the required scale, shielding, radiators, fuel, repair and retirement arrangements |

A regenerative fuel cell electrolyses water during surplus generation and recombines hydrogen and oxygen during darkness. In the reference calculation, 55% fuel-cell efficiency and 85% electrolyser efficiency, both on hydrogen's higher-heating-value basis, give **46.75% round-trip efficiency**. One kilogram of hydrogen accompanies eight kilograms of oxygen; their combined water-equivalent inventory is counted once. Tanks for all operating states are still needed. The model therefore avoids both a continuing fuel-burn charge for a closed cycle and a fictitious supply of free atmospheric oxygen.

[NASA's regenerative-storage assessment](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220005243/downloads/RFC%20for%20Lunar%20Surface%20Applications%20SPW%2004062022.pdf) supports the importance of long discharge duration, thermal control and fluid-handling equipment in this comparison. Its components and targets do not establish a flight-qualified integrated system. NASA's [2026 testing report](https://www.nasa.gov/general/nasa-regenerative-fuel-cell-testing/) describes initial testing in 2025 and further closed-loop work planned for 2026. The development path is credible, but vacuum operation and sustained maintainability remain material conditions.

Solar thermal storage changes the mass problem. The reference case requires **7,771 tonnes of local medium per MW of mean electrical service**, plus approximately **252 tonnes of collecting, storing, converting and rejecting-heat equipment**. It assumes 0.20 kWh of recoverable thermal capacity per kilogram before holding losses, 80% cycle-equivalent retention, a 30% heat engine, and containment/insulation mass equal to 2% of the medium. Local medium procurement and movement cost 1.20 USD2026/kg together. The model never charges Earth delivery against this bulk material.

Those thermal assumptions need a temperature-dependent design. Heat transport, falling discharge temperature, insulation, refractory compatibility and fatigue could change both recoverable energy and containment mass. [NASA's regolith thermal-storage analysis](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19920005262) establishes a mathematical precedent for examining heat retention and repeated cycles; it does not demonstrate a utility supplying electricity for 354 hours. The [2006 lunar-fission study](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20070001535/downloads/20070001535.pdf) judged high-temperature regolith storage impractical because of the required heat-distribution and retrieval network. That objection remains a design task: a scalar retention factor cannot resolve it. Direct solar heat for suitable industrial reactions may avoid the heat engine and reduce electrical demand separately. [NASA's CaRD work](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/johnson/sunlight-extracts-oxygen-from-regolith-using-solar-chemistry/) supports investigating that route, subject to each process's temperature and product requirements.

## Comparative costs and development uncertainty

The following results use the middle engineering assumptions, the ordinary site and unchanged continuous demand. All mass except thermal bulk material is imported in the first cost column. The second assumes 90% local supply of equipment and fluid mass. Neither assumption is assigned a probability.

| Supply | Initial equipment and delivery, million USD2026 per MW mean, imported | Same cost with 90% local equipment/fluid mass | 100-year levelized cost, USD2026/MWh, imported |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Beamed solar | 169.4 | 96.4 | 1,110 |
| Solar and batteries | 2,278.8 | 511.8 | 30,779 |
| Solar and regenerative fuel cells | 763.2 | 185.1 | 5,029 |
| Solar thermal and local storage | 334.0 | 111.9 | 2,160 |
| Half fission and half solar with fuel cells | 436.9 | 113.0 | 2,889 |
| Fission | 110.5 | 40.9 | 749 |

The long night drives the high battery result: every MW of continuous service needs approximately **354 MWh of usable cyclic storage**, before outages and emergency reserves. Regenerative storage lowers that mass, but tanks, water and conversion equipment remain costly to deliver. The reference hybrid reduces storage proportionately without beating fission on these unit costs. Its potential value is supply diversity and staged deployment; that reliability benefit requires an outage model before it can be priced.

The three engineering bundles vary hardware mass, price, conversion efficiency and storage performance together. They are transparent development stresses, **not confidence bounds or a forecast that all improvements arrive together**. The complete assumptions are in [the input manifest](enclosed_power_inputs.json). Selected values show why the ordering changes:

| Input | Development target | Middle assumption | High burden |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Surface photovoltaic equipment, kg/kW rated | 5 | 10 | 25 |
| Photovoltaic equipment, USD2026/kW rated | 1,000 | 3,000 | 10,000 |
| Usable battery energy, kWh/kg | 0.30 | 0.18 | 0.10 |
| Complete fission equipment, kg/kW electric | 15 | 50 | 250 |
| Fission equipment, USD2026/kW electric | 5,000 | 20,000 | 80,000 |
| Thermal-store energy retained | 90% | 80% | 60% |
| Thermal-store hardware mass / medium mass | 0.5% | 2% | 5% |
| Heat-engine efficiency | 40% | 30% | 20% |
| Beam assembly mass and price relative to inherited values | 0.5 | 1 | 2 |

With the reference freight charges and imported hardware, initial fission costs span **31.6–521.1 million USD2026/MW mean** across these bundles; beams span **100.4–307.2 million**; solar thermal spans **61.5–3,373.9 million**. The development-target thermal system beats the corresponding beam system. In the high-burden bundle, beaming beats the heavier reactor and storage alternatives. Thus the thermal design deserves serious work, while the available assumptions cannot justify selecting it universally.

Fission's middle mass of 50 kg/kW is a **development assumption**, not current lunar performance. A [NASA 40 kW deployable concept](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220004670/downloads/40%20kW%20Deployable%20FSP%20Paper_FINAL.pdf) budgets 10,046 kg for its generating system, control and cable, approximately 251 kg/kW, with mobility listed separately. A much larger [NASA 10 MW concept](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20070001535/downloads/20070001535.pdf) reports a calculated 43,170 kg system with a different conversion arrangement and shielding scope. These concepts show why reactor scale matters; neither qualifies the 50 kg/kW lunar installation assumed here. Fifteen years allows development and trials, but a heavier qualified plant could reverse the apparent saving.

For the middle fission mass, the initial factory-price ceiling that matches inherited beams is approximately **57,300 USD2026/kW electric** with imported equipment, versus the assumed 20,000. At roughly 250 kg/kW, reference surface delivery alone exceeds the inherited beam comparison even with a free reactor. Local manufacture, transport cost and reactor mass must therefore be evaluated together.

Beaming has its own crossover. Holding the middle hardware coefficients fixed, its initial cost falls below the middle fission case when useful constellation capacity exceeds approximately **35%**, compared with the inherited 20%. With 90% local equipment mass for both options, the threshold rises to approximately **51%**. Useful capacity is the fraction of total rated generating/transmitting capacity that can serve the load after orbital access and allocation; the conversion and capture losses are charged separately. Reaching those thresholds requires a different network and dispatch design, not a bookkeeping increase. [Caltech's wireless-power experiment](https://www.caltech.edu/about/news/space-solar-power-project-ends-first-in-space-mission-with-successes-and-lessons) supplies a relevant demonstration of transfer, while the proposed utility scale remains to be qualified.

## Scheduling industry and choosing sites

Moving work into daylight preserves its total energy requirement while reducing storage. In the model, moving 50% of dark-time consumption into the lit half of the cycle raises daytime demand to 1.5 times its mean and halves storage. The calculation increases electrical capacity when the revised peak exceeds the original allowance. It does not yet add the factories, intermediate inventory and staffing needed to make that production schedule possible.

| Regenerative-storage case | Storage output, MWh per MW mean | Imported equipment and delivery, million USD2026 per MW mean |
|---|---:|---:|
| Ordinary site, continuous demand | 354.4 | 763.2 |
| Ordinary site, 15% of dark-time demand moved | 301.2 | 663.8 |
| Ordinary site, 50% moved | 177.2 | 432.0 |
| Ordinary site, 85% moved | 53.2 | 215.3 |
| Synthetic polar site, continuous demand | About 100 | 268.0 |

The current occupied-services account limits how much shifting can be assumed. Moving **all currently modeled lunar processing** could shift at most approximately 12% of total demand at opening, 41% at year 100 and 15% at year 200 in the strong-market propellant/logistics case. These upper bounds do not establish that every process tolerates interruption. The 50% and 85% cases require additional changes to food production or other occupied services. Daylight-only operation also reduces productive hours: a process previously available 80% of the time would require about 1.6 times its operating capacity to preserve output within a 50% daylight window, before assessing existing idle capacity.

High-illumination polar sites can reduce storage, but terrain, seasonal shadows, low solar elevation, protected areas and the location of resources constrain their use. [NASA's lunar-power overview](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220005105/downloads/SWEDE%20Lunar%20Surface%20Power%20Draft%203-30-22.pdf) illustrates both long ordinary nights and substantial interruptions even at favorable polar sites. The synthetic case is a sensitivity for a site-selection study, not evidence for relocating the entire population programme.

A regional grid can share spare plants and favorable local terrain. It cannot by itself span the ordinary night: 100 km of equatorial longitude changes solar time by only **6.49 hours**. Interconnecting widely separated day and night regions is another possible architecture, requiring thousands of kilometres of lines and eventually a route around a roughly 10,916 km equatorial circumference. Its transmission mass, losses, installation and protection corridor deserve their own comparison before any global-grid saving is credited.

## Early production and later expansion

Early robots and industrial pilots need a different service from continuously occupied communities. Under the middle photovoltaic and freight assumptions, **1 MW during daylight** requires approximately **1.40 MW of panels and 14 tonnes of equipment**, costing **18.25 million USD2026** in equipment and delivery. Mean output over the full cycle is 0.5 MW and night output is zero. Survival heating, short buffers and any overnight machinery must be supplied separately. This is a practical candidate for an initial sequence in which useful production precedes a large continuously operating utility.

Conversely, the inherited beam geometry cannot shrink indefinitely with demand. Ten 1.2 km apertures occupy 11.31 km². An illustrative structural floor of 1 kg/m² implies **11,310 tonnes** before other components; even 0.1 kg/m² implies 1,131 tonnes. The model takes the greater of this floor and the inherited watts-per-kilogram mass, avoiding a duplicate subsystem charge. Neither density is a finished transmitter design. Smaller apertures, different frequencies, ranges or fewer stations could produce a different early beam system, but the large network cannot acquire a small pilot's cost merely by scaling its electrical output.

The inherited reference portfolio requires the following surface service. The mean values combine occupied services with actual lunar processing; firm capacity retains the producer's original allowances.

| Programme year | Mean surface electricity, GW | Specified surface capacity, GW |
|---|---:|---:|
| 8 | 0.359 | 0.547 |
| 32 | 0.579 | 0.889 |
| 100 | 9.540 | 14.676 |
| 200 | 191.255 | 292.148 |
| 220 | 196.969 | 301.076 |

At year 8, the middle imported-equipment comparison values the required beam capacity at **96.3 billion constant USD2041**, versus **62.8 billion** for fission. The approximately **33.5 billion USD2041 difference** is a comparison of installed capacity at that checkpoint. It is not a saving from the complete seed campaign: preproduction peaks, delivery sequences, common development expenditure, critical reserves and the existing freight system require reconciliation first. The inherited surviving orbital beam stock alone is valued at 46.3 billion USD2041 at its equipment quote, approximately 1.76% of the physical seed outlay; receivers, delivery and attrition add costs. Power is consequential without accounting for most of the existing multi-trillion-dollar founding account.

At year 200, extending today's comparison assumptions without further improvements would require very large capital stocks under every option. The model reports those checkpoint stocks separately from lifetime spending. It does not rebuild a founding programme for every increment, or claim that a 200-year-old technology and supply chain remain economically optimal. Deployment should add modules, shared infrastructure and replacement capacity as demand grows, with qualified later generations replacing earlier designs.

## Heat rejection and preservation

Vacuum removes the Open Moon atmospheric-heating constraint from this decision. It does not remove local thermal engineering. Reactors and heat engines need conversion radiators; fuel cells and electrolysers need temperature control; all occupied facilities must reject the heat from consumed electricity. The calculation sizes radiators by the Stefan–Boltzmann relation with 0.9 emissivity and an illustrative 0.7 effective emitting fraction. A complete lunar design must resolve solar illumination, warm-ground view, dust, temperature variation, spacing and redundancy. Fission's radiator mass is checked within its complete-system allowance rather than added twice.

For context, converting the reference year-200 mean electricity through a 33%-efficient fission system and the assumed local electrical loss yields a Moon-area-averaged heat equivalent of only **0.016 W/m²**. That is not a local temperature prediction. Concentrating the same heat near a habitat could impose significant radiator and layout requirements, even though it supplies no reason to import the later atmospheric dimming architecture.

Local power also creates physical footprints. Arrays, tanks, hot stores, reactor standoffs, cables and receivers must follow the existing [source-access and preservation requirements](../../research/studies/enclosed_feasibility/README.md#preservation-and-source-qualification). The middle thermal-storage case would hold approximately 2.8 Mt of local medium at the first occupied checkpoint and 1.49 Gt at year 200. This additional material stock requires integration with the mining, construction and reclamation accounts. Thermal medium is abundant bulk material only where investigation and release permit its removal. Construction must retain pre-disturbance surveys, representative samples, provenance and accessible records; equipment should support material recovery and controlled residues at retirement. No power option gains free use of protected sites, cold traps, heritage locations or scientific archives through this cost calculation.

## Development priorities through 2041

The recommended development sequence starts with local photovoltaic electricity and suitable direct solar heat for the first productive modules. It then qualifies continuous supply against actual sites and loads. Fission and combinations of local supplies deserve a primary development track; solar thermal storage deserves parallel work because local bulk storage can change the import burden substantially. Beaming remains a competing utility investment whose useful coverage, transmitter design, receiver land and delivered cost must justify its stage of deployment.

| Period | Engineering work | Evidence needed for the next commitment |
|---|---|---|
| 2026–2030 | Allocate real loads by time, temperature and criticality; survey illumination and permitted sites; compare complete supplier concepts | Dated electric and process-heat demands, site shadows, protection constraints and component-level mass/cost accounts |
| 2029–2035 | Test local solar equipment, regenerative storage, hot-store materials and reactor conversion hardware; develop beam hardware where its deployment case warrants it | Repeated environmental cycles, recoverable energy, dust and thermal performance, inspection and repair requirements |
| 2033–2038 | Operate integrated pilots sized to a first productive facility; demonstrate nighttime service, shutdown and restart | Measured delivered energy and availability, independent reserve performance, actual delivery and installation burden |
| 2037–2041 | Select qualified first-production supplies and prepare expansions as separate increments | An executable commissioning sequence, maintainable equipment, supply diversity and a costed contingency for the chosen plant |

The existing common programme allowance is **100 billion USD2026**. This comparison does not charge it again or claim that it covers every power-development alternative. A work breakdown must allocate that allowance and identify any additional qualification, civil works and fuel obligations before a revised funding requirement is stated.

The next coupled calculation should replace the surface supply cohorts with the chosen modules and feed their materials, freight, maintenance, spare capacity and commissioning dates back through the industrial and financial accounts. It should allow productive daytime operation before full residential opening and price any extra factory capacity used for load shifting. **The current returns, gross domestic product and U monetary estimates remain conditional on their inherited power supply until that calculation is run.**

## Reproduction and evidence records

[The calculation](model.py) reads versioned, hashed physical inputs and emits [the comparison product](results/power_comparison.json). The product records three technology bundles, six architectures, three freight cases, three equipment-import fractions, site and schedule sensitivities, small-system geometry limits and mappings for all 81 primary year-200 cases. [The source register](enclosed_power_sources.json) identifies the primary material and its reading scope. [The verification record](checks.md) distinguishes numerical controls, source assessment and design work still required.

```bash
OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS=1 OMP_NUM_THREADS=1 python provisioning/enclosed_power/model.py
OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS=1 OMP_NUM_THREADS=1 python -m pytest -q provisioning/enclosed_power/test_power.py
OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS=1 OMP_NUM_THREADS=1 python visualization/enclosed_power/plot.py
```

The renderer writes a comparison figure and its provenance to ignored `research/runs/enclosed_power/`. It reads the result product without introducing a new physical model. Input prices and technological performance bounds are disclosed study assumptions; the source literature supports mechanisms and development context rather than a future market quotation.
