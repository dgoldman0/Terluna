# Evidence for snow-aggregate strength and breakup

The literature supports a model that follows **contact formation, bond aging, deformation and loss of branches**, with humidity and collision history attached to those processes. This targeted search found useful measurements for several mechanisms, but no measured strength law that transfers directly to a freely falling 15–60 cm aggregate. A size cutoff would therefore remain a chosen scenario. The numerical repairs identified in [the audit](numerical_audit.md) can proceed while these physical uncertainties are preserved.

This note serves the project's model developers and assumes familiarity with the [snow-column analysis](analysis.md). Eight primary studies were selected on 21 October 2026. Their methods, transferable quantities and limits are summarized below; [strength_sources.json](strength_sources.json) records the passages retrieved, access routes and unresolved retrievals. Experiments, simulations and our proposed treatment are distinguished. The companion [geometry review](geometry_literature.md) covers measured large flakes and growth laws. No coefficients from this review have been installed in the simulator.

## Direct snow breakup measurements

[Grzegorczyk et al. (2023)](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/23/13505/2023/) collided artificial approximately 10 mm dendritic aggregates with 4 mm graupel at −15°C. Their reported collision-energy range was 10⁻⁷–10⁻⁵ J, and the snow collisions produced tens to hundreds of fragments, with a size mode near 400 µm. Five no-collision releases also shed 26–140 fragments, averaging 51, which the investigators treated as a preparation/release contribution. Dendrites were grown near 120% humidity relative to ice; this may have made them unusually fragile. The [2024 correction](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/23/13505/2023/acp-23-13505-2023-corrigendum.pdf) reduces the fitted asperity density fourfold because the theory uses spherical surface area rather than projected area. These experiments inform small-fragment production and distinguish release loading from impact. They do not measure the strength or largest remnant of a decimetre flake.

[Gautam et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1175/JAS-D-23-0122.1) used naturally falling snow striking fixed ice spheres in an outdoor chamber. Dendritic snow spanning 3–12 mm produced about 12 fragments per collision on average, versus about one for 1–3 mm nondendritic snow. Fragment detection was reliable above approximately 0.15 mm; mass was inferred from event-specific mass–size relationships. Their fitted fragmentation treatment includes impact energy and habit, but does not separately resolve monomers and aggregates. This supplies a second observational target for a collision module. Its different preparation, detection and impact conditions prevent direct calibration by comparing fragment counts with the preceding experiment alone.

These studies support distinguishing collision-induced chipping from the failure of a whole aggregate. Counting numerous tiny splinters does not establish that most parent mass was dispersed. A future breakup calculation should export the largest-remnant mass and the mass distribution of other remnants alongside fragment number, so that these outcomes remain observable.

## What the contact measurements constrain

| Primary study | Conditions actually examined | Quantity it can inform |
|---|---|---|
| [Szabo and Schneebeli (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.2721391) | Ice-cone tips with 3 mm curvature radius; −1, −5, −12 and −23°C; approximately 50% relative humidity; 0.5–8 N contact loads; 20–1000 ms in the methods | Rapid bond formation and pull-off force under substantial compression |
| [Bahaloo et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0073824) | Smaller radii 0.61–2.02 mm against an approximately 5 mm ice surface; −2 to −16°C; approximately 50% relative humidity; 1–15 mN maximum load; specified loading, holding and unloading histories | Tensile pull-off dependence on temperature, curvature, time and contact load |
| [Demmenie et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jpclett.5c00050) | Approximately 1 mm ice spheres at −3°C, in horizontal contact without external pressure, at controlled vapour equilibrium; approximately 2.5 h | Neck growth through local sublimation and condensation without an imposed compressive load |

The loaded-contact experiments cannot supply a default force capacity for a dendritic junction formed during gentle aggregation. Their preloads, smooth local shapes and humidity differ from suspended-snow contact histories. Bahaloo's duration series holds maximum load for 0.1–100 s **in addition to** 21 s loading and 21 s unloading; calling this a 0.1 s total-contact experiment would misread the methods. Its illustrative 5.7 mN pull-off result follows a 10 mN preload on a 1.2 mm radius particle at −12°C. The fitted strength incorporates a stress-concentration factor for that geometry.

Demmenie's uncompressed contacts are relevant to long residence times. Four experiments give neck-radius time exponents of approximately 0.26–0.33, consistent with vapour transport; a deliberately drier experiment gives an apparent exponent near 1/7 while the spheres also lose mass. These are geometrical measurements, rather than pull-off measurements. They support tracking the neck separately from total flake mass and using actual humidity history. They do not establish a universal bond-growth law for −15°C dendrites or hours of supersaturated growth.

## Sublimation and deformation change the contact network

[Oraltay and Hallett (1989)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0169-8095(89)90044-6) observed habit-dependent fragmentation during controlled evaporation and melting. The publisher abstract reports dendritic-crystal experiments over −6 to +5°C and 50–90% relative humidity, with approximately 5 mm crystals breaking and sometimes reattaching; columns and plates did not fragment during their evaporation experiments. Only the abstract was obtained, so its humidity convention and full controls have not been checked here. This supports retaining thermal and humidity history, without supplying a calibrated lunar sublimation-breakup rate.

[Aumatell and Wurm (2011)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1108.0805) directly observed sublimating porous ice aggregates separating at weak contacts while levitated above a plate. The thin aggregates, made from grains of several to a few tens of micrometres, floated at a few millibars under a strong temperature gradient; the plate was near 260 K. The reported ice coverage spanned about 10 mm and was only fractions of a millimetre thick. The nearly two-dimensional geometry and rarefied gas differ substantially from the lunar snow layer. This demonstrates contact loss during sublimation with little gravitational compaction, while supplying no transferable atmospheric breakup timescale.

Contact strengthening also changes how an aggregate absorbs impact. [Sirono and Ueno (2017)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.04778) simulated collisions between aggregates of 1,024 spherical ice grains, each initially 0.1 µm in radius. Unsintered contacts could roll and dissipate energy throughout a porous structure. Strongly sintered contacts restricted that motion, allowing bouncing or brittle disruption despite larger individual bond strength. Their interstellar-grain scale, prescribed contact physics and head-on collisions make the numerical velocity thresholds unsuitable for atmospheric snow. The applicable inference is structural: contact age and strength alone cannot determine sticking efficiency without deformation and network connectivity.

## Consequences for the physical model

These are proposed development requirements inferred from the studies, rather than a calibrated parameterization:

1. **Keep the numerical repair independent of new strength assumptions.** Retain the existing physical closures as an explicitly extrapolated control while testing conservative weights and collision resolution. This isolates numerical changes before adding uncertain breakup physics.
2. **Give a collision more than a binary outcome.** Distinguish sticking with little deformation, compaction, rebound, branch shedding and separation into substantial remnants. Use relative collision energy, temperature, habit and rime state where evidence supports them. Preserve every outcome's mass budget.
3. **Represent the bonds that carry load.** A mechanical sensitivity model needs contact ages, neck dimensions and connectivity, together with component size and habit. Peripheral branches attached through one narrow junction behave differently from the same ice mass joined through several paths. A resolved contact network would provide a reference calculation for a simpler closure.
4. **Evolve geometry and bonds under the same water history.** Deposition, sublimation, neck growth, riming and compaction change different parts of an aggregate. A mass–dimension power law does not identify which part changed. Growth and erosion must account for both the water budget and load-bearing connections.
5. **Apply aerodynamic loads to substructures.** Settling drag, orientation, airflow differences across a flake and particle acceleration determine junction forces and torques. Whole-flake dynamic pressure is only a load diagnostic. Comparing it directly with snowpack tensile strength cannot establish survival. Collision energy and sustained aerodynamic loading also need distinct failure criteria.

A useful next physical calculation would compare matched aggregate geometries with sparse contact networks and additional sintered connections, varying humidity history and imposed air motion. It would report required junction strengths, deformation and largest surviving remnants. This can identify which unmeasured properties control the answer before choosing a strength value. Ordinary millimetre-to-centimetre experiments would constrain reference cases; decimetre outcomes would remain extrapolations with their dependence on assumed connectivity reported.

The search makes the physical task more specific: useful contact and fragment measurements exist, and low-load sintering has a direct experimental example. It leaves the strength distribution of a large, freely suspended dendritic network unresolved. No study examined here establishes either a universal 15–60 cm prohibition or the abundance of such flakes in lunar snowfall.

## Review and evidence limits

This was a targeted pass rather than an exhaustive review. Experimental sections and relevant results were inspected for the two collision studies, the two loaded-contact studies, the levitation experiment and the aggregate-collision simulation. For Demmenie, indexed publisher methods/results were retrieved despite direct access failures; Oraltay's evidence is abstract-only. Figures were not digitized or visually reviewed, and no papers or outside datasets were added to Git. The source ledger is syntactically checked JSON; the prose was read against the retrieved conditions and existing numerical audit. This note adds no numerical prediction or lunar empirical validation.
