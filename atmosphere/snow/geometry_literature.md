# Literature constraints on aggregate geometry and growth

This targeted review supports repair of the snow population model. Its reader is the project researcher choosing physical closures after the numerical audit; the claims below distinguish measured snow, geometric simulations, theoretical scaling and proposed lunar tests. It complements the [mechanical review](strength_literature.md) and the existing [habit definitions](habits.md). Source identities, reading extent and retrieval gaps are recorded in [geometry_sources.json](geometry_sources.json). No new snowfall distribution is calculated here.

The literature supplies more support for large, porous aggregates than a warning about extrapolation alone conveys. It also identifies a specific missing feedback: aggregation changes geometry, and geometry changes subsequent growth. A near-quadratic mass–size law has theoretical and observational support; its coefficient, size-dependent scatter, collision efficiency and mechanical survival remain separate questions. None of the sources examined supplies a validated 15–60 cm lunar population or an empirical upper diameter transferable to lunar gravity.

## Direct observations and their limits

[Lawson et al. (1993)](https://doi.org/10.1029/92GL02917) obtained airborne shadowgraphs of 4–5 cm snowflakes during CASP II and reported ground collections reaching about 6 cm. Their component dendrites were 2–5 mm across. The instrument was designed to image particles up to approximately 5 cm, so that airborne maximum is partly an instrument limit, not a measured physical ceiling. Growth occurred along a path into a roughly 600 m layer near freezing. These observations directly establish that coherent aggregates several centimetres across form and survive in terrestrial storms.

The expanded [Lawson, Stewart and Angus (1998) study](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/atsc/55/21/1520-0469_1998_055_3209_oansot_2.0.co_2.xml) associates those particles with an approximately 9 mm/h precipitation event, strongly sheared transport, and regions of 0.2–0.6 m/s ascent. Its combined observations and microphysical modeling suggest breakup inhibited above roughly −3 °C while aggregation continued. That is a case interpretation, not a universal breakup threshold. It provides a useful counterexample to assuming that any wind or shear destroys a large aggregate, and a terrestrial case for checking growth-path and temperature dependence.

A targeted search located older reports of still larger flakes, but did not retrieve an independently calibrated population at 15–60 cm. Such historical reports are not used to set a size ceiling or fit a lunar closure. The measured centimetre examples likewise cannot be treated as an upper limit imposed by ice itself.

## Why approximately quadratic geometry is defensible

[Westbrook et al. (2004, GRL)](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2004GL020363) compared a geometric aggregation simulation with cirrus images and distributions, finding a mass–span fractal exponent near 2.05 ± 0.1. Its particles join rigidly without subsequent consolidation. Independently, [Stein et al. (2015)](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2014GL062170) retrieved fractal dimensions close to 2 from triple-frequency radar. The radar calculation integrated particle sizes only through 20 mm; its evidence supports fractal structure in terrestrial clouds, not decimetre strength.

The companion [Westbrook et al. (2004, PRE) theory](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0312057) explains a feedback between differential settling and structure. If unusually open geometry makes large particles collect small ones too efficiently, growth becomes dominated by unequal-size encounters, which build denser clusters. In its inertial-flow limit this selects a fractal dimension near 2. The theory assumes dilute binary encounters, rigid permanent contacts and undeflected approach trajectories; it has neither fracture nor riming. It supports a possible asymptotic geometric law without proving indefinite survival.

### A discriminating test for our fixed closure

The local model fixes `m = a D^1.9` and a constant projected-area ratio. At fixed air state, its high-Reynolds fall-speed law approaches `v ∝ m^0.5/D`, hence `v ∝ m^(0.5 − 1/1.9)`. Consider one fixed small partner and vary the large particle. While the large particle still falls much faster, the limiting sweepout kernel scales approximately as `K ∝ m^(0.5 + 1/1.9) = m^1.0263`. In the more remote limit where its speed has declined below the fixed partner's speed, the relative speed approaches a constant and `K ∝ m^(2/1.9) = m^1.0526`.

These are calculations from the local closure, not measurements and not a claim that either asymptote describes every encounter. They identify superlinear preferential growth worth testing. The PRE theory identifies runaway behavior for a fixed large-particle kernel exponent above one and explains how evolving aggregate geometry can oppose it. Our fixed exponent removes that particular structural response. The paper also requires fractal dimension at least two for its assumed projected-area scaling; combining 1.9 with an indefinitely constant area ratio needs an independent check.

Repairing particle weights and collision time resolution therefore should precede a separate comparison of geometric closures. Preserve the inherited law for that numerical comparison, then test a mass law tending toward quadratic scaling, anchored at an explicit reference size, and a geometry model that retains monomer size and aggregate history. Changing the exponent alone would be a controlled hypothesis test, not a calibrated physical repair. Runaway removal by a new breakup diameter would otherwise confound a geometry defect with unknown mechanical strength.

## Available routes beyond one mean particle shape

[Seifert et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.16993/tellus.4127), now published in *Tellus*, models the joint variation of span, aspect ratio and area from mass, monomer count and monomer habit. The reviewed preprint methods use 2–2,048 monomers and mean monomer sizes 50–500 μm. They regularize tenuous contacts geometrically rather than calculating bond forces. Published geometry results report exponents 2.03–2.12 across the three pure habits. Their stochastic representation is a candidate way to include shape-dependent velocity differences, including among particles of similar mass. It provides no fracture criterion. The present model's fitted coefficients remain bound to arXiv v1; this review identifies the published version without asserting an equation-by-equation version match.

[Westbrook, Hogan and Illingworth (2008)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0610038) independently computed diffusive capacitance for synthetic aggregates. Their near-constant `C/D ≈ 0.25–0.28` follows from how diffusing molecules encounter porous structures, with shape-dependent projected areas. This supports retaining the current aggregate capacitance as a comparison closure. It does not establish a universal ventilation factor, collision area or strength for giant aggregates.

The model of [Seifert et al. (2019)](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2018MS001519) provides a geometric transition from unrimed aggregates toward compact graupel as rime accumulates. Their simulation first assembles aggregates from crystals with a 200 μm mean size, then attaches 40 μm droplets; a representative initial aggregate is approximately 2 mm. Area and maximum dimension evolve during riming, affecting subsequent growth and settling. This is useful for a later mixed-phase extension, but its geometric compaction procedure is not a mechanical calculation of branch failure. Our continuously liquid-saturated column does not currently predict a droplet population or rime mass.

## Collision efficiency and lunar transfer

The McSnow model of [Brdar and Seifert (2018)](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2017MS001167) separates the probability of reaching contact from the probability of sticking after contact. Its Böhm-based collision efficiency is near unity for comparable millimetre particles but decreases when one partner is much smaller. The local model currently uses geometric sweepout and temperature-dependent sticking without that explicit hydrodynamic factor. This distinction is especially relevant to runaway collection of small particles; it should be evaluated independently of the particle algorithm.

The extension by [Welss et al. (2024)](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2023MS003805) supplies an implementable account of habit-dependent fall speeds and collision efficiencies, including a potential-flow correction at low Reynolds number and atmospheric-state dependence. Its authors treat Böhm's framework as a parameterization with known limits. These equations offer a better starting point for changing gravity and air properties than importing an Earth aggregation efficiency unchanged. They do not validate hydrodynamic interactions between giant porous aggregates.

The reduced-gravity searches in this pass found no atmospheric snow experiment directly matching the Moon's gravity, temperature and gas density. A [2025 conference abstract](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU25/EGU25-5395.html) describes a cryogenic vacuum drop tower for icy aggregates below 130 K and proposed 0.2–0.5 m/s encounters; it supplies apparatus and experimental intent rather than measured lunar-snow limits. The [mechanical review](strength_literature.md) assesses the more useful contact and restructuring analogues with their temperature, loading and material differences attached.

The immediate physical priorities are thus specific: measure the implemented kernel's size scaling; distinguish hydrodynamic interception from sticking; retain monomer and rime information when improving geometry; and compare fracture or restructuring models only with the relevant contact histories. These additions belong after benchmarked numerical repair, with each physical change isolated so that a smaller tail has an identifiable cause.
