      Why the light lingers

      Twilight that lasts for days

      The Open Moon's air presses down at 1.2 atmospheres and stretches more than six times taller than Earth's, holding about seven times as much air above each spot. Sunlight scattered high in that deep air keeps lighting the ground long after sunset, while the Sun itself sinks thirty times more slowly than on Earth.

      How this works

      Computed from the Terluna research

      The sky and its light

      Every number on this page comes from Terluna's lighting model of the Open Moon: a deep, clear air of 1.2 atmospheres under the titania solar shield, with sunlight and Earthlight traced through it colour by colour. The Sun and Earth move on a lunar ephemeris checked against NASA JPL Horizons for 2000–2500.

      The sky picture takes its colour and brightness from the solved sky atlas of the same air. The ground is open and level, and the sky is clear. Clouds, hills and weather are further layers of the research.

      Reading the picture

      A screen cannot show a hundred-thousand-fold range of light, so the picture follows an eye that adapts: bright days, glowing twilights, and nights where colour fades and stars appear. The sea's glitter, the ground's colour and the glare around the Sun are drawn to match the light, as illustrations.

      Earth shows its real continents turning beneath a cloud picture from NASA's Blue Marble. Earth's actual weather on a given day will differ.

      Words and thresholds

      Golden twilight lasts while the ground gets at least 2.98 lux, the light at the end of Earth's civil twilight in the same model, enough to work outside without lamps. Earthlit night is when Earth gives more light than the Sun's twilight. A full Moon on Earth gives at most about 0.3 lux.
