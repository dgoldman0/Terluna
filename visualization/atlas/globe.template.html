What the appearance shows

Computed. Land, sea and water depth come from LOLA topography above the GRAIL geoid at 16 pixels per degree (1.9 km), filled to the geography atlas's common sea level. Rivers and the rain-fed lakes above sea level come from the geography domain's drainage estimate, which routes the climate run's runoff over the terrain: the lakes cover a further 12% of the Moon, and holding their water takes about a third as much again as the seas. The air comes from the illumination domain's Open Moon atmosphere, . The page ray-marches that air through its whole height, so the halo around the limb and the sunlit air beyond the terminator come from the same calculation as the haze over the disk. The ground's direct and diffuse light at each Sun height comes from the Open Moon clear-sky atlas, 55 orders of scattering.

Informed. Clouds come from the ExoPlaSim climate run : . Each deck moves with the Sun through the run's composite by latitude and local time, and keeps the run's pattern over land and sea. Light scattered more than once in the air is an isotropic source proportional to the sky atlas's diffuse light at each point's Sun height, scaled per channel so that the air seen straight down matches Eddington's multiple-scattering reflectance of the same column.

Guesstimate. Surface cover follows rainfall, soil moisture, nearness to water, floodplains, height, hollows and slope: forest, woodland, grassland, bare highland or basaltic soil, and rock on steep ground. Water colour follows depth, and rivers carry sediment. River width follows discharge, by Earth's relation widened for the slower flow of lunar gravity; most channels are narrower than a pixel. Cloud shapes are noise the page draws in 3D, thresholded to the run's cover, and the decks' opacity is a guess (the low deck holds the run's cloud water, the high deck little). The biosphere and climate work replace all of this.

Display. Colours are the computed radiances with Earth daylight (D65) as white and no white balance. Exposure scales them like a camera: at 0 a white surface under the unfiltered overhead Sun sits at display white, and the page opens one stop brighter, where the brightest cloud reaches white. A soft shoulder above 80% keeps bright cloud from clipping, and about +10 stops shows the twilight and the night side. Red light only shows the red channel in grey, as a red filter would: the air is thinnest there, so land and sea show best. The Sun slider sets the Moon's age from new moon: the Sun stands over longitude 180° − 360° × age / 29.53 days. From Earth puts the camera at the Moon's mean distance, 384,400 km, with a narrow field of view.

Next refinements. Refraction: a ray grazing the surface bends about 0.28°, which carries sunlight further past the terminator. Multiple scattering along the limb and in twilight, where the calibration for the disk is weakest. Earthshine on the night side. Clouds with depth, and their shadows in the air. Finer terrain for close views: at 16 pixels per degree the surface softens below about 1,500 km altitude.

What the map shows

Water in depth bands and land in a neutral grey, with the light following the viewer so every face reads as a map. Relief is exaggerated by the slider, and the seas stay flat at sea level. Heritage sites and sampling targets come from the conservation study's register. Groundwater in the porous crust and crustal loading will move the shoreline, and each catchment's rainfall and evaporation will set which closed depressions hold lakes; here every depression below sea level is filled.
