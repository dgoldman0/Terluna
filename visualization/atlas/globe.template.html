What the appearance shows

Computed. Land, sea and water depth come from LOLA topography above the GRAIL geoid at 16 pixels per degree (1.9 km), filled to the geography atlas's common sea level. Rivers and the rain-fed lakes above sea level come from the geography domain's drainage estimate, which routes the climate run's runoff over the terrain: . The air comes from the illumination domain's Open Moon atmosphere, . The page ray-marches that air through its whole height, so the halo around the limb and the sunlit air beyond the terminator come from the same calculation as the haze over the disk. The ground's direct and diffuse light at each Sun height comes from the Open Moon clear-sky atlas, 55 orders of scattering.

Informed. Clouds come from the ExoPlaSim climate run : . Each deck moves with the Sun through the run's composite by latitude and local time, and keeps the run's pattern over land and sea. Light scattered more than once in the air is an isotropic source proportional to the sky atlas's diffuse light at each point's Sun height, scaled per channel so that the air seen straight down matches Eddington's multiple-scattering reflectance of the same column.

Guesstimate. Surface cover follows rainfall, soil moisture, nearness to water, floodplains, height, hollows and slope: forest, woodland, grassland, bare highland or basaltic soil, and rock on steep ground. The climate run's rain and soil moisture are smoothed over 0.7 of its 5.5° grid before they set the cover, so that its grid does not show as straight edges. Water colour follows depth, and rivers carry sediment. River width follows discharge, by Earth's relation widened for the slower flow of lunar gravity; most channels are narrower than a pixel. Cloud shapes are noise the page draws in 3D, thresholded to the run's cover, and the decks' opacity is a guess (the low deck holds the run's cloud water, the high deck little). The biosphere and climate work replace all of this.

Display. Colours are the computed radiances with Earth daylight (D65) as white and no white balance. Exposure scales them like a camera: at 0 a white surface under the unfiltered overhead Sun sits at display white, and the page opens one stop brighter, where the brightest cloud reaches white. A soft shoulder above 80% keeps bright cloud from clipping, and about +10 stops shows the twilight and the night side. Red light only shows the red channel in grey, as a red filter would: the air is thinnest there, so land and sea show best. Air in view peels the air away from the top down, as a descent through it would show the ground: the view looks through the air below the chosen height only, so the haze, the halo and any cloud deck above that height leave it, while every place keeps the light the whole air gives it, the decks' shade included. At the ground no haze is left, and the ground shows in its own colours under its sky, much as satellite mosaics of Earth, corrected for the air between, show its ground. With the zoom, the zoom stands in for the descent: from the opening view to the nearest zoom the share of the air's column left in view falls from all of it to none, evenly with the logarithm of the camera's height, while the camera itself stays above the air; moving the slider takes the peel off the zoom. A camera that truly descended would keep most of the haze until the last few tens of kilometres, where the ground this page holds is a blur. The Sun slider sets the Moon's age from new moon: the Sun stands over longitude 180° − 360° × age / 29.53 days. Fully lit gives every place the same Sun, 45° up, the cartographers' hill-shading light, in the north-west of its own sky and overhead near the poles, so that the globe reads evenly from limb to limb; it keeps the clouds of the slider's day, and with no one Sun there is no sunglint. From Earth puts the camera at the Moon's mean distance, 384,400 km, with a narrow field of view.

Next refinements. Refraction: a ray grazing the surface bends about 0.28°, which carries sunlight further past the terminator. Multiple scattering along the limb and in twilight, where the calibration for the disk is weakest. Earthshine on the night side. Clouds with depth, and their shadows in the air. Finer terrain for close views: at 16 pixels per degree the surface softens below about 1,500 km altitude.

What the topography shows

The ground's height above sea level and the sea floor's depth below it, from the atlas's LOLA heights above the GRAIL geoid at 4 pixels per degree (7.6 km), with the water taken away. Below sea level each of the atlas sheets' depth bands keeps its colour at the band's middle depth; above it the colours run from lowland green to the near-white of the highest ground. Shading comes from the full relief at 8 pixels per degree under light that every place has alike, from high in its own north-west, so that level ground keeps the legend's colours anywhere on the globe, and contour lines mark the chosen interval, with sea level darker. In the topography mode the slider exaggerates the relief down into the sea floors as well as up into the highlands. Over the appearance the same colours and contours lie on the rendered globe at the chosen opacity, unlit by the Sun so that they read on the night side and through the air. Every depression below sea level takes the sea's colours, as the atlas fills it, and the rain-fed lakes above sea level are left out, so their beds show. Groundwater in the porous crust and crustal loading will move the shoreline. Heritage sites and sampling targets come from the conservation study's register. In both modes, a click on the globe reads the height of the 0.25° atlas cell under it.

Regions and districts: a first draft

Computed, then judged. A district is a region that reads as a whole and meaningful place and stands apart from the others. The draft applies that as rules: each district gathers round one centre (a named crater's floor, a large lake, or the ground between them); its edges follow the ground's own ridges and rim crests and the edges of its moisture zone and rock; neighbours alike in zone, rock and the waters they drain to, with only a weak edge between them, merge; and districts run from about 5,000 to 150,000 km². Regions gather districts round the great basins and are never crossed by a district. The coastal seas, the water within 60 km of the shore, are districts of their own, each linked to the land that faces it and the land whose rivers reach it, so that the coast and its land can be planned together; selecting either shows them both. The draft's significance is computed, from landform, water, climate and rock: written judgment of each place, and the kept landforms, settlement and heritage treatments, come later.

Names. Working names in the Moon's own tongues, each region in one of them, chosen loosely as Earth's peoples sit in like landscapes: . Terlunan and Proto-Olivolingvo come from the author's own languages; the others are lunar descendants of their Earth roots, built for the atlas from everyday landscape words. A name joins a head chosen by the place's centre (a lake, a crater, a river, a plain, a coast) to a word for what sets it apart (its zone, rock, height, lakes, falls or the Earthlight of the near side). Some match places on Earth, as names from real roots will. They are labels for the people who come to live there to keep or replace.
