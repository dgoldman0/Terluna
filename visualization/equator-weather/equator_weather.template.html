Weather on the Open Moon's equator

CM1, a model that forms its own storms, clouds and rain, run along the equator as a two-dimensional ring 10,916 km round, for the chosen design: 28% seas with lakes, 1.2 atm of Earthlike air, the 5% dimmer shield. The Sun crosses the ring once a lunar day, so the ring holds every local time at once. What follows is its second lunar day (model days {{span_from}}–{{span_to}}, {{snapshots}} snapshots), after one lunar day of spin-up.

A lunar day at the equator

Over land the night is calm and saturated. The air cools slowly, from {{sunset_air}} °C at sunset to {{dawn_air}} °C at dawn a fortnight later, and in the second half of the night fog or cloud topped about {{fog_top_m}} m up covers {{fog_pct}}% of the land. After sunrise the land warms for a week: by noon the air at 2 m is {{noon_air}} °C, the ground {{noon_ground}} °C, the humidity {{noon_rh}}% and the wet-bulb {{noon_wet_bulb}} °C, and cumulus towers reach {{noon_tower_km}} km without raining. Storms fill the afternoon, from about {{storms_from_days}} to {{storms_to_days}} Earth days after noon, and cool the land air back to {{late_air}} °C. The seas, held at {{sea_surface}} °C, keep the air above them between {{sea_air_min}} and {{sea_air_max}} °C and rain only in the afternoon.

The circulation that follows the Sun

Near the ground the air parts about {{parts_before_dawn}}° before dawn and flows toward the afternoon on both sides: through the morning at up to {{morning_flow}} m/s (at 1 km) and back from the evening and night at up to {{evening_flow}} m/s. The two streams meet about {{meets_after_noon}}° past noon, where the storms begin. The air rises through the afternoon and sinks slowly everywhere else, about {{sink_cm_s}} cm/s ({{sink_km_day}} km per Earth day), which keeps mornings and nights clear. Above 10 km the wind blows eastward at all hours; its mean is the GCM's, held there.

Winds by height and the flight band

Near the ground the wind is light: {{wind10_land}} m/s at the median over land, {{wind10_sea}} over the seas, and at most about {{gust_max}} m/s in storm gusts. It strengthens slowly with height. In the 35–45 km flight band the wind is {{band_median}} m/s at the median and under {{band_p99}} m/s 99% of the time, and the air's vertical motion is under {{band_w_p99}} m/s 99% of the time. Storm cloud reaches the band in {{band_cloud_lo}}–{{band_cloud_hi}}% of the afternoon's land columns, with updrafts up to {{band_updraft}} m/s inside.

Water

A lunar air column holds about {{water_mm}} mm of water, seven times Earth's for the same humidity: at 1.2 atm under a sixth of the gravity, the column has seven times Earth's mass per square metre. Evaporation is Earth-like, {{evap_all}} mm/day on average ({{evap_land}} over land, {{evap_sea}} over the seas), so water stays in the air about {{residence_days}} days, against Earth's 9. The ring took most of its first lunar day to settle: evaporation first filled the air, then a burst of storms released what it had stored.

Rain comes in episodes of a few days; the quietest came as the afternoon crossed the ring's widest seas. A place on land gets rain above 1 mm/h in about {{spells}} spells per lunar day, all in the afternoon, most lasting a few hours: {{rain_per_lunar_day}} mm per lunar day, about {{rain_per_year}} mm per Earth year. The GCM gives the equator more rain, {{gcm_rain_land}} mm/day over land, spread from mid-morning to evening, and more cloud over land ({{gcm_cloud_land}}% against the ring's {{cloud_land}}%); part of the difference is the ring's, whose storms cannot draw moisture from the north and south.

What the ring leaves out

  • It is two-dimensional: one line along the equator, with no north–south wind and no moisture converging on its storms from higher latitudes. Two-dimensional storms organise into lines more readily than real ones.
  • The ground is flat and at sea level, with a labelled placeholder land surface (albedo 0.20, fixed moisture availability, 10 cm roughness). The seas are held at 25.7 °C.
  • Above 8–16 km the mean temperature, water vapour and east–west wind are held to the GCM design case, which stands in for the circulation the ring cannot have.
  • Columns are 6 km wide, 1 km at Earth scale: deep storms are resolved, the smallest clouds are not. Storm lifetimes come from snapshots three hours apart.
