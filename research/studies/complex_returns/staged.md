# Financing industrial expansion from productive facilities

An off-Earth complex can earn from Earth customers while its permanent population remains small. A competition with hundreds of athletes can attract millions of viewers; the same production network can distribute documentaries, performances, games, education and licensed intellectual property. Research access, data products, software and professional services introduce other customers. Their demand depends on the usefulness and appeal of the products, while habitats, staff, power, equipment and transport constrain their production.

This study asks whether those earnings can finance successive industrial additions after an externally funded first stage. It extends the [whole-complex socioeconomic appraisal](README.md), preserving its physical boundary and stakeholder standing: the U socioecosystem includes the founding off-Earth industrial network, with Earth investors, customers, workers and institutions among its stakeholders. The analysis concerns an enclosed system with a sustained eventual cap. It introduces neither a planetary array nor an Open Moon economy.

The principal result is conditional but consequential. With a **$2.789 trillion externally funded first stage**, the middle worldwide-service scenario finances subsequent expansion and recovers that initial funding without discounting in **elapsed year 152**. A much stronger worldwide-service scenario recovers it in **year 24**, sixteen years after first production, and has **+$2.947 trillion pooled capital-recovery net present value over 100 elapsed years at 3% real**. The lower-demand scenario exhausts its cash in year 19. Self-financing operation and meeting a required investment return are therefore separate outcomes.

These are reproducible financing scenarios for researchers assessing the build, rather than forecasts or investment offers. The small first module, common infrastructure requirement, service capabilities and paying demand remain unqualified. All monetary amounts are constant **USD2041**. The hypothetical monetary unit U supplies no exchange rate for the new service prices.

## Reconcile the campaign before changing its timing

The original **$14.501 trillion establishment / $41.892 trillion campaign** remains the matched space-fuel and locally fabricated tug account. Its [transport product](inputs/transport.json), pinned to the same provisioning revision as the earlier appraisal, provides the following decomposition. These amounts include the source's 35% contingency:

| Original campaign component | Trillion USD2041 |
|---|---:|
| Industrial establishment | 14.5006 |
| Initial construction materials, delivery and restoration | 2.6706 |
| Supplier operation and renewal during construction | 23.3294 |
| Resident replenishment during construction | 1.3913 |
| **Original campaign total** | **41.8919** |

Establishment and initial material delivery together are **$17.1712 trillion**. The separately costed larger commercial programme adds **$0.3952 trillion**, yielding **$17.5663 trillion** of one-time deployment and commercial uses at founding scale. The remaining original campaign expenditure, **$24.7207 trillion**, finances recurring activity during its specified construction period. It cannot also be charged as an initial investment when the same activity is charged through a new annual operating schedule.

The staged calculation therefore retains the one-time bill and replaces the legacy recurring allowance with dated costs for deployment and operating capacity. It does not adopt the earlier cheaper transport architecture. It also does not claim that dividing the original bill produces a qualified small system: the provisioning study explicitly leaves initial deployment under the newer modular operating assumptions unresolved. The original full campaign remains a separate fixed-commitment benchmark.

The first stage allocates **10% of establishment, $1.4501 trillion**, to common infrastructure before any receipts. It then purchases **5% of the remaining founding deployment bill**, together with a $10 billion cultural platform and one $15 billion institutional-service facility. This produces **$2.2809 trillion of initial deployment uses**. Its annual no-sales cost determines an additional **$225.8 billion deployment carrying allowance** and **$282.2 billion startup liquidity reserve**. Their sum is the **$2.7889 trillion external seed**. The carrying allowance covers eight years at half the seed system's annual no-sales cost; the liquidity reserve covers five such years after first production. Both are new planning allowances, rather than quoted costs.

The initial module supports 12,500 residents alongside proportional mines, factories, power, source operations and logistics. That number describes a production and habitation footprint. It does not define its customer population or capital providers. A 5% module and the common infrastructure floor need a discrete equipment and commissioning design; smaller 1% and larger 10% starts are sensitivity cases, not established alternatives.

## Worldwide services and what the evidence supports

Existing businesses demonstrate the separation between participants, audiences and purchasers. Formula 1 reported a corporate estimate of **827 million fans** in its 2025 half-year review; this is neither a unique paid-account count nor an audited measure. Its parent's audited 2025 report records **$3.873 billion of Formula 1 revenue and $1.400 billion of team payments**, illustrating both worldwide rights income and substantial claims on that income. [F1 audience review](https://corp.formula1.com/formula-1-2025-season-a-half-year-review/), [Liberty annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1560385/000110465926020653/lmca-20251231x10k.htm).

Netflix's 2025 revenue of **$45.183 billion** accompanied **$23.275 billion in cost of revenue** and **$3.301 billion in sales and marketing**. Its content cash expenditure and amortization differ, so a financing calculation must follow cash commitments and avoid charging the same production twice. Roblox reported **127 million average daily active accounts**, approximately **1.8 million daily paying accounts**, and an operating loss despite substantial bookings. Those figures support separate audience, payment, creator-cost and cash-recognition accounts; they establish no lunar conversion or retention rate. [Netflix annual report](https://s22.q4cdn.com/959853165/files/doc_financials/2025/ar/99482238-46b2-4d0d-b292-40e6781bdf03.pdf), [Roblox annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1315098/000131509826000024/rblx-20251231.htm).

The new scenarios deliberately explore worldwide businesses larger than the earlier prescribed service programmes. The reported company amounts remain in their original-year dollars; the following prices are independently specified USD2041 assumptions, not inflated transfers from those companies.

| Mature annual demand or price | Limited | Global | Breakthrough |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Distinct followers of shared sports/cultural output | 150 million | 600 million | 2 billion |
| Paid account-equivalents as a share of followers | 20% | 30% | 40% |
| Annual gross paid-account invoice | $120 | $240 | $360 |
| Additional advertising/rights receipts per follower | $6 | $18 | $36 |
| Separate games, education and IP licenses | 20 million | 80 million | 400 million |
| Annual invoice per separate license | $100 | $300 | $500 |
| Research/data/access contract demand | 2,000 | 5,000 | 20,000 |
| Annual invoice per research/access contract | $5 million | $20 million | $40 million |
| Professional/software institutional clients | 10,000 | 25,000 | 100,000 |
| Annual invoice per institutional client | $1 million | $2 million | $5 million |

The cultural platform contains **four competitions with eight organizations each**, alongside lunar-life programming and performance. Visiting teams and artists can participate without becoming permanent residents. The earlier [sports](../../../humanities/economics/sports.md) and [entertainment](../../../humanities/economics/entertainment.md) studies supply the programme concept and worker/venue questions; their hypothetical-U accounts are not converted into these dollars. Additional teams could change production, travel and rights costs. They do not mechanically multiply a unique audience.

Paid account-equivalents are a subset of followers, with household and individual usage folded into an explicit conversion assumption. Advertising and rights consideration excludes payments already included in the paid bundle. Separate licenses represent additional purchases, potentially by the same people; an included game or lesson creates no second sale. Audience size is reachable annual interest, not a measured number of monetizable impressions. The advertising yield therefore remains a distinct, demanding commercial assumption.

The institutional cases concern remote experiment access, instrument data, software, intellectual property and outsourced professional delivery. They do not assume that thousands of institutions buy physical visits. At mature demand, one facility serves up to 1,000 research contracts and 5,000 professional clients simultaneously, with 350 off-Earth staff and 20 MW of reserved power. The resulting $30 billion annual gross invoices per facility in the global case, and $65 billion in the breakthrough case, require valuable products and extensive paid external participation. Staff-hours, equipment occupancy, communications, buyer budgets and contract qualification remain unresolved. These are especially ambitious service businesses, not prices for ordinary research visits.

At all capacities commissioned and all demand realized, the four new service channels total **$26.5 billion, $228 billion and $1.860 trillion per year**, respectively. A small complex can reach the whole cultural audience, but its institutional capacity initially binds: at the first module's one service facility, mature receipts are **$16.5 billion, $108 billion and $625 billion**. The maximum audience is independent of the number of facilities; available institutional slots are not.

## Costs, capacity and revenue rights

Variable production, rights, Earth partners, distribution, visiting personnel travel and purchased inputs consume **50% of cultural invoices, 55% of license invoices, 65% of research invoices and 70% of professional invoices**. These are gross project invoices before the specified deductions. Earth subcontractors' production is not presented as lunar employment or local value added. Their paid contribution can support a useful off-Earth service, while only the remainder contributes to its financing account.

In addition, paid accounts churn at 25% per year and replacement or growth accounts cost $30 each to acquire. Licenses use 20% churn and $8 acquisition cost. Counts represent average annual billable equivalents; the model does not award a full year of receipts to explicitly end-year signups. It does not resolve subscription months or payment dates. Initial demand starts at 10% of the mature scenario and grows 35% annually until that scenario is reached. Slower adoption and smaller launch audiences are tested separately.

The cultural platform employs 200 resident staff, uses 5 MW, purchases $600 million of fixed services annually and funds $1 billion of annual replacement. Each institutional facility adds 350 staff, $400 million of fixed purchases and $750 million of annual replacement. Staff receive an assumed $250,000 annually. Replacement funding is deducted once and remains unavailable for expansion; actual asset retirement cohorts are not simulated. These charges are additional to the variable production shares.

Two constraints limit new service facilities: an assumed 15% of supported residents available for new service work, and **500 MW reserved for these activities per founding-scale equivalent**. Both are scenario allocations. They have not been reconciled into an engineering dispatch alongside all industrial growth loads. The model checks compliance with those allocations; it does not certify the allocations themselves. Hotels, arenas, pressure systems, passenger routes and safety provisions still require detailed design within the capital allowances.

The consolidated core retains the original manufacturing, computing, propellant and destination-service accounts, including its unqualified higher-value manufacturing orders and prices. At founding scale the source prices the selected manufactured goods at $2,000 per kilogram in USD2026 and supplies 3 GW of computing capacity; those capabilities and customers remain inherited conditions, not new evidence from this financing calculation. Its media, research and professional receipts are removed before the new ones enter, together with their specifically identified variable Earth-service costs. Original physical service assets, logistics and other expenses stay charged. No second computing business or additional fan tourism is added. Spectator accommodation remains an opportunity within the hospitality account, subject to additional beds, routes, induced demand and displacement of other visitors.

Below founding scale, a 5% common recurring-cost floor prevents the first module from receiving perfectly proportional cheap infrastructure. At and above founding scale, operating imports and provision reproduce the source's proportional costs. The capital fund collects the stated share of positive available cash; the default is 100%, with 70% tested. This is an optimistic coordinated funding arrangement across businesses and institutions, not an assertion that investors own every surplus. Service wages are already paid; taxes, debt, negotiated equity claims and further stakeholder entitlements are not specified.

## The financing and construction rule

First production begins at elapsed year eight, consistent with the source's bootstrap assumption. Industrial receipts ramp over five years; every later addition waits two years after a fully funded order and then follows the same production ramp. Operating costs apply to installed capacity even while output ramps, so idle capacity does not receive full sales or escape its support costs.

For each year, the fund pays imports, operating costs, service wages, acquisition, replacement funding and resource provision before determining investable cash. Negative balances consume the externally funded startup reserve. Subsequent industrial and service orders may use only accumulated **earned** cash after those losses, previous distributions and previous orders. No outside funding arrives after first production; GDP, projected discoveries, inventory valuations and future orders cannot pay an invoice. A negative cash balance stops the path and records the unmet payment. It does not silently refinance the project.

The fund normally distributes 20% of positive pooled cash and reserves the remainder for expansion. A demanded service facility must fit currently commissioned staff and power. Earnings can accumulate across years until its indivisible $15 billion order is funded. Industrial additions use the remaining earned cash, subject to a 1% requested annual growth rate, positive marginal operating contribution and the source's **1.122% high-export growth screen**. Applying that source screen proportionally to a small system is an unqualified extrapolation. It deliberately grants no faster self-replication merely because customers pay promptly.

Below full founding scale, industrial orders complete the allocated one-time deployment bill. Beyond that point, they use the source's marginal cost for extending an established system: **$5.985 trillion per additional founding-scale equivalent**, including its resource provision. That is a different cost basis from reproducing the original bootstrap campaign. Civil expenditure and local wage/resource requirements still need reconciliation; doubling marginal capital is an explicit sensitivity.

At 70% of the enclosed cap, the requested growth rate tapers to zero. The **6.468-million-resident architectural reference** then remains supported, together with its industry and recurring costs. Industrial demand is independent of installed capacity: the central scenario allows 30 times founding sales capacity, while a five-times case stops construction at that market limit. The cap is a physical planning condition, not a revenue-generating population target.

The policy is deterministic and seeks funded development. It does not maximize investor return or assign a monetary value to learning. Conditional annual demand and available cash govern its decisions; market success probabilities and new information are unestimated. This follows the earlier appraisal's distinction between staged decision options and a measured real-option value.

## Returns across elapsed time

The following are **pooled capital-recovery NPVs in trillion USD2041 at 3% real**, measured from the first construction payment. A horizon includes its final elapsed year. Only liquid cash above the continuing operating buffer is available at the endpoint. No facility sale, business multiple, perpetual franchise value or release of restricted resource provision is credited.

| Service scenario | Year 30 | Year 100 | Year 300 | Year 800 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Limited | Fails in year 19 | — | — | — |
| Global | −2.314 | −2.208 | −2.167 | −2.167 |
| Breakthrough | −0.055 | +2.947 | +3.462 | +3.465 |

The limited case ends with a $4.914 billion liquidity shortfall after spending its seed funding. Its saved cash-flow result describes funding spent through failure, not a valuation of an orderly closure; rescue, shutdown and continuing liabilities are uncosted. The global case starts financing industrial additions in year 19 and remains self-financing, yet does not earn 3% on the seed investment within any tested horizon. Its undiscounted recovery occurs in year 152; at 1% real, its 800-year NPV is **+$0.407 trillion**. The breakthrough case starts additions in year nine and recovers undiscounted funding in year 24. At 7% real, even its 800-year NPV remains **−$0.619 trillion**. Patient capital and commercial success have different roles.

The number of residents remains small while these service returns develop. At elapsed year 100 the global and breakthrough paths support about **27,400 and 30,300 residents**, respectively. Their annual new-service invoices are **$138 billion and $690 billion**. Neither receipt amount is calculated from those resident counts. By contrast, completing the full founding industrial capacity takes until **years 327 and 315**, and the enclosed cap until **years 704 and 691**. That long construction path is the price of combining a small seed with the conservative growth screen. An engineering-qualified faster industrial expansion could change those dates, but cash alone does not establish it.

For a direct timing-and-scope comparison, retaining the full original campaign, delaying service receipts to year 33 and adding the same expanded service businesses produces 100-elapsed-year NPVs of **−$25.259 trillion for global demand and −$19.847 trillion for breakthrough demand**. These fixed commitments support 250,000 residents from year 33; the staged alternatives produce much smaller physical systems at year 100. The difference therefore measures alternative development strategies, including deferred output and investment, rather than a pure financing gain for identical timed deliverables. Broader service receipts improve both strategies, but early earning and deferred construction change the capital exposed to that demand.

## What changes the conclusion

The sensitivities below use the 800-year window and show **3% pooled capital-recovery NPV, trillion USD2041**. Their very long physical trajectories remain conditional; most discounted value comes much earlier.

| Change from the staged policy | Global | Breakthrough |
|---|---:|---:|
| Central scenario | −2.167 | +3.465 |
| Distribute earnings without industrial or service expansion | −2.025 | +3.570 |
| Halve all new-service unit prices | −2.495 | −0.031 |
| Remove new institutional research and professional demand | −2.415 | +2.761 |
| Increase all new-service variable costs to 80% | Fails in year 70 | −0.493 |
| Collect only 70% of positive available cash | −2.341 | +1.583 |
| Double marginal industrial capital costs | −2.369 | +3.157 |
| Double common capital and recurring floors | Fails in year 113 | +1.388 |

The strong cultural and licensing case remains positive without the especially ambitious institutional contracts. Conversely, a large audience with weaker pricing or stronger talent, platform and production claims can lose its discounted advantage. The prices and retained margins deserve at least as much empirical attention as the headline audience count.

Distributing earnings without further expansion slightly improves financial NPV in both central markets. Industrial growth consumes money now to create productive and social capacity much later. It can be affordable and valuable to the complex's purposes without maximizing discounted distributions to its original funders. The five-times industrial-market case stops at 1.25 million supported residents instead of building expensive unused capacity; it leaves the independently specified cultural audience intact.

## Socioeconomic interpretation and the next evidence

This financial result concerns the pool that funds expansion and repays capital. It is one part of the whole-complex return. Workers can gain earnings and opportunities, customers can gain enjoyment or useful products, researchers can produce new knowledge, and future communities can use capabilities built earlier. Earth production displaced by these purchases, alternative uses of capital, environmental effects and heritage losses also belong in the comparison. The [original appraisal](README.md#stakeholders-and-the-return-each-receives) retains those stakeholder accounts and counterfactual methods.

One additional valuation screen asks what **net customer surplus beyond paid prices and alternatives** would contribute. If every follower in the successful global scenario received an additional $10 per year of such benefit, its discounted value at 3% over the 800-year path would be **$0.140 trillion**; in the breakthrough scenario it would be **$0.467 trillion**. At $50 per follower-year the respective values are $0.701 trillion and $2.335 trillion. These are valuation sensitivities, not measured enjoyment, willingness to pay or complete social NPVs. They do not pay construction invoices. Empirical valuation would need to establish additional benefit relative to alternatives and exclude overlap with existing benefit measures.

Research and software require an outcome-specific counterfactual: what Earth or robotic alternatives would deliver, what resources would be saved, and which additional discoveries or capabilities would depend on this access. Adding the full economic value of every customer's subsequent work to service revenue would duplicate benefits and ignore other contributors. GDP, wages, supplier turnover and capital distributions likewise cannot all be added as independent returns.

The most useful next work is concrete: specify a functioning first industrial module and its common dependencies; quote its deployment and carrying costs; sell and retain small paid cohorts across several sports and cultural products; test rights and acquisition expenses; and obtain scoped institutional contracts with demonstrated delivery capacity. Those observations can replace individual assumptions without abandoning the staged model. The present calculation establishes that a small off-Earth population is compatible with economically significant worldwide services, and that earned receipts can finance gradual industrial expansion. Whether that path rewards its full set of stakeholders depends on the products, contracts, capabilities and alternatives that people actually create.
