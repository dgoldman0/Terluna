# U prices under the nine export portfolios

U is the general economic unit used throughout the earlier studies: a common unit for valuing goods, services, assets and financial claims. The [world-economy comparison](../world_economy/comparison.md) scales its reference with 100,000 U of annual future-Earth product per person. That benchmark establishes the scale of that account; it does not give U a dollar exchange rate, define a consumption basket or prescribe a currency issuer. The [monetary study](../monetary_system/institutions.md) consequently separates real U valuations from nominal contracts and monetary institutions. Constant-U calculations hold prices fixed for their comparisons; they do not establish permanently indexed contracts or prevent an analysis of changing prices.

Here U serves the entire currently planned economic zone beyond Earth through enclosed development, before the Open Moon, planetary array and subsequent outer Solar System buildout. The zone includes the cities, towns, industrial sites, power, source operations, depots and transport in the matched provisioning account. Earth suppliers, customers, investors and other affected people remain stakeholders. U names the economic unit, while these assets and operations define the geographical and productive scope.

The [U trade and monetary transition plan](../monetary_system/trade_transition.md) develops the missing external-payment arrangements: genuine U pricing, invoice and settlement currency, balances held outside the zone, a single U or basket of U-related currencies, and partial reserves funded from investment and earnings. Those mechanisms must be coupled to this spending model before it can estimate U's exchange rate or monetary inflation. The price comparisons below remain conditional domestic spending experiments.

The nine portfolios can generate substantial spending pressure even with a small resident population. In a specified experiment, redirecting **1% of actual distributions into additional domestic expenditure** gives a **4.2–6.4% higher domestic final-use price level** around year 100 under the strong service market. This result assumes a price-responsive supply curve, an already funded baseline basket and the producer's illustrative valuation of domestic services. It is a conditional price-level comparison, not an estimated annual inflation rate. Under the same rule, the middle service market gives 0–1.0%. Neither spending rule has been established as the likely one.

## What the existing accounts establish

The [financial model](strategies.md) fixes real prices while allowing receipts, investment, productive capacity and distributions to change. Gross export receipts pass through operating purchases, renewal, reserves, investment and distributions. Only expenditure directed at output inside the zone competes for that output. Earth ownership does not by itself remove or create demand: an Earth investor can spend a distribution on local facilities, while a lunar owner can save it externally. This analysis varies the eventual expenditure rather than assigning every receipt to residents.

The producer supplies a base-price domestic final-use basket consisting of resident consumption, housing and civic services, plus domestic replacement work. It excludes exports and imported capital. Intermediate supplies and wages are already embodied in final uses and are not added again. The basket contains imputed civic-service and housing values, rather than an observed household budget or verified set of market purchases. Its domestic valuation includes the provisioning account's illustrative 30 USD2041/U convention. That convention remains a valuation assumption, not an adopted exchange rate; halving and doubling the basket value are separate sensitivities.

Three measures therefore remain separate. The domestic final-use index below combines consumption, civic services and domestic capital work. Identified import-price sensitivity measures the effect on the operating cash account. Physical capacity and construction schedules constrain possible supply responses but do not themselves determine either price index. A household consumer-price index, installation-cost index and complete import-price index would require more detailed transaction baskets. The [ABS methodological explanation](https://www.abs.gov.au/articles/frequently-asked-questions-faqs-about-consumer-price-index) distinguishes these kinds of price measures.

## A conditional price calculation

For each portfolio and dated window, let B be the base-price domestic final-use basket, D actual distributions, and I the cash committed to industrial and service expansion. The calculation scales B by installed capacity and uses ten-year average annual values ending at years 30, 100, 300 and 800. A failed funding path receives no sustained price estimate. The reported maximum annual response also shows the peaks hidden by averaging.

The experiment assumes additional domestic expenditure equal to sD + lI, above expenditure already represented in B. The fraction s describes additional spending from distributions, rather than investor residence. The factor l describes assumed extra basket spending associated with investment commitments. Investment orders include financing allowances and are not a measured domestic procurement flow, so this second channel requires an explicit assumption about both amount and order-year timing. The 10% experiment is not an estimate of local construction content; actual disbursement and commissioning schedules would be needed for construction-price peaks.

With d = (sD + lI)/B, prices and quantities satisfy

\[
PQ = 1+d,\qquad Q=(1+a)P^\epsilon,
\qquad P=\left(\frac{1+d}{1+a}\right)^{1/(1+\epsilon)}.
\]

P and Q are relative to the same dated portfolio without the additional spending or supply shift. The price index is 100P. The budget equation holds the assumed current-U expenditure on this basket fixed as prices adjust; it supplies the demand-side assumption that the fixed-price financial accounts lack. The supply elasticity epsilon is tested at 0, 1 and 3. The independent supply shift a represents productivity or additional deliverable output beyond the capacity already included in B. At epsilon = 1, for example, a 1% higher price induces a 1% larger supplied quantity, holding a fixed.

These are uncalibrated sensitivity cases. The additional quantities implied by positive supply elasticities have not passed the physical capacity checks; the fixed-supply case shows the response when quantities cannot increase. [Berg and colleagues' investment model](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a08a7f40f0b6497400061c/wp12274.pdf) illustrates why traded and nontraded goods, investment, saving and the price numeraire need explicit treatment. Its substitution and production elasticities measure different relationships and are not transferred here. [Gurara and colleagues' construction research](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2020/english/wpiea2020048-print-pdf.pdf) supports examining costs during investment surges, but terrestrial road-project coefficients do not calibrate lunar supply.

All ratios use the source account's consistent base valuation. A common rescaling of monetary units leaves the price experiment unchanged, so no absolute conversion of its dollar cashflows to U is asserted. Changing the relative valuation of the domestic basket does change the result. This distinction is tested explicitly.

## Nine portfolios over four horizons

The following experiment uses the strong worldwide-service market, s = 1%, l = 0, epsilon = 1 and a = 0, with the inherited basket valuation. Entries are **percent differences in the domestic final-use price level**, each against its own dated counterfactual. They are neither annual inflation nor a cumulative price path from year zero.

| Portfolio | Years 21–30 | Years 91–100 | Years 291–300 | Years 791–800 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Raw-resource price proxy | +7.8% | +5.0% | +3.6% | +3.2% |
| Commodity refined materials | +5.9% | +4.2% | +2.6% | +2.1% |
| Specialty products | +6.1% | +4.4% | +2.9% | +2.4% |
| Premium lunar manufacturing | +8.0% | +6.4% | +3.1% | +1.9% |
| Propellant and logistics | +8.1% | +5.7% | +4.2% | +0.9% |
| Computing and digital exports | +7.9% | +5.1% | +3.7% | +3.3% |
| Services with domestic support | +8.0% | +5.2% | +3.7% | +3.3% |
| Diversified portfolio | +6.6% | +4.6% | +3.1% | +2.6% |
| Earlier manufacturing mix | +7.4% | +4.6% | +2.8% | +1.9% |

The difference among portfolios follows actual distributions relative to the imputed domestic basket. Around year 100, for example, the premium case has average annual distributions of $271.2 billion against a $20.7 billion basket in USD2041. The diversified case has $280.6 billion against $29.8 billion. The assumed 1% extra spending therefore produces different demand shifts even though their distributions are similar. These amounts do not imply that residents own the capital or receive the distributions. All nine portfolios retain the same worldwide-service demand scenario, including the cultural audience.

The earlier capacity-headroom percentages cannot substitute for these price calculations. They measure neither the marginal response of this final-use basket nor expenditure in U. Even the table above depends more strongly on the spending and supply assumptions than its rounded portfolio differences might suggest. Holding this experiment's basket and supply response fixed, raising s from 1% to 5% increases the year-100 price-level differences to **19.4–28.7%**. Setting s to zero by directing distributions externally gives zero additional spending through this channel; existing operating and construction pressures remain outside that distribution-only result.

## Sensitivity and deflationary pressure

The diversified portfolio at years 91–100 shows the importance of the assumptions:

| Change from the 1% distribution experiment | Domestic final-use price-level difference |
|---|---:|
| Fixed supplied quantity, epsilon = 0 | +9.4% |
| Responsive supply, epsilon = 1 | +4.6% |
| More elastic supply, epsilon = 3 | +2.3% |
| Extra 5% supply beyond installed-capacity scaling | +2.1% |
| Also spend an assumed 10% of investment commitments on the domestic basket | +5.9% |
| Domestic basket valued at half the inherited amount | +9.0% |
| Domestic basket valued at twice the inherited amount | +2.3% |
| No additional spending, with the extra 5% supply | −2.4% |

These comparisons give a useful threshold without selecting an inflation target. In the responsive-supply case with no extra supply shift, a 2% higher price permits a 4.04% expenditure increase. Across the nine strong-market portfolios at year 100, that corresponds to approximately **0.31–0.48% of distributions** being spent additionally on the modeled domestic basket. Under the 1% spending experiment, keeping its price unchanged instead requires approximately **8.5–13.1% extra deliverable basket output** beyond the capacity already in the financial path. These thresholds depend on the imputed basket; they are not recommended distribution limits or engineering claims.

Logistics improvements, automation, material substitution, better equipment yields and useful research can increase deliverable output or lower input requirements. Whether their benefits exceed new expenditure is the price question. More service exports alone do not lower domestic costs. Conversely, falling service or manufactured-product export prices can reduce purchasing power even while some local prices fall. The [IMF explanation of export booms](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/series/back-to-basics/dutch-disease) distinguishes spending from resource movement; the direction of a currency exchange rate additionally depends on monetary arrangements.

A separate, fixed-output sensitivity raises identified core import prices by 10%, leaving other prices and receipts unchanged. Across the strong-market portfolios at years 91–100, annual available cash falls by **$4.0–7.0 billion in USD2041**. This excludes unidentified import content within the new service-cost allowances and does not reprice the whole economy. The opposite price shock gives the corresponding first-order cash improvement. It establishes an exposure, not a forecast of import-price inflation or a revised investment NPV.

## Growth, the cap and remaining inference

Construction expenditure precedes commissioned supply, so a staged programme can experience temporary upward pressure before new facilities ease it. Financing expansion from earned cash controls funding requirements but does not eliminate this timing problem. The current price experiment retains the actual installed-capacity path; its separate construction case assumes spending at commitment and does not resolve a construction draw schedule. The saved annual maxima identify modeled peaks, not a forecast of their market clearing.

In the successful financial paths, growth of the domestic basket generally reduces distribution spending relative to that basket over the centuries. Each horizon in the table is a fresh price-level comparison; the percentages must not be added or compounded. The paths also remain below the architectural population cap. The separate imposed-cap operating accounts cannot be treated as funded equilibria: eight of the nine strong-market cap cases have operating deficits under the existing market limits.

At a genuinely sustained cap, stable expenditure and stable supplied quantities can support a stable price level. Continued expenditure growth against fixed supply produces further inflationary pressure; continuing productivity growth can instead support deflation or higher real expenditure. A constant population alone specifies neither outcome. The weaker-market failures likewise establish no deflation forecast: spending contraction, import shortages and unpaid obligations can coexist.

This experiment identifies the assumptions that control U prices and quantifies their consequences under all nine portfolios. An annual inflation forecast would additionally require current-U spending and credit dynamics, separate consumer and investment baskets, contractual and import-price responses, and supply responses constrained by the actual physical processes. A joint price-and-finance model must then let prices change operating costs, construction decisions, quantities and distributions. Until that feedback is implemented, the existing real-return results remain the fixed-price counterfactual rather than returns recalculated under the price experiments.
