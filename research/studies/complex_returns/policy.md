# U monetary policy, financial returns and payment resilience

13 November 2026. **Mild monetary accommodation can improve financing, but its strongest demonstrated use here is sustaining otherwise viable operations through payment delays.** A small reduction in U-local loan rates produces modest borrower savings. Wider supplier acceptance can replace other external funding and sometimes advance useful capacity. A temporary receivables facility produces a larger change in payment resilience, provided it combines actual foreign assets with voluntary term saving and a strict allowance for additional circulating balances.

U retains its meaning as the general economic unit and possible single currency or basket of U-related currencies. Its service boundary is the presently planned off-Earth economic zone before the Open Moon, planetary array and subsequent outer Solar System buildout. Earth investors, customers and suppliers remain participants in the financial account. Earth currencies can form part of the reserve portfolio without defining the U basket.

This study extends the [credit-supported expansion comparison](credit.md), preserving its nine alternative industrial portfolios, worldwide cultural and professional service markets, physical limits and provisioning architecture: **14.5006 trillion establishment / 41.8919 trillion campaign USD2041**. Numerical money amounts below are constant USD2041-equivalent valuations. They establish neither a nominal U exchange rate nor future nominal interest rates. All policy contracts and behavioral parameters remain scenarios.

## What is compared

The reference is the existing international-U financing path: commercial lending at 3% real, twenty-year principal amortization after two years of grace, and eligibility for suppliers to retain U term claims against 25% of identified external capital purchases. The existing retained-earnings path continues to supply the common price-pressure control. Three experiments answer different questions.

| Experiment | Change | What its result measures |
|---|---|---|
| Modest rate transmission | Offer new U-local loan portions at 2.75% or 2.50%; retain original origination dates and principal schedules | Borrower interest saving and corresponding reduction in bank gross income |
| Wider capital-supplier acceptance | Raise eligible external-capital supplier-U share from 25% to 50%; rerun the unchanged investment controller | Funding substitution and any resulting change in real investment timing |
| Temporary receivables support | Bridge three-, six- or twelve-month delays in customer payments, using a monthly ledger | Payment continuity, collateral demand, reserve use, saving claims and financing cost |

The first experiment leaves outside-lender and matched supplier-claim interest unchanged. It also keeps the 3% real project-appraisal hurdle unchanged. The [ECB's transmission account](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/mopo/intro/transmission/html/index.en.html) distinguishes policy rates from indirectly affected bank loan rates and describes uncertain transmission. Accordingly, a 25–50 basis-point pass-through is an assumption; a U policy decision does not automatically lower every creditor's required return.

The second experiment changes actual financing eligibility and therefore can alter construction dates. Applying rate relief to that new schedule gives a combined funding comparison, but the study does not reinvest the resulting interest savings or optimize a new response to lower rates. Investment gains from that additional feedback remain unmeasured.

The rate saving is small because the relevant U-local loan portion is small. At fifty basis points, affected borrowers save **133.6–239.9 million** in undiscounted interest over the realized strong-market loan schedules. The 25-basis-point result is half as large. For that portion alone, the fully amortizing annual debt-service coefficient falls from 8% to 7.5%, increasing the algebraic coverage-limited borrowing allowance by 6.7%. Complete-package funding, physical capacity, collateral and the price rule can still bind before that allowance is used.

The borrower saving equals the bank's reduction in gross interest income on the fixed schedule. Its consolidated transfer value is zero before changes in intermediation costs, risk and real output. A subsidy that restores bank income would need an explicit funder. Neither the reduced interest expense nor newly issued principal is additional socioeconomic production.

Wider supplier acceptance mostly substitutes U-denominated external claims for other external borrowing. In the diversified case, cumulative supplier-U finance rises from **21.36 to 42.72 billion**, while productive outcomes stay the same. Computing behaves differently: the changed eligibility permits a different complete financing package, accompanied by more outside borrowing, and improves operating-minus-capital NPV by **3.80 billion at year 30, 11.54 billion at year 100 and 12.48 billion at year 800**. These gains are specific to the controller and assumed acceptance; they do not establish a general return from increasing the U share.

At the baseline strong-demand schedules, the discounted rate savings have effectively reached their final value by year 100. A longer horizon alone does not keep creating an interest benefit once the affected debt has amortized. Middle-market and failed paths remain in the data product; monetary support does not remove their original market limits or turn negative whole-programme returns into positive ones.

## A facility for late customer payments

The liquidity experiment begins immediately after each year-30, -100, -300 or -800 checkpoint. Annual sales, operating costs and existing debt-service obligations are held at that checkpoint's rates for the episode. For the first three, six or twelve months, customers receive invoices but delay payment by the same number of months. Later invoices are paid normally; the original invoices then clear through an explicit queue. The model appends twelve further months and checks collection and repayment. This is a temporary disruption, not a permanent extension of trade-credit terms or a forecast over centuries.

During the entire episode, including the collection tail, ordinary owner payouts, new investment orders and reserve contributions pause. Existing restricted construction proceeds, noncash supplier prepayments and separately owned custody assets remain unavailable. Initial usable cash is project cash minus restricted external loan proceeds. It includes the operating buffer: consuming that buffer is part of emergency survival. Cash earmarked from the project's own resources for future orders can also be retained when those orders pause. Thus successful payment continuity does **not** preserve the original expansion and payout schedule. The freeze is a common conservative experimental rule; an operational facility could release restrictions sooner after repayment and restored liquidity.

The comparison includes cash alone; a U facility with unabsorbed local balances; a U facility with voluntary term absorption and either 25% or 50% acceptance by uncovered external operating suppliers; a stress in which only half the reserve assets are accessible; and an externally funded bridge priced at an assumed 6% real. Supplier acceptance never changes the currency required by existing outside lenders. Cash pays their obligations first; the uncovered remainder requires outside settlement assets.

Each bridge is capped at 25% of original seed funding and 75% of outstanding qualified receivables. It charges 3% real plus a 0.25% origination fee; term holders receive 3% real. The external benchmark uses the same collateral and facility caps but requires a committed outside lender. The [Bank of England operations guide](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/markets/bank-of-england-market-operations-guide/our-tools) supports the institutional distinctions among eligible borrowers, collateral, haircuts and temporary liquidity. It supplies none of these lunar parameter values.

### Monetary and reserve limits

Additional spendable local balances have one fixed allowance throughout each episode:

\[
\Delta M_{\max}=kB\left(P_{\max}^{1+\epsilon}-P_0^{1+\epsilon}\right),
\]

where the inherited annual expenditure basket is \(B\), holding time \(k=90/365\) years and supply elasticity \(\epsilon=1\). The permitted price level is the lower of 0.5% above the checkpoint level and the existing 5% cumulative premium over the matched control. The model does not renew this allowance each month. The closure is a conservative balance-pressure screen, not an estimated CPI response to emergency lending.

The inherited basket covers imputed resident consumption and domestic capital formation. It does not measure the entire demand for business working balances, financial saving or outside U assets. Enterprise payments and worldwide stakeholders are included in the financial ledger, but their independent demand to hold liquid U is not calibrated. Consequently the calculated allowance bounds this particular price closure; it is **not a ceiling on the monetary capacity of U as a whole**. Establishing additional business and international balance demand could permit more accommodation without the same term-saving requirement.

Local bridge balances beyond that allowance require willing holders to accept term claims. External suppliers can separately retain U term claims. Both have actual interest costs and remain locked until matched repayment. Upon repayment, term principal and interest are transferred into external saving assets; redeeming them into unrestricted local expenditure would need another price assessment. This contract assumption is material. A promise that recipients will save does not suffice, and a basket label does not provide their consent.

Foreign payments use actual reserve assets. The ordinary facility retains three months of future core-import needs plus 25% of existing convertible U exposure and new spendable local bridge balances. It also requires fund equity of at least 15% of reference U loans plus bridge loans. The import floor falls from the credit producer's normal one-year earmark during the emergency; a sensitivity preserves that full year. Equity is a net-worth constraint, while accessible foreign assets are a payment constraint. Counting both as independent piles of cash would double-count resources.

The bridge subledger holds the old loan book as a fixed conservative exposure reference while applying the checkpoint debt-service rate. It is not a monthly re-amortization of every legacy loan. New draws add loan assets and claims; conversion spends foreign assets and retires the converted claims. On repayment, the foreign-funded portion restores reserves and the other portions extinguish claims through matched asset transfers. No term redemption also replenishes unrestricted reserves. Intermediation income is reported before operating costs and losses.

## How much resilience improves

The following counts show strong-demand portfolios that pay all modeled obligations and repay the bridge. Each denominator is nine alternative portfolios. A failed case means the requested complete payment cannot pass one or more facility constraints; the model stops rather than spending a partial advance on an incomplete payment.

| Checkpoint | Cash only: six-month delay | U facility, 25% outside acceptance: six months | U facility, 50% outside acceptance: six months | Cash only: twelve months | U facility, 50% outside acceptance: twelve months |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Year 30 | 8/9 | 9/9 | 9/9 | 3/9 | 9/9 |
| Year 100 | 8/9 | 9/9 | 9/9 | 1/9 | 8/9 |
| Year 300 | 3/9 | 8/9 | 9/9 | 0/9 | 4/9 |
| Year 800 | 0/9 | 3/9 | 6/9 | 0/9 | 0/9 |

Both U columns include term absorption. Without it, the facility leaves these survival counts unchanged from cash alone: its first required local advance exceeds the small price-balance allowance. This does not show that lending is ineffective. It shows that preserving low additional price pressure requires a substantial saving or outside-holding counterpart to a large bridge.

For the strong propellant/logistics case at year 100, annual operating costs are **614.08 billion**, usable project cash is **162.90 billion**, and the six-month cash gap is **144.14 billion**. With 25% outside supplier acceptance, the facility uses **54.55 billion** of foreign assets, **18.18 billion** of outside U term claims and **71.31 billion** of local term claims. Only **0.091 billion** remains in additional spendable local balances. The separate reserve fund falls from **127.80 to 73.25 billion** of accessible foreign assets and is restored on repayment. The first draw occurs in month four and final principal repayment in month eight.

That episode costs the borrower **1.129 billion** in interest and origination fees, compared with **1.897 billion** for the assumed 6% externally funded bridge: a conditional financing saving of **0.769 billion**. Term-holder interest is **0.486 billion**; the intermediary retains **0.643 billion** before administration, reserve opportunity cost and losses. Undrawn commitment costs remain unpriced. These are allocations within the episode, not additions to real output. The operational benefit is the ability to continue paying bills; the value of avoided shutdown, asset sales or lost customers has not been estimated.

The same episode holds the checkpoint's **373.06-billion annual owner payout rate** and **13.56-billion annual new-order rate** back for twenty-four months. This exposes **746.13 billion** of modeled payouts and **27.12 billion** of new orders to delay. Paying those owner distributions at episode end instead of monthly costs **20.31 billion** in present value at 3% real, conditional on that release schedule. That delay cost is common to the U and outside-lender bridge comparison; it is not included in the quoted interest saving or asserted to be an optimized policy. Foregone returns from deferred construction are unestimated. These costs prevent interpreting payment survival as a free increase in whole-programme returns.

Larger service receipts can raise working-capital needs even when resident numbers are modest. The original buffer uses no-sales operating burn; it does not cover a full year of the much larger variable cost of fulfilling worldwide contracts. That explains why the later strong-demand checkpoints become more exposed. Their profitable operating accounts and their liquidity requirements describe different risks.

## Remaining financial risks and sensitivities

At year 800, the six-month U facility with 50% outside acceptance succeeds in six portfolios under the central supplier-geography assumption. It succeeds in all nine if the unclassified operating costs are local and in three if they are external. Holding the full one-year core-import reserve floor untouched reduces success to zero. Delaying only half the invoices lets all nine pass. Thus the headline resilience gain depends materially on supplier geography, payment interruption and an explicit emergency reserve mandate.

Freezing half the foreign reserve assets reduces six-month success from nine to five portfolios at year 300 and from six to one at year 800. Coordinated access across U-related issuers could avoid that particular fragmentation loss if their agreements actually make the assets available. The reference already assumes pooled access, so the study credits no further automatic basket efficiency or netting gain. Foreign funding remains subject to currency and maturity access, as the [BIS summary on cross-border liquidity](https://www.bis.org/publications/cgfs-paper-71-foreign-currency-funding-risk-and-cross-border-liquidity) emphasizes.

The facility cap remains tied to original seed investment, while reserve funding follows the inherited core-import account. Later failures therefore test those continuing institutional rules, not the maximum scale of a future U financial system. A facility that grows with qualified receivables and operating obligations could provide more support; its outside liquidity and loss capital would need funding alongside that growth. No additional reserves or financial capacity are silently credited here.

Permanent invoice impairment is tested separately against the receivables present at peak bridge use. In the year-100 propellant example, losing 75% of those receivables produces a **19.05 billion** bridge credit loss and leaves **108.76 billion** of fund equity. Created claims do not disappear with the asset write-down; the result preserves **89.58 billion** of issued U claims before resolution. This is a collateral-only recovery stress, not an operating forecast after such a sales loss. Losses on the externally funded benchmark belong to its outside lender; no U guarantee is assumed.

No scenario count is a default probability. No rate saving is counted as new social output. A genuine expected-return comparison would need customer credit evidence, interruption frequencies, contract-level supplier exposure, facility administration costs and the consequences of interrupted operations. Failed baseline paths receive no invented future receivables.

## Policy implication and reproduction

The supported direction is a selective programme: maintain routine accommodation as qualified demand for U balances grows; offer modest rate relief where local U funding actually matters; negotiate longer U holdings with capital and operating suppliers; and prearrange collateralized liquidity with an agreed reserve drawdown and loss-bearing institution. New expansion still needs a complete financing package, qualified customers and physical capacity. The model gives a stronger case for improving payment continuity than for increasing general circulating money to lower the whole campaign's financing bill.
