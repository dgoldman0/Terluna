# The proposal

The enclosed lunar buildout would put towns in craters and cities in lava tubes on the Moon from 2041, with the industry, power and supply of water that keep them. Whether it should go ahead turns on open questions: how much of what the settlements would sell Earth will buy, whether the technologies they depend on mature in time, and how the people living there would spend their pay. Fifteen years of focused research and development can answer most of them before the first money for the build is committed.

The study team proposes a fund of 100 million dollars, spent from 2027 to 2041 at about 6.7 million a year, with two tasks. The first is to answer the questions the decision to build depends on, with evidence the partners can act on by 2041. The second is to turn the work into things that earn and keep their value on Earth whatever is decided about the Moon: companies, licences, products, software and services, data, and media, experiences and education. In 2041 the partners then hold the evidence to commit to the build, or businesses, licences and assets that stand on their own, and most likely some of both.

The fund is small beside what it prepares. The build would need about 0.78 trillion dollars (1.21 trillion of 2041) before its settlements open in 2049, some eight thousand times the fund. The technologies the build shares with the wider space industry, such as cheaper launch, lie beyond its means. Within its means, it can find out whether the build would pay, bring the parts that only the build needs to the scale of ground demonstrations, and earn along the way.

# What must be known by 2041

The economic model of the buildout shows how far each uncertain input moves its central result, the number of residents the settlements can carry by 2341 while repaying their funding partners and paying their own way with Earth: 20.1 million in the central case (Appendix A). Five unknowns decide most, and the fund's work is built around them.

Launch prices decide most. If launch from Earth cheapens only on its slow path, the settlements stay at their first 12,500 residents and the funders earn 2.9% a year. The wider space industry sets those prices; the fund follows them, and its decision points use them as a gate.

Computing bought at the opening rests on the least evidence. Computing sold to Earth from lunar facilities is the settlements' largest line of sale, and the model assumes 5 GW of it under contract when they open in 2049, a figure of the study team's own: at 1 GW the settlements carry 8.1 million residents by 2341, and at 20 GW 48.2 million. The evidence so far is investors' expectation. A firm planning lunar data storage raised a seed round of 5 million dollars in 2023 [Reg23], and investors have put 450 million dollars since 2024 into one firm building computing in orbit, which has flown a single processor [HPC26; WM26], while priced contracts for computing in orbit or on the Moon have yet to appear in the team's search. The fund's work turns that expectation into buyers' terms.

Local industry comes next. If making equipment from lunar rock, the growth of the settlements' factories and the output of each worker advance only on their slow paths, the settlements reach their plan in 2174, 32 years late, and carry 12.9 million by 2341. Oxygen and metal have been extracted from imitation lunar soil in laboratories, and a factory that grows from its own output remains to be built.

How the residents spend is the study team's own figure: the model assumes they spend a tenth of their pay on goods and services from Earth. At a fifth the settlements carry 11.8 million by 2341, and at a twentieth 30.9 million.

Firm power matters most to the funders. On its slow path it barely changes the residents the settlements carry, but it delays the plan to 2148 and lowers the funders' return from 3.9% to 3.4% a year.

Food and life support decide less of the economic result: on their slow paths the settlements carry 18.7 million, and the funders' return falls from 3.9% to 3.8% a year. They remain what the residents live by, and the part of the work that meets the largest markets on Earth. People have lived inside closed systems for a year or two at most, two years in Biosphere 2 from 1991 and 370 days in Lunar Palace 1 in 2017–2018, as reported [SN96; LP1-C], and food from electricity has reached its first factory, of 160 tonnes a year [SF].

The settlements' own money matters less to the result, yet it must be settled before money can flow. In the model it carries a quarter more residents than the dollar (20.1 million against 16.2), and its issuer, reserve and clearing await design. Visitors' response to price moves the result by under a million across its published range, within the model's variation between neighbouring cases, and the tugs and the asteroids' water by under half a million.

Three questions lie outside the economic model and bear on any decision to build. Health over a lifetime at one-sixth of Earth's gravity, including pregnancy and childhood, is known only from stays of a few days at lunar gravity, while the settlements assume families. Enclosed living systems have run with people inside for two years at most, against the decades the settlements need. Lunar dust, which wears seals, joints and lungs, is known only from short missions.

# The work, 2027–2041

The work runs in three phases. Each ends at a decision point where the partners continue, narrow the work to what the evidence supports, or stop and keep what has been made.

**Evidence and first results, 2027–2031** (30 million dollars). The buyers of computing and travellers are studied, and the first terms gathered. The spending of people in remote and closed communities is measured. Laboratory work begins on food from electricity, water recovery and materials from imitation lunar soil. The closed test bed is designed. Media and education work begins, and its audiences are counted. *Decision point in 2031.*

**Demonstrations, 2032–2036** (40 million). The test bed is built and runs crewed campaigns of several months. Materials and construction from imitation lunar soil are shown at pilot scale, operated remotely and by automation. The first companies and licences begin to earn, and a visitor and education centre opens at the test bed. *Decision point in 2036,* where launch prices and the terms offered by buyers of computing serve as the gate to the third phase.

**Preparing the decision to build, 2037–2041** (30 million). The test bed's longest campaign, a year or more. Integrated demonstrations of the founding settlements' farms, water and materials. The design of the settlements' money. The evidence for the decision in 2040 and 2041.

The allocation is the study team's proposal, weighting the work by how much each unknown decides and how far the fund can move it. Local industry decides much and lies within reach, so it takes the largest share. Food and life support decide less of the economic result but are what the residents live by and carry the largest markets on Earth. Power and the tugs carry much of the funders' return. Launch decides most and lies beyond any fund of this size, so it is followed.

The evidence streams cost least and come first. Demand takes 7 million for studies of the buyers of computing and of travellers, letters of intent and pilot terms, and the audience counts from the media work; evidence costs little beside demonstrations, and demand decides most. The residents' economy and their money take 4 million: how people in remote and closed communities spend, and the design of the settlements' money, its issuer, reserve and clearing, tested in simulation and in a pilot within the fund's own community.

The demonstrations take most of the fund, each priced against comparable work. Food and life support take 20 million for crops under efficient lamps, food from electricity, water recovery, and a closed test bed where people live with them. Biosphere 2 cost an estimated 150 million dollars to build [Sci01], beyond this fund, while a cabin of Lunar Palace 1's reported size, about 150 m² [LP1-C], lies within it, and food from electricity is pursued with partners already making it, whose first factory cost about 42 million euros [SF]. Local industry takes 26 million for oxygen and metal from imitation lunar soil, construction from local material, the first cells of a factory that makes its own equipment, and remote operation of machines across the delay of signals to the Moon. A complete ground demonstration of a key technology costs about that much: the compact fission reactor test KRUSTY cost under 20 million dollars from design to final test [KRUSTY20], and the lunar construction contract awarded to ICON in 2022 is worth 57.2 million over about six years [NASA-ICON22], so the fund shares each demonstration with industrial partners.

Power, tugs and the asteroids' water take 12 million for the parts specific to the settlements: storage through the fourteen-day lunar night, receivers for power beamed from orbit, prospecting data on near-Earth asteroids, and extraction of water from imitation asteroid rock. Larger work in this field is followed; one firm building power beaming from orbit has raised 60 million dollars for its first demonstration [TC25]. Following the work of others, on launch prices, large power systems and research on health at partial gravity, takes 3 million and reports at each decision point. Design and simulation tools take 5 million to keep the economic and engineering models current with each result and to build design tools for closed settlements. Media, entertainment, experiences and education take 12 million for a slate of modest projects built around the work, a visitor and education centre at the test bed, and education programmes. Running the fund takes 7 million, and 4 million is held in reserve for the decision points.

# What the work earns and leaves behind

The work is chosen so that most of it has buyers on Earth whatever is decided about the Moon:

| Line | Sold to | Evidence of buyers | Independent of the build |
|:----------|:--------|:--------------------|:----|
| Food from electricity | food makers | the first such protein sells at about the price of whey protein, and its maker targets 4.30 to 5.20 euros a kilogram from its second factory [AGF25; GQ25] | Yes |
| Water recovery | remote sites, ships, dry regions | established markets for water reuse | Yes |
| Metal and oxygen from rock by electrolysis | metal makers | a firm developing a lunar process raised 6 million dollars to make iron on Earth free of emissions [MIN22] | Yes |
| Construction from local material | builders | the lunar construction contract above [NASA-ICON22] | Yes |
| Remote operation and autonomy of heavy machines | mining and construction | three firms raised 30, 115 and 200 million dollars in 2019–2026 [RR24; SA26a; SA26b] | Yes |
| Design and simulation software and services | remote and closed sites, space firms | the fund's own tools, offered as services | Yes |
| Media and entertainment | audiences | the launch of the crewed Artemis II flight around the Moon drew 18.1 million US television viewers in April 2026 [Wrap26] | Partly |
| Visitor and education centre | visitors and schools | Space Center Houston took 61 million dollars in 2024 from about 1.3 million guests a year; Biosphere 2 draws over 100,000 visitors a year [SCH; PP-SCH; VT-B2] | Yes |
| Market evidence and test data | space firms and investors | the fund's studies and test results | Partly |

In the study team's estimates from these benchmarks, licences pay little directly. US research institutions earn 3.5 to 4 cents of licence income a year for each dollar they spend on research a year, the median institution far less than the average [AUTM22; AUTM23], so at that rate the fund's technical work, about 4 million dollars a year, would bring licence income of about 0.15 million dollars a year; royalties on high-technology licences average about 5% of the licensee's sales [LES21]. Companies carry most of the value. The US average of about 11 companies formed a year for each billion dollars of research [AUTM22, derived] predicts about two-thirds of one company from the fund's fifteen years of technical work. The fund aims for three to five, in food, water, materials, construction and remote operation, by choosing work with buyers on Earth from the start; it holds a share in each, and each decision point judges it on that count.

Visitors and education earn steadily. A centre drawing 100,000 to 300,000 visitors a year, at the 35 dollars a guest Space Center Houston takes from its programmes [PP-SCH, derived], would take in about 3.5 to 10 million dollars a year, though its running costs can be as large: Biosphere 2 spent about 17 million dollars a year in 2001, much of it on energy [Sci01]. Media earns on rare successes. The top 1% of games on Steam take 84.6% of the store's revenue, the median game has earned 3,558 dollars, and one game in seven passes 100,000 [WN26], while four in five of the games released there in 2022–2025 cost under a million dollars to make, by one analyst's estimates [GDRep26]. The fund therefore makes several small projects, each within reach of its budget. The documentary Apollo 11 took 12 to 15 million dollars at the box office [TN-A11; BOM-A11]; a success of that kind is possible and stays out of the estimates. The surer return is the audience the slate builds and counts, which is evidence for the demand work.

Taken together, the study team's estimate is that by the late 2030s the fund's own lines could earn a few million dollars a year, enough to cover part of its running costs. Its larger value lies in what it leaves: companies, licences, the test bed, data and an audience. If the build goes ahead these carry into it, the test bed becoming the founding settlements' pilot plant, the companies their suppliers and the audience their first visitors and viewers; otherwise they stand on their own.

Two earlier efforts show what the fund must avoid. Breakthrough Starshot pledged 100 million dollars in 2016 toward a flight to another star; one participant counts about 4.5 million spent on some 30 contracts, and by 2025 the programme was on hold with laboratory results only [SciAm25]. Vertical farms grew ahead of their market: two of the best funded, which had raised about 885 million dollars between them, closed or went bankrupt in 2023 and 2024, and an industry analysis names the cost of capital and of electricity as the causes [FF25; FD23]. The fund's answer to both is money released in tranches against delivered results, and products sold before they are scaled.

# Partners and decisions

Funding partners commit the 100 million dollars in tranches tied to the decision points and hold the decision rights in proportion to what they commit. Research partners, institutions and companies, carry out the work under contract and share in what they invent, while industrial partners co-fund demonstrations whose results serve their own markets, supply equipment and take licences. Customers and audiences, the buyers of computing, travellers and viewers, are the market evidence itself, through the terms and numbers the fund gathers. A small fund team runs the work, keeps its evidence and accounts and prepares each decision, and an independent review panel reads each stream's results before every decision point and reports to the partners.

What the work invents belongs to the fund. It licenses it free of charge to any later buildout the partners join, and on market terms to everyone else. Income from licences, from the companies spun out and from the media, visitor and service lines goes first to the fund's running costs, and then, by a rule set when the fund is founded, either back into the work or to the funding partners.

At each decision point the partners receive the evidence gathered against the questions above, the state of each demonstration against what the build assumes, the fund's earnings and assets, and the review panel's report. The 2036 decision also reads launch prices and the terms offered by buyers of computing against what the build needs, since the economic model shows the build depends on both.

# Risks

The evidence may come back against the build, with demand for computing or visits proving thin or the technologies lagging. Finding that out is the fund's first purpose, and the decision points let the partners stop early and keep what has been made. What the build needs most, launch prices and large power systems, lies with the wider space industry; the fund follows them and leaves rockets and reactors to others. Media returns depend on rare successes, so a slate of small projects spreads the bet, and the audiences it gathers serve the demand work whatever a title earns. Companies and licences pay late, mostly after the work they rest on, and the fund's own earnings in its first years are small. Partners change over fifteen years, and tranches tied to the decision points, with rights in proportion to commitments, let partners enter and leave while the work goes on.

# Appendix A. The unknowns in the economic model {-}

The economic model steps year by year from 2041 to 2341 and finds the fastest steady growth the settlements' own accounts can carry, under tests the scoping study sets out: the funders repaid with a return and, once repaid, receiving at least as much as they put in over every ten years, the settlements' own work staffed and the water open to mining lasting. In its central case they carry 20.1 million residents by 2341. Each row moves one input, everything else central.

| Input moved | Setting | Residents by 2341 |
|:------------------|:--------|------------:|
| Computing contracted at the opening (5 GW centrally) | 1 GW · 20 GW | 8.1 million · 48.2 million |
| Earth's demand for all lines | low · high | 12,500 throughout · 20.2 million |
| The whole technology stack | slow · fast | 5.9 million · 30.2 million |
| Share of pay residents spend on Earth (10% centrally) | 20% · 5% | 11.8 million · 30.9 million |
| The settlements' own money, against the dollar | dollar alone | 16.2 million |
| Visitors' response to price (1.25 centrally) | 0.5 · 2 | 19.4 million · 20.6 million |

High demand adds about 0.1 million, because its sales at the depot draw the asteroids' water so fast that faster growth past the plan would exhaust it before 2341.

The second table moves each group of the technology stack alone to its slow and its fast path, with the rest central. The central case reaches the planned 6.47 million residents in 2142, and its funders earn 3.9% a year.

| Group moved alone (slow · fast) | Residents by 2341 | Plan reached | Funders' real return, a year |
|:------------------|:------------|:--------|--------:|
| Launch from Earth and the Moon | 12,500 throughout · 18.9 million | — · 2134 | 2.9% · 5.1% |
| Local industry, equipment and output per job | 12.9 · 22.6 million | 2174 · 2130 | 3.9% · 4.0% |
| Food and life support | 18.7 · 19.7 million | 2143 · 2142 | 3.8% · 4.0% |
| Power | 19.2 · 19.8 million | 2148 · 2141 | 3.4% · 4.1% |
| Tugs and the asteroids' water | 19.7 · 19.7 million | 2141 · 2142 | 3.8% · 4.0% |
| Computation per watt (unread by the model, which sells computing by the watt) | 20.1 · 20.1 million | 2142 · 2142 | 3.9% · 3.9% |

On the fast side, local industry alone brings the plan to 2130 and carries 22.6 million by 2341, and launch alone brings it to 2134 and raises the funders' return to 5.1% a year, though its residents by 2341 fall a little short of the central case, within the model's variation between neighbouring cases. The slow side of each row is the robust reading.
