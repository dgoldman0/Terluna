abstract: |
  A fund of 100 million dollars, spent from 2027 to 2041, settles the questions on which a decision to build enclosed settlements on the Moon from 2041 depends. Six questions decide that decision: whether buyers on Earth would contract computing from the Moon, how fast launch from Earth cheapens, whether the settlements' factories can grow from their own output, how their residents would spend, how their money would work, and how people stay healthy over a lifetime there. The fund answers what can be learned on Earth, through market evidence and ground demonstrations, and follows what lies beyond it: launch prices, which the wider space industry sets, and the effects of a lifetime at lunar gravity, which need the Moon itself. It decides three times, in 2031, 2036 and 2040, whether to go on. Its work is chosen to earn on Earth whatever is decided about the Moon, and it leaves companies, licences, a test bed, data and an audience either way.

  Amounts are in dollars of 2026; the costs cited for past projects are in the dollars of their day.

# What is known

The settlements' founding technologies already work at small scale. Oxygen and metal have been extracted from imitation lunar soil in laboratories, and a firm developing that process has raised money to make iron on Earth by the same means [MIN22]. A compact space fission reactor was taken from design to its final full-power test for under 20 million dollars [KRUSTY20]. Crops grow under efficient lamps, food made from electricity has reached its first factory, of 160 tonnes a year [SF], and people have lived inside closed living systems for up to two years, in Biosphere 2 from 1991 and, as reported, for 370 days in Lunar Palace 1 [SN96; LP1-C].

The markets for the same work exist on Earth. The first protein made from electricity sells at about the price of whey protein, and its maker aims at 4.30 to 5.20 euros a kilogram from its second factory [AGF25; GQ25]. Remote operation of heavy machines has drawn 30, 115 and 200 million dollars of investment into three firms since 2019 [RR24; SA26a; SA26b], and the lunar construction contract awarded in 2022 is worth 57.2 million dollars over about six years [NASA-ICON22].

The economics of the buildout are mapped. A year-by-year model of the settlements' economy from 2041 to 2341, with the engineering of each year, finds that on central assumptions the settlements repay their founding capital, 1.14 trillion dollars at its deepest, about three decades after they open in 2049, and grow to millions of residents. The same model shows which inputs decide that answer, and they are the questions below.

# What remains to be learned

Six questions decide whether to build:

1. **Computing bought from the Moon.** Computing sold to Earth is the settlements' largest line of sale, and the model assumes buyers contract 5 GW of it at the opening, a figure of the study team's own. At 1 GW the settlements carry 8 million residents by 2341, at 20 GW 48 million. Investors have backed the idea, with 450 million dollars in total since 2024 for one firm building computing in orbit [HPC26; WM26], and priced contracts from buyers have yet to appear.
2. **Launch prices.** If launch from Earth cheapens only slowly, the settlements stay at their founding size and the return to the build's funders falls to 2.9% a year. The wider space industry sets these prices.
3. **Factories that grow from their own output.** If the settlements' industry advances only slowly, they reach their planned 6.47 million residents in 2174, 32 years later than on central assumptions, and carry 12.9 million by 2341 against about 20 million.
4. **How the residents spend.** The model assumes residents spend a tenth of their pay on goods and services from Earth. At a fifth, the settlements carry 12 million residents by 2341; at a twentieth, 31 million.
5. **The settlements' money.** A money of their own lets the settlements grow about a quarter larger than the dollar would, and its issuer, reserve and clearing are yet to be designed. How Earth's buyers take to paying in it matters as much: if they bought a quarter less, the settlements would carry 15 million residents by 2341 in place of 20.
6. **Health over a lifetime.** The settlements assume families who live their whole lives at a sixth of Earth's gravity. Radiation is well understood and the designs shield against it with soil and rock; dust and closed air have measured limits for crews of months; and the course of pregnancy and childhood at lunar gravity is unknown, since the longest human stay there lasted 75 hours.

**Evidence and first results, 2027–2031.** The buyers of computing and travellers are studied and their first terms gathered, the spending of people in remote and closed communities is measured, laboratory work begins on food, water and materials, the closed test bed is designed, and the media work begins to build and count an audience. *Decision in 2031.*

**Demonstrations, 2032–2036.** The test bed runs crewed campaigns of several months, materials and construction from imitation lunar soil reach pilot scale, the first companies and licences begin to earn, and a visitor and education centre opens at the test bed. *Decision in 2036,* which reads launch prices and the terms buyers of computing offer against what the build needs.

**The decision to build, 2037–2041.** The test bed's longest campaign, of a year or more, integrated demonstrations of the founding settlements' farms, water and materials, the design of the settlements' money, and the evidence for the decision in 2040.

Funding partners commit in tranches tied to the three decisions and hold the decision rights in proportion to what they commit. Research and industrial partners carry out and co-fund the work, an independent panel reads each stream's results before every decision, and a small team runs the fund.

# What the work leaves and earns

The work is chosen so that most of it has buyers on Earth whatever is decided about the Moon:

| Line | Sold to | Evidence of buyers |
|:----------|:--------|:--------------------|
| Food from electricity | food makers | the first such protein sells at about the price of whey protein [AGF25; GQ25] |
| Water recovery | remote sites, ships, dry regions | established markets for water reuse |
| Metal and oxygen from rock | metal makers | a firm developing a lunar process raised money to make iron on Earth free of emissions [MIN22] |
| Construction from local material | builders | the lunar construction contract of 2022 [NASA-ICON22] |
| Remote operation and autonomy of heavy machines | mining and construction | three firms have raised 30, 115 and 200 million dollars since 2019 [RR24; SA26a; SA26b] |
| Design and simulation software | remote and closed sites, space firms | the fund's own tools, offered as services |
| Media and entertainment | audiences | the launch of the crewed flight around the Moon in April 2026 drew 18.1 million US television viewers [Wrap26] |
| Visitor and education centre | visitors and schools | Space Center Houston took about 45 million dollars from its programmes for about 1.3 million guests in 2023, about 35 dollars a guest; Biosphere 2 draws over 100,000 visitors a year [SCH; PP-SCH; VT-B2] |
| Market evidence and test data | space firms and investors | the fund's own studies and test results |

By the late 2030s these lines could earn the fund a few million dollars a year, in the study team's estimate. A visitor and education centre drawing 100,000 to 300,000 visitors a year would take about 3.5 to 10 million dollars a year, with running costs of the same order [PP-SCH, derived; Sci01]. Licences pay little directly: research institutions earn 3.5 to 4 cents of licence income a year for each dollar they spend on research [AUTM22; AUTM23], about 0.15 million dollars a year on the fund's technical work. Most of the value lies in companies, and the fund aims for three to five, in food, water, materials, construction and remote operation, holding a share in each. Media earns on rare successes, so the fund makes several small projects; the surer return is the audience they build, which is evidence for the demand work [WN26; GDRep26].

What the work invents belongs to the fund, which licenses it free of charge to any later buildout the partners join and on market terms to everyone else, and shares the income with the research partners who made it. If the build goes ahead, the test bed becomes the founding settlements' pilot plant, the companies their suppliers and the audience their first visitors and viewers. Otherwise the companies, licences, test bed, data and audience stand on their own. Two earlier efforts show the discipline this asks for. Breakthrough Starshot pledged 100 million dollars in 2016 toward a flight to another star, spent about 4.5 million on some 30 contracts by one participant's count, and was on hold with laboratory results by 2025 [SciAm25]. Two of the best-funded vertical farms, with about 885 million dollars between them, closed or went bankrupt in 2023 and 2024 on the cost of capital and of electricity [FF25; FD23]. The fund releases money in tranches against delivered results, and sells products before it scales them.
