# Culinary evaluation and product qualification

4 November 2026, revised 7 November 2026. The product programme of the [aquatic-food study](aquatic_novelty.md): what a breeding programme delivers to a cook, how products are compared fairly, and the evidence that qualifies a product for raw or cooked service.

## What a breeding programme delivers to a cook

The endpoint is an enjoyable, repeatable food with a working supply chain. More fat, more glutamate, a faster-growing fish or a mineral-rich habitat are intermediate traits on the way to it. The [chapters](aquatic_novelty.md#read-the-study) give the source evidence, and the table sets the product objectives.

| Product | Tissue properties to select and measure | Preparations to compare | Main confound to control |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 rich fish at different phases of the cycle | Fat distribution in edible muscle, melting, oxidative stability, clean aroma, acceptable firmness | Qualified raw slice, fully cooked nigiri topping, plain skin-on grill | Size, maturity, ration, catch stress, cut and time in storage |
| H2 sweet or savoury pale fish | Free taste-active compounds with a sensory benefit; clean aftertaste and bite | Plain steamed fillet, grilled fillet, qualified raw slice | Salt and sauce can hide the intrinsic difference; tissue chemistry is a different measure from preference |
| H3 pale fatty fish | Contrast of loin and belly, slice cohesion, water retention, favourable prey-derived fat | Thick and thin qualified raw cuts; steamed and roasted portions | Differences of diet or species read as an effect of carbonate habitat |
| Eel for unagi | Behaviour of skin and connective tissue, fat retention, fillet yield, boning and reheating | Grilled, steamed-and-grilled and glazed, with unsauced controls first | Sauce, cooking loss and thickness of cut can dominate an apparent effect of line |
| Other kabayaki-style fish | The same objectives, each fish judged as its own species | Catfish or jade perch fillets through a standard sequence of cooking and glazing | A shared preparation leaves the species and its flavour distinct from eel |
| Prawns and crayfish | Tail yield, sweetness, springiness, consistency across moult stages | Poached or steamed tail, grilled tail, cooked sushi topping | Moult stage, size, cooking loss, added salt, time since harvest |
| Food-qualified bivalves | Edible yield, tenderness and aroma, from a source of independently acceptable quality | Steamed meat, broths and other fully cooked products | Filtration and pearl quality say nothing of edible quality or safety |

Plain controls show the organism's own contribution, and culturally plausible recipes are assessed after them. A product may excel with glaze or smoke while its plain form is unremarkable, and the two outcomes are kept apart. Working names such as "fatfish", "sweetfish" and "butterperch" name hypotheses and carry no standing as species or as certifications.

## Measurements and fair comparisons

Samples are anatomically matched and follow a standard, humane sequence of harvest and handling. The record covers capture or harvest stress, size, sex and maturity, the local phase of the light cycle, feeding history, sample site, time to chilling, rigor and storage, freezing and thawing, and serving temperature, with biochemical and sensory measurements paired on edible portions.

The panel includes lipid classes and fatty acids, the location of fat, thermal behaviour, free amino acids, nucleotides and their breakdown products, collagen and connective tissue, instrumental firmness, water loss, colour, oxidation, and geosmin and 2-MIB. None of them alone measures deliciousness, and calculated equivalent umami is a chemical index whose sensory meaning is tested in each product's matrix.

Coded samples and serving order are randomized, trained descriptive testing is kept apart from consumer liking, and meaningful differences and their uncertainty are set in advance, so that a significant chemical change never becomes an unmeasured sensory claim. Comparisons repeat across families, cohorts and harvest phases, with analyses matched for body size and for age where different growth rates mislead a single comparison. A harvest calendar follows the local solar phase of the 29.5-day cycle and the measured lag of the food web.

For raw products, the validated freezing and thawing process is itself compared as a factor of quality. If freezing spoils one line's texture, tolerance of freezing joins the selection objective or that cut takes the cooked route. A cooking process is judged by the food it yields and its validated thermal exposure.

## Three qualification states

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Raw-qualified product | A defined line or species, source, season or phase, cut and processing chain carries the required evidence on hazards and quality; qualification can be suspended |
| Cooked-qualified product | The source is acceptable and its defined cooking and handling are supported; raw use is unestablished or not the intended product |
| Food use unqualified or excluded | The evidence is insufficient, the contamination unacceptable, or the organism serves ecology or materials; cooking makes such a product acceptable only where the evidence shows it |

The food-safety reference is an external benchmark. FDA's 2026 model Food Code, §3-402.11, keeps time and temperature schedules that destroy parasites, one of them freezing at −35 °C until solid and holding at −35 °C or colder for at least 15 hours [C1]; "flash frozen" names no such exposure. FDA's seafood guidance attends to the parasite's identity, the product's thickness and the freezing process, and unusually thick fish can need validation of their own [C2].

For each product, qualification covers the source's conditions, the relevant pathogens and parasites, toxins and chemical contaminants, the cold chain and the intended process. Freezing, curing and searing do different things, and none of them makes a raw product generally safe. Natural toxins can survive cooking and freezing [C3], and cooked products keep their own controls against later contamination.

Fishing can supply part of the heritage meat where the parasite risk is kept low ([register, 12 October](../../decisions.md#the-domain-synthesiss-decisions-12-october-2026)). Organisms will cross between Earth and the Moon over time, and the design plans for the crossing and mitigates its risks, the parasite risk of raw fish from the Moon's warm waters among them, with the DNA watermark telling planned lines from arrivals ([register, 6 November](../../decisions.md#the-food-systems-branchs-evaluation-wild-lines-from-earth-stock-the-eels-seawater-spawning-and-organisms-crossing-between-worlds-6-november-2026)). Surveillance therefore follows the source populations and food webs as they change, since a parasite absent at first can arrive later. A family selected for resistance can still carry an organism without showing illness, and a healthy-looking catch or clear water is no substitute for evidence on the source and the process.

Keeping the parasite risk low needs a defined comparison. A comparison with Earth's regulated sushi, an idea of the ideation session of 4–5 November ([aquatic foods](aquatic_novelty.md#ideas-from-the-ideation-session-of-45-november)), would specify the mix of species, products and processes, illness per serving, severe outcomes, uncertainty, the sensitivity of surveillance and under-reporting; the evidence gives risks by product and process, with no single figure for "sushi risk". Cooked products remain worthwhile whether or not a line reaches raw qualification.

## Raw preparation across the waters

The most controlled setting is the first route to reliable raw qualification, and the rest of the waters keep their place in the programme. Which habitats support desirable fish, which catches can meet a defined raw standard after processing, and which supply cooked foods are three separate questions, and the habitats below differ in the control they give over the hazards of raw products. The table states hypotheses about relative control.

| Habitat | Possible advantage | What qualification still needs |
|---|---|---|
| Clear, nutrient-poor freshwater coast | Low nutrient inputs remove one driver of harmful blooms [C5] | Productive margins, microbial exposure and toxins assessed; clarity is no measure of safety |
| Carbonate habitat with invertebrate prey | Chosen species can break particular parasite cycles: some freshwater flukes need a snail before their larvae infect fish [C6] | Prey or resident hosts can still carry transmission, and richness alone predicts neither safety nor danger |
| Moderately alkaline water or soda lake | The chemistry might exclude particular parasites or hosts | A pH near 8.3 permits many pathogens [C4], and cyanotoxins occur in Earth's alkaline habitats [C7] |
| Cooler highland water | Slower growth of some warm-adapted microbes | Highland water of 17–21 °C still permits microbial growth [C4]; the fish's performance through the whole cycle |
| Controlled freshwater grow-out | The most control over feed, inflow, introductions and waste | Controls through the life cycle; some freshwater parasites enter fish through the skin [C2] |

Blooms that follow the phase of the cycle, exposure to prey and oxygen stress are the likely clocks for monitoring and harvest limits, and the trials measure them. A community assembled without a parasite's needed host can stay free of the parasite after it arrives, which makes the choice of hosts a concrete ecological lever under continuing exchange. Processed feeds reduce exposure through infected prey, and parasites that penetrate the skin need controls of their own [C2]. Validated freezing for parasites supports raw use of a catch that is otherwise suitable, while it neither sterilizes fish nor removes accumulated natural toxins [C3]; the management of the site, hygienic harvest and handling, and the cold chain each keep their own role.
