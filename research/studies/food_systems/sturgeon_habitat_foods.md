# Sturgeon on the Open Moon: cold-lake adults, hatcheries, meat and caviar

7 November 2026 · `domain/food-systems` · literature assessment and research design

The Open Moon has no seasons and no cold fresh water. Its axis lies 1.5° from the ecliptic, so no polar winter forms, and its fresh water stays above about 15 °C everywhere: the deep highland lakes hold water near 21 °C, and only shallow ponds on the highest ground cool below 18 °C, in the last days of the lunar night (`research/studies/cold_waters/README.md` on `domain/hydrosphere`, 1ac9357d). Sturgeon spawn in cold water. Beluga spawn at 6–16 °C and their eggs and larvae develop at 9–15 °C [ST02], Siberian sturgeon embryos are incubated at 13–14 °C [ST01], an Aksay bester line incubated best at 16 °C [ST10], and white sturgeon females gave fertile eggs after seasonal holding at 10–15 °C while constant warmer holding impaired them [ST07]. No natural water on the Moon lets a sturgeon spawn: the coldest standing water, in ponds 0.5–1 m deep beside the coldest highland lake, stays below 18 °C for 4.5–39 hours of each 709-hour lunar day.

Adults can live where the water is coolest, deep in the coldest highland lakes, whose floors settle near 21 °C. The 543 lakes whose air falls below 20 °C in its coldest spell cover 220,000 km², and the coolest of them lie on the highest ground, 5–10 km above sea level, mostly near the far side's equator. Bester, the hybrid of a beluga female and a sterlet male, ranks first among the founders: captive bester have matured and spawned at intervals of 10–12 months while held at 20–21 °C [ST11], the warmest reproductive record among the candidates, and the hybrid carries beluga's size and roe with sterlet's earlier maturity. Sterlet and Siberian sturgeon follow as freshwater references. Beluga adults range through water of 20–31 °C in the Caspian summer [ST02], so pure beluga could live in the warm seas as a large fish-eater, bounded by the prey the nutrient-poor seas produce.

Reproduction therefore runs through hatcheries. A chilled hatchery conditions broodstock and incubates eggs at the temperatures sturgeon already use, with methods in hand, and keeps every generation dependent on it. Selection for warmer incubation could move spawning toward the lakes' own 21 °C over generations of slow-maturing fish, and it runs inside the hatchery while it proceeds, so the two are stages of one path (section 3). Every founder comes from captive stock: beluga and the wild Siberian sturgeon are Critically Endangered and sterlet Endangered on the IUCN Red List of July 2022, in which all 26 surviving sturgeon and paddlefish species are threatened [ST20, ST21].

## 1. Where sturgeon can live

Height cools a lunar lake little. The design years' air warms going down by 1.04 K a kilometre over land, against about 6.5 K on Earth, and half the area of geography's 27,865 rain-fed lakes lies above 4.0 km. Carried to each lake's level, the air's coldest 3-day spell falls below 20 °C over 543 lakes covering 220,000 km² and below 18 °C over 15 lakes covering 583 km²; no lake's air falls below 15 °C. The coldest air stands over lakes high above their cells' mean ground near the far side's equator, such as lakes at 5.5–5.7 km by 0.3° N, 139° E, whose coldest spell is 17.5 °C (cold-waters study).

The water runs warmer than the air, because the 354 hours of daylight heat it. The project's lake model at the coldest lake's site keeps a 30 m lake's surface at 26.7 °C on average and no colder than 21.9 °C in a 3-day mean; its floor averages 21.7 °C and never falls below 21.1 °C in ten years, and deeper lakes' floors settle at their surface's coldest water as well. The seas' deep water settles at 25–30 °C at 301 K, and the older sea-oxygen forcing puts the basins' means at 25–32.5 °C with a median sea surface of 29.6 °C. The deep highland lakes are therefore the coolest permanent water the Moon has, near 21 °C under a warmer surface.

Oxygen limits the coldest layer. Deep, nutrient-poor lakes keep 5.7–6.3 mg/L in their deepest tenth at 301 K by Osborn's rule and 3.5 mg/L under Shih and Bouffard's form (register, 19 October), which is 39–70% of the 8.94 mg/L that water holds at saturation at 21 °C under the design air *(computed with `biosphere/ecology/lake.py`)*. In beluga of about 281 g and 1.22 kg held for eight weeks at 18 ± 0.7 °C under 2–3, 5–6 and 9–10 mg/L, low oxygen impaired feeding and growth [ST12]. Sturgeon feed on the bottom, where the water is coolest and the oxygen lowest, so the adults' habitat is the deep water above the lowest tenth, and growth there needs the lakes' resolved oxygen profiles.

Bester ranks first for four reasons. Its maturation and repeated spawning at 20–21 °C [ST11] come closer than any other candidate's record to the highland lakes' water. It joins beluga's size, flesh and roe to the sterlet's smaller body and earlier maturity, so it supplies meat and caviar without waiting the 15–20 years most beluga females take to mature [ST02]. Its breeding has run through several generations, second-generation broodstock and a fourth-generation Aksay backcross among them [ST09, ST10]. And a hybrid line bred from captive stock puts no demand on Critically Endangered wild beluga. Its weaknesses are documented as well: hatching and spontaneous triploidy varied with the eggs' maturation state in second-generation bester [ST09], so ploidy, cross direction and family contribution are recorded for every line. Sterlet ranks second as the smallest and earliest-maturing species, the fastest to select, and the Siberian sturgeon third, as the widely farmed reference whose upper tolerance confines it to the highland lakes.

## 2. No water for spawning

The highland lakes give adults a home and give their eggs nothing. Beluga eggs develop at 9–15 °C [ST02], Siberian sturgeon embryos at 13–14 °C [ST01], European sturgeon embryos all died at 30 °C and failed to hatch at oxygen saturation of 50% or less [ST06], and the Aksay bester line incubated best at 16 °C [ST10]; the lakes' coldest water is 21 °C. Shallow ponds beside the coldest lake cool further at the end of the night: a 0.5 m pond falls to 16.9 °C and stays below 20 °C for 244 hours and below 18 °C for 39 hours of each lunar day, and a 1 m pond for 181 and 4.5 hours, while a 2 m pond never reaches 18 °C (cold-waters study). Those spells are hours long where incubation takes days, and the same ponds warm far above 20 °C through the day.

The ovaries need a cold season as well. In a small experiment all five white sturgeon females held through a seasonal 10–15 °C cycle gave fertile eggs after induction, while the constant treatments at 15 and 18 °C impaired reproductive performance [ST07], and the Moon offers no season to replace that cycle. Captive bester maturing at 20–21 °C [ST11] are the counter-example the programme builds on, with the conditioning and induction methods of that study still to be read in full. The lunar day gives sturgeon no substitute for a year: its long light cycle may shift benthic prey and feeding, while nothing in the sources read ties a sturgeon's reproduction to a 29.5-day cycle.

## 3. Chilled hatcheries and selection for warmer incubation

A chilled hatchery uses methods already in hand. Broodstock come up from the lakes into chilled holding through a seasonal cycle like the white sturgeon's 10–15 °C [ST07]; ovulation is induced, eggs are fertilized artificially and incubated near 13–16 °C [ST01, ST04, ST10], and juveniles return to the lakes. Its energy is small at the Moon's scale: it chills only broodstock and eggs, 5–11 K below the lakes' 21 °C, and for comparison the closed systems that would grow fed eel for 8 billion people at 1 kg of cooked eel each draw about 5.4 GW on average ([eel chapter](eel_habitat_hatchery.md), section 7), a grow-out load far larger than any broodstock unit. Its cost is dependence: every generation passes through the hatchery, so the lake populations last only as long as the hatchery runs, and every reproductive intervention enters the welfare record.

Selection for warmer incubation aims at lines whose eggs develop at the lakes' own temperatures. The evidence shows plasticity and variation: sterlet larvae warmed to 27 °C after day 17 improved several traits [ST05], lake sturgeon families responded differently to thermal regimes [ST08], and Aksay bester incubated at 18–20 °C grew faster after hatching, though with heavy early egg loss [ST10]. The distance is 5–8 K, from incubation at 13–16 °C to the lakes' 21 °C, and the ovaries must also mature without a cold season. Selection is slow in these fish: sterlet first reproduce at 3–5 years as males and 5–8 as females [ST03], so ten generations take 40–80 years, and most beluga females mature at 15–20 years [ST02]. Families are ranked on complete cohort performance, hatch losses and deformities included, since the fastest-growing survivors of a warm incubation can come from a cohort with heavy egg losses [ST10].

The two paths are stages of one programme. Selection needs controlled incubation at stepped temperatures, which only a hatchery provides, so the chilled hatchery starts the lines and the selection runs inside it, raising the incubation temperature a step per generation and keeping families split between the step and the cooler control. A line that spawns in the lakes' inflows would end the dependence; until one exists, the lake populations are managed stocks.

An idea from the review of 6 November, not yet started: the lunar night as the sturgeon's winter. Bester broodstock living deep in the coldest lakes would spawn into shaded, shallow, flowing inflows during the last ten days of the night, when ponds there stay below 20 °C for 181–244 hours of each lunar day, and selection would raise the eggs' tolerance by 2–4 K over generations. Its first check is the hydrosphere's lake model for a shaded, flowing reach 0.3–1 m deep at the 543 coldest lakes, counting consecutive cold hours against incubation times.

## 4. Living wild under the rules

Adults in the highland lakes live wild even when a hatchery breeds them, so every sturgeon line meets the rules for what lives wild: selective breeding first, then a minimal engineering step that gives the line the shared signals, reporting traits and DNA watermark (register, 12 October and 6 November; [machines](../../../biosphere/ecology/machines.md)). Temperature holds the lines to the coldest lakes, which answers the rule of no advantage everywhere, and selection for warmer incubation loosens that hold, so a warm-spawning line needs its limit set before it spawns in open water. The rain-fed lakes keep 96% of the land's phosphorus (the older food study, finding 8) and are held nutrient-poor (register, 19 October): a sturgeon harvest from them returns phosphorus to the land, while hatchery juveniles raised on feed bring phosphorus in. Beluga in the seas is a large predator whose prey demand comes first in any placement. Organisms will cross between Earth and the Moon, and parasites and pathogens arriving with founders, feed and connected waters are screened and watched for (register, 6 November).

## 5. Ecological roles and effects

Smaller benthic sturgeon turn locally renewed invertebrates into food; pure beluga places a larger claim on fish prey, which in the nutrient-poor seas must come from prey production after the prey's own replacement and the other predators' consumption. Cultured beluga eat formulated feeds [ST13, ST14], which moves their resource account to the feed's production. The first comparisons measure benthic consumption and disturbance with near-bottom oxygen; overlap with eels, shellfish and other benthic fish, and juvenile fish lost to beluga, against reaches without sturgeon; feed and prey inputs, retained biomass, excretion and the nutrients exported in meat and roe; mixing among founder and hybrid lines; and the health of animals moving between hatcheries and lakes. A mollusc or prey fish is counted once across human harvest, eels and sturgeon, within the waters' phosphorus limits ([resources' sea phosphorus](../../../resources/sea_phosphorus.md)).

## 6. Feed, standing biomass and oxygen

A caviar fishery supports years of immature females, resting females, breeding males and replacement cohorts, so its accounts record standing biomass through the years of little output; meat can come from earlier harvests, and a meat cohort, a cohort keeping females for roe and the reproductive reserve are compared side by side. In juvenile beluga of about 206 g, soybean or canola oil replaced fish oil over 120 days with no detected penalty to growth or feed conversion and with altered tissue fatty acids [ST13], and in a six-week trial on beluga of about 215 g, muscle and liver lipid rose with dietary oil [ST14]; neither abstract read gives the water temperature, and neither covers a reproductive diet or taste. The oxygen trial at 18 °C [ST12] gives growth under low oxygen at a temperature 3 K below the lakes' floor, and supplies no respiration rate for adults at 21 °C, larvae or breeding females.

| Account | Calculation and units | Required observations |
|---|---|---|
| Juvenile supply | Fertilized eggs = grow-out entrants / combined hatch and nursery survival | Survival at each transfer and feed change, at the incubation temperature; interventions and broodstock costs |
| Feed | Supplied dry feed integrated over each cohort's life | Ration by size and temperature, losses, growth, maintenance, mortality and maturation |
| Natural food | Prey consumption integrated by prey group and cohort | Diet, prey energy, assimilation and renewable prey production |
| Standing biomass | B(t) = sum of living cohort masses, kg | Abundance, sizes, sex, reproductive stage and survival |
| Fish oxygen | D_fish(t) = Σ B_i(t) q_i(t), kg O2/day for q_i in kg O2 per kg fish per day | Fed routine demand by temperature, activity and reproductive state, including peaks |
| Nitrification | 4.57 kg O2 per kg of ammonia nitrogen oxidized ([E10](eel_sources.json)) | Excreted ammonia nitrogen and the fraction oxidized within the boundary |
| Chilling | Heat removed from broodstock holding and incubation water, and the electricity to remove it | Volumes, temperatures held, insulation and the heat pump's performance |
| Nutrients | Feed and prey input minus harvested and exported nutrients, with changes in stock and sediment | N, P and mineral composition of every stream |

Edible output is reported per unit of feed and per unit of integrated standing biomass, beside peak oxygen demand, natural prey and manufactured feed kept apart. The present literature screen assigns no sturgeon tonnage, feed conversion or oxygen per kg of product.

## 7. Meat, caviar and a lunar cuisine

Sturgeon is a meat fish in its own right. A comparison of Siberian sturgeon and its reciprocal hybrid with green sturgeon found differences in tissue composition and colour, the backcross losing the parental hybrid's pink, with fillet yield given against gutted, deheaded carcass [ST15], so the actual cross and cut are measured and carcass percentages stay apart from live-weight yield.

| Product trial | Preparation | Traits to measure |
|---|---|---|
| Smaller freshwater sturgeon | Roasted or steamed portions; whole grilled fish where size permits | Firmness, juiciness, skin and scute removal, labour and cooked retention |
| Larger bester or Siberian fish | Steaks, skewers, hot-smoked fillets and poached portions | Fat distribution, connective tissue, flaking, smoke and oxidation in storage |
| Richer belly cuts | Plain grilled, then a glazed kabayaki-style preparation labelled as sturgeon | Rendered fat, glaze balance and succulence |
| Raw slices | Frozen and thawed slices after evaluation under the [culinary evaluation](culinary_evaluation.md) | Slice integrity, chew, fat melting, drip loss and flavour |
| Roe | Fresh-roe cooking and cured caviar | Egg maturity and size, membrane texture, salt uptake, aroma, oxidation and processing loss |
| Trimmings | Broth and cooked minced products | Actual recovery and flavour, kept out of edible-yield totals |

Caviar offers a credible route to distinction. Eight products from six species separated by texture and flavour, buttery, nutty and briny notes among them, with beluga caviar marked by gamma-glutamyl peptides and processing shaping the profiles [ST17]. Roe tracks diet less than muscle does: two dietary lipid treatments changed white sturgeon caviar's fatty acids only slightly [ST16], and microbes and phospholipids are linked with flavour in Kaluga–Amur caviar [ST18]. Family, maturation, diet and processing are compared under control.

The FAO account's 8–14% of female body mass is collected ovulated eggs, and only part of a broodstock cohort matures each year [ST01]; finished caviar awaits measured maturity, intervals, mortality, rejected eggs and processing recovery. Terminal harvest and repeated live roe collection are separate systems, the latter carrying its induction and handling in the welfare account. For terminal harvest, meat and roe are co-products of the same lifetime inputs and are reported with a stated allocation:

```
harvested live mass = recovered raw roe + prepared raw meat + all other material
cooked meat = prepared raw meat × measured cooking retention
finished caviar = recovered raw roe − processing losses + retained added salt
```

Lunar distinctiveness would come from a recognizable combination of ancestry, the lakes' benthic food web, cut, harvest timing and preparation: a highland-lake bester with its own diet, richer bester cuts, or roe from a defined lake line. Raw flesh and lightly cured roe follow product-specific hazard controls [ST19] and the [culinary evaluation](culinary_evaluation.md), whose freezing and cooking schedules address particular hazards.

## 8. Selection priorities beyond temperature

| Objective | Evaluation | Trade-off to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Warm incubation and maturation | Hatch, deformity, growth and later fertility at stepped incubation temperatures; maturation without a cold season | Fast juvenile growth from cohorts with heavy egg losses |
| Meat | Edible cuts per lifetime feed, growth, health and cooked retention | Faster growth with low edible recovery or visceral fat |
| Caviar | Age and size at first usable roe, intervals, quality, fertility and survival | Earlier roe with small or poor-textured eggs or shorter lives |
| Fit to the lakes | Performance at 21 °C, oxygen demand at the lakes' profiles, ionic tolerance | Survival with poor growth, chronic stress or failed reproduction |
| Culinary quality | Regional muscle fat, texture, taste, oxidation and storage | Better chemistry scores with no sensory gain |
| Ecological fit | Prey use, competition and movement across cohorts | Productivity tied to excessive prey demand or spread |

Families are split across environments to separate inheritance from acclimation, parental condition and tank effects; genomic prediction is validated on held-out families; and early biomarkers are checked against later fertility before replacing reproductive follow-up. Pure founder references are kept even where hybrids perform well, since a food line and a line for the lakes may become different populations.

## 9. Research sequence

1. **Assemble founders and sites.** Documented bester, sterlet and Siberian families from captive stocks, with ancestry, age, sex, diet, health and reproductive histories; pure beluga as a separate large-fish line; the coldest lakes' sites refreshed on the turned Moon.
2. **Build the chilled hatchery and measure the adults.** Condition broodstock and incubate eggs at 13–16 °C; measure growth, diet, fed oxygen demand and cooked products of adults held at 21 °C.
3. **Step the incubation temperature.** Raise it a step per generation in split families, ranking complete cohorts, and test maturation without a cold season.
4. **Place lake populations.** Stock bounded highland lakes, measure prey, benthos, competitors and harvest, and keep parentage records.
5. **Check the lunar-night idea.** Obtain the hydrosphere's cold-hour counts for shaded inflows at the coldest lakes and compare them with the lines' incubation times.

## 10. Open checks and ideas

Open checks: the coldest lakes on the turned Moon, after the GCM and CM1 agree; oxygen profiles of the deep highland lakes; respiration of adults at 21 °C; the conditioning and induction methods behind bester's 20–21 °C maturation [ST11]; the Red List status of kaluga, Amur, white and lake sturgeon; chilling energy for a sized hatchery. Ideas from the review of 6 November: the lunar night as the sturgeon's winter (section 3); captive lines of Critically Endangered beluga and Siberian sturgeon kept in the highland lakes as insurance populations. Idea from the branch's ideation session of 4–5 November: lake populations that recruit on their own, which no water on the Moon yet allows.
