# Sturgeon in Terluna: habitats, recruitment, meat and caviar

6 November 2026 · branch `domain/food-systems` · literature assessment and research design

Sturgeon merit a substantial place in the aquatic-food programme. Freshwater
founders already exist, cultured lineages provide meat and roe, and beluga offers
a large predatory fish with distinctive culinary possibilities. Their prospects
depend on the entire habitat and production system: nursery survival, adult
foraging, reproduction, prey supply, standing biomass, harvest and processing.
This dossier evaluates that system, with thermal adaptation as one component.

The preferred Gaean endpoint is a population that recruits within its ecosystem
without indispensable hatchery reproduction or replacement stocking. Farms can
produce food and preserve breeding lines alongside such populations. The initial
research priority is **sterlet and Siberian sturgeon as freshwater references,
with bester as the principal beluga-derived comparison**. Pure beluga deserves a
separate large-fish and caviar programme. This ranking is a design inference from
Earth evidence, subject to site and lineage tests.

Evidence labels used below distinguish **Earth observations/experiments**,
**inherited lunar models**, and **proposed trials or accounting identities**.
No lunar sturgeon, sustainable harvest, thermal breeding response or sensory
advantage has been demonstrated. Source access and limitations are recorded in
[sturgeon_sources.json](sturgeon_sources.json); verification is recorded in
[sturgeon_checks.json](sturgeon_checks.json). The [eel dossier](eel_habitat_hatchery.md)
provides the comparison with a presently saline early life cycle.

## 1. Species and hybrid candidates

| Candidate | Earth basis | Proposed lunar role | Main qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sterlet, *Acipenser ruthenus*** | Freshwater river species; feeds on benthic insect larvae and molluscs. FAO describes first reproduction around 3–5 years in males and 5–8 in females in some populations, later in Siberian populations [ST03]. | First reference for a smaller, naturally recruiting river fish; whole-fish and portioned cooked foods; smaller-scale roe trials. | Suitable flowing spawning habitat, a complete thermal history, sufficient benthic production and recruitment under harvest. Earlier maturity is relative to large sturgeons. |
| **Siberian sturgeon, *A. baerii*** | Freshwater species with established culture and river migrations. Temperate farm maturation can be substantially earlier than in its cold native waters [ST01]. | Main meat/caviar culture reference, followed by connected lake–river recruitment trials. | Warm-water growth, ovarian maturation and egg performance need separate evaluation. Long-distance freshwater movement can remain necessary. |
| **Beluga, *Huso huso*** | Anadromous fish that spawns in rivers; adults are strongly piscivorous. Historical Volga data place female maturity around 16–22 years [ST02]. | Large fillets, substantial roe harvests and a possible major predator in selected connected waters. | Long replacement time, prey demand, migration and reproductive habitat. Freshwater farming does not establish an autonomous freshwater population. |
| **Bester, beluga female × sterlet male** | Reproductive descendants and further generations exist in aquaculture [ST09–ST11]. | Principal beluga-derived meat/roe and breeding comparison; potential compromise between founder traits. | Establish the actual line's size, maturity, fertility, ploidy, diet and habitat behavior. Hybrid ancestry alone predicts none of these reliably. |
| **Sterlet-backcross derivatives, including Aksay bester** | A fourth-generation Aksay line, *A. ruthenus* × (*H. huso* × *A. ruthenus*), has been studied through embryos and juveniles [ST10]. | Compare a stronger sterlet contribution where reproductive tractability and modest adult size are priorities. | Performance can change across generations and environments; retain the exact cross and pedigree. |
| **Russian sturgeon, *A. gueldenstaedtii*, and Kaluga–Amur hybrids** | Established culture/caviar comparators; the Kaluga–Amur cross joins *H. dauricus* and *A. schrenckii*, distinct from beluga [ST04, ST18]. | Broaden the flesh and roe comparison if their founders outperform the initial panel. | Separate reproductive, ecological and thermal dossiers before ecological placement. Caviar success supplies no warm-water eligibility certificate. |

White sturgeon and lake sturgeon provide useful experimental comparisons for
reproduction and thermal variation [ST07, ST08]. Their results help frame tests;
they do not supply transferable thresholds for beluga or bester. Preserve pure
founder references even if hybrids perform well. A food-producing hybrid line
and an ecologically autonomous lineage may ultimately be different populations.

For the first trials, select documented families from several suitable captive
stocks rather than treating a species name as a genotype. Record ancestry,
relatedness, health history, broodstock size and environmental history. Obtaining
founders from established stocks should be assessed with the relevant conservation
and exchange requirements; this study makes no procurement or release decision.

## 2. From lunar biomes to connected habitat

The [environmental basis](aquatic_biomes.md) is inherited unchanged. Its older
sea-column forcing gives approximate basin means of 25–32.5 °C and a median sea
surface temperature of 29.6 °C. These values are a screening context, not the
latest GCM coupled to local fish habitats. Supplementary highland work suggests
some deep lakes around 21 °C and shallow ponds cooling toward 17 °C late at night,
with roughly 15 °C still dependent on unresolved cold hours. A brief cool minimum
does not establish months of broodstock conditioning or a viable nursery season.

| Habitat | Proposed sturgeon use | What must be resolved |
|---|---|---|
| Connected river, tributary and lake margins | Sterlet/Siberian foraging, migration, spawning and juvenile dispersal | Flow continuity, suitable substrate, egg oxygen, larval retention, seasonal cues and access between habitats |
| Productive freshwater delta or moderate silt margin | Benthic feeding and larger-fish foraging | Sediment oxygen, invertebrate replacement, food quality and access to separate spawning grounds |
| Carbonate coast and structured shallows | Feeding among invertebrate patches; adjacent open bottom for movement | Competition with shellfish harvest, substrate abrasion, sediment disturbance and water chemistry |
| Highland lake with tributaries | Candidate refuge and reproduction network | Temperature duration at every stage, adult access, nursery food and oxygen through the full lunar cycle |
| Warm open sea and deeper slopes | Conditional adult habitat for tested lineages; beluga only with a prey account | Joint temperature/oxygen profiles and migration options; depth alone supplies no cold refuge |
| Managed ponds, flow-through culture or recirculation | Initial production, family comparisons, optional hatcheries and finishing | Feed imports, oxygen and waste loads, cooling/pumping costs and dependency on husbandry |

The habitat network must include both feeding areas and reproductive areas.
FAO describes sterlet spawning on gravel in strong current, with benthic feeding
elsewhere [ST03]. A productive silty margin therefore does not automatically
supply a suitable egg bed. Candidate corridors need stable hard substrate,
oxygenated interstices, accessible spawning reaches, a downstream nursery and a
route by which surviving fish can return as adults. Floodplain feeding and
juvenile retention should be evaluated as parts of that network.

Oxygen remains a spatial constraint. The inherited deepest-tenth averages include
5.57 mg/L in central-case Procellarum, with an unsettled alternative at or below
1.54; Ingenii is 2.49 with an anoxic alternative, and Smythii is 0.55 even in the
central case. These are modeled decade averages, neither fish exposure minima
nor sturgeon limits. They justify withholding deep-habitat allocation until
resolved profiles exist. Oxygenated shorelines and tributaries remain separate
possibilities.

The maintained fresh seas are near pH 8.3 and 0.14 g/kg total salts, with an
unresolved magnesium floor. Trials need the actual ionic medium, including
calcium, magnesium and alkalinity. Salt concentration alone cannot characterize
larval suitability. The existing ammonia assessment also applies: evaluate
unionized ammonia using local pH and temperature, especially near fed enclosures
([eel sources E14](eel_sources.json)).

Low gravity creates additional questions about current-generated bed shear,
sediment settling, egg attachment, larval drift and hydrostatic exposure. It
does not establish greater edible yield or remove the energy cost of swimming.
Use the project's [shared constants](../../../shared/constants.json) and local
hydraulics when those calculations are developed. Likewise, the long light cycle
may alter benthic prey and feeding schedules; it supplies no evidence that a
sturgeon's reproductive season will become a monthly cycle.

## 3. Complete life cycles and juvenile supply

All candidates require a chain from viable gametes through feeding juveniles to
reproductively successful adults. Sturgeon already offer freshwater spawning,
and some founders live wholly in freshwater. This gives the programme a valuable
starting point. Nevertheless, successful farm reproduction often includes
broodstock conditioning, induced ovulation, artificial fertilization and managed
incubation [ST04]. Those successes establish tools for initial juvenile supply;
ecological independence requires additional evidence.

| Stage | Initial managed route | Autonomous-habitat question |
|---|---|---|
| Maturing adults | Track sex, ovarian development, condition and environmental history | Can fish obtain enough food, reach suitable habitat and complete maturation under the local cycle? |
| Spawning and fertilization | Pair known families and document any induction or intervention | Do courtship, release and fertilization occur without reproductive handling? |
| Eggs and hatch | Controlled incubation with known water quality and egg losses | Do substrate, flow, temperature and predators permit sufficient hatch in place? |
| First feeding and nursery | Known live/prepared feeds and measured survival | Is appropriate prey available when larvae become dependent on external food? |
| Juvenile grow-out | Document transfers, feed and mortality by cohort | Can recruits disperse, feed and survive habitat transitions? |
| Return to reproduction | Retain and test locally born descendants | Do their descendants also recruit, maintaining genetic diversity and replacement under the proposed harvest? |

Record the egg-to-juvenile survival product explicitly. If a grow-out cohort needs
`J` surviving juveniles and the observed combined hatch/nursery survival is `s`,
its initial fertilized-egg requirement is `J / s`. Count broodstock maintenance,
failed batches and live-feed production in that supply route. A high egg count
cannot substitute for dependable survival or adequate parent representation.

For a Gaean candidate, report the share of recruits born in the habitat, the
frequency of supplementation, movement among spawning and feeding areas, and
reproductive contribution by family. Following locally born fish through their
own successful reproduction provides a stronger test than a single hatch event.
Evaluate replacement with a stage-structured population model and uncertainty;
high egg survival alone leaves juvenile mortality, adult longevity and harvest
unresolved. Maintenance of the world's general climate and waters is part of the
shared premise; species-specific compulsory spawning intervention is the
dependency this endpoint seeks to remove.

## 4. Thermal evidence and the comparison with eel

| Evidence | What was observed | What follows for the lunar study |
|---|---|---|
| Historical beluga field account [ST02] | Summer occurrence in Caspian waters reported at 20–31 °C; early developmental conditions are cooler | Beluga is not confined to cold feeding water. Occurrence gives neither lifetime production limits nor proof of warm embryos. |
| Siberian sturgeon culture account [ST01] | Broad reported tolerance, with special measures near 25–26 °C; incubation example at 13–14 °C | Assess adult growth and reproduction separately; the upper tolerance range is unsuitable as a production optimum. |
| Sterlet thermal experiment [ST05] | Larvae were gradually warmed; the 27 °C group reached that treatment at 17 days after fertilization and remained through day 32, followed by a return toward 18 °C. Several growth/development traits improved. | Positive short-exposure evidence. Fertilization, continuous lifetime exposure and inherited adaptation were not tested. |
| Aksay bester experiment [ST10] | The authors identified 16 °C as the best incubation treatment; 18–20 °C produced faster subsequent growth with substantial early egg loss | Rank complete cohort performance, including hatch losses, rather than selecting the fastest surviving juveniles alone. |
| European sturgeon embryos [ST06] | In that experiment no embryos survived 30 °C; hatch failed at oxygen saturation at or below 50% | Oxygen and temperature interact at vulnerable stages. These values belong to *A. sturio*, not to every sturgeon. |
| White sturgeon maturation [ST07] | In a small experiment, all five females in a seasonal 10–15 °C treatment produced fertile eggs after induction; constant warmer treatments impaired reproductive performance | Thermal history can constrain ovaries before spawning. This does not establish a universal cold-conditioning threshold. |
| Bester oogenesis [ST11] | Captive hybrid forms showed 10–12-month interspawning intervals at reported 20–21 °C holding conditions | Encouraging reproductive plasticity, requiring appraisal of complete conditioning and spawning methods. It does not demonstrate an autonomous all-warm life cycle. |
| Lake sturgeon family experiment [ST08] | Families differed in responses to thermal incubation regimes | Family screening is justified. Responses combine genetic and other family effects; they do not establish selectable variation in the lunar target species. |

The comparison with freshwater eel reproduction has three distinct endpoints:

1. **Using existing sturgeon founders in a heterogeneous freshwater landscape**
   has stronger biological precedent. Existing freshwater reproduction may allow
   much of the work to concern habitat matching, recruitment and quantitative
   improvements. The eel programme must additionally overcome the early-life
   salinity dependence documented in its [dossier](eel_habitat_hatchery.md).
2. **Extending warm-water feeding and growth while retaining cooler reproductive
   habitat** is a plausible sturgeon development route. Its feasibility depends
   on a connected habitat actually providing the required exposures for long
   enough. Seasonal Earth culture does not establish that lunar geography does.
3. **Completing every stage continuously in 25–32.5 °C water without cool refuge**
   remains a much larger unknown. The reviewed evidence provides no defensible
   estimate of its difficulty relative to complete freshwater reproduction in
   eel, or of the generations required for either goal.

Thus the user's intuition supports a research priority under the first two
conditions. A broad claim that warm adaptation is inherently easier would exceed
the evidence. Sturgeon generation times also slow direct selection on successful
adult reproduction, even when juvenile growth responds quickly.

## 5. Ecological roles and effects

Smaller benthic-feeding sturgeon could turn locally renewed invertebrate production
into valuable food. Pure beluga would place a larger claim on fish prey. The
distinction matters in the project's nutrient-poor seas: predator biomass must
be supported by prey production after the prey population's own replacement and
other predators' consumption. Cultured beluga can eat formulated feeds [ST13,
ST14]; imported rations shift the resource account to their production systems.

The first ecological comparisons should measure:

- **Benthic effects:** consumption of insect larvae, worms and molluscs, changes
  in sediment disturbance, suspended particles and near-bottom oxygen. Whether
  disturbance improves habitat or damages spawning beds is a site hypothesis.
- **Competition and predation:** overlap with eel, shellfish, other benthic fish
  and human gathering; losses of juvenile fish to beluga. Maintain comparison
  reaches without the proposed sturgeon population.
- **Nutrient movement:** feed and prey inputs, retained biomass, excretion,
  sediment deposition and nutrients exported in meat or roe. A fish does not
  eliminate the nutrients released during digestion.
- **Population mixing:** movement and possible reproduction among founder and
  hybrid lines. Evaluate local benefits alongside loss of distinct reference
  populations or altered recruitment.
- **Health and exchange:** parasites, pathogens and food contaminants entering
  through animals, feed and connected waters. Terrestrial ancestry and selective
  breeding do not themselves establish compatibility after an escape.

These are proposed measurements, not predicted outcomes. Coupled accounts should
prevent the same mollusc or prey-fish production being assigned to direct human
harvest, eel consumption and sturgeon consumption simultaneously. Retain the
existing [phosphorus constraints](../../../resources/sea_phosphorus.md) and
[food-system accounts](README.md); no additional lunar harvest allocation is
adopted here.

## 6. Feed, standing biomass and oxygen budgets

A juvenile feed-conversion ratio answers a limited growth question. A fishery
producing caviar also supports years of immature females, mature resting females,
breeding males and replacement cohorts. Record those animals as standing biomass
even during periods with little saleable output. Mortality late in maturation
loses both a future producer and the resources already invested.

Meat production can use earlier harvests; it need not wait for a female's first
caviar crop. Compare a meat cohort, a cohort retaining selected females for roe,
and the population reserved for reproduction. This makes the opportunity cost of
delayed harvest visible while allowing the same regional food system to supply
both regular fish portions and less frequent caviar.

Earth feeding experiments demonstrate useful flexibility. In juvenile beluga,
soybean or canola oil replaced fish oil without a detected growth penalty during
a 120-day trial, while changing tissue fatty-acid composition [ST13]. A different
six-week experiment found increased muscle and liver lipid with higher dietary
oil [ST14]. Neither result supplies a complete reproductive diet or establishes
equivalent taste. Candidate lunar feeds can therefore compare existing protein
and oil sources, including local microbial or algal ingredients, with digestibility,
essential nutrients, edible tissue and fertility measured separately.

The oxygen experiment most directly relevant to beluga used approximately 281 g
and 1.22 kg fish at 18 ± 0.7 °C. Eight-week treatments at 2–3, 5–6 and 9–10 mg/L
showed impaired intake/growth under low oxygen [ST12]. The trial supports avoiding
growth budgets based on survival tolerance. Its conditions do not supply a
respiration coefficient for warm lunar adults, larvae or breeding females.

The following identities define the next measured account. They introduce no new
production coefficients:

| Account | Calculation and units | Required observations |
|---|---|---|
| Juvenile supply | Fertilized eggs = required grow-out entrants / combined hatch-and-nursery survival | Survival at each transfer and feed transition; intervention and broodstock costs |
| Feed | Integrate supplied dry feed over each cohort's lifetime; distinguish offered, recovered and consumed feed | Ration by size/temperature, losses, growth, maintenance, mortality and maturation |
| Natural food | Integrate prey consumption by prey group and cohort | Diet, prey energy, assimilation and renewable prey production; report alongside direct prey harvest |
| Standing biomass | `B(t) = sum of living cohort masses`, in kg | Abundance, size distributions, sex, reproductive stage and survival |
| Fish oxygen | `D_fish(t) = sum[B_i(t) × q_i(t)]`, in kg O2/day when `q_i` is kg O2/(kg fish·day) | Fed routine demand, activity, temperature and reproductive state; include peak conditions |
| Nitrification | Oxidation-only demand = `4.57 × TAN-N nitrified`, kg O2/day | Excreted ammonia-N and the fraction oxidized inside the accounting boundary; stoichiometry from [E10](eel_sources.json) |
| Whole-system oxygen | Fish + live-feed organisms + other animals + microbial/organic and sediment demand, separating nitrification to avoid counting it twice | Oxygen supply, local minima and duration, gas exchange and additional aeration |
| Flow-through support | `Q = D / (0.001 × ΔC)`, m³/day for demand `D` in kg/day and usable DO drop `ΔC` in mg/L | Inlet/outlet oxygen, mixing, intake availability and an acceptable outlet criterion |
| Nutrients | Feed/prey nutrient input minus harvested and exported nutrients, with changes in living stock and sediment recorded | N, P and mineral composition of every material stream |

Flow-through support is a transport balance. Recirculated water needs restored
oxygen, and a natural reach needs an actual supply from advection, reaeration or
production. Translate oxygen transfer into power only after transfer efficiency
and operating conditions are known. Include cooling, pumping, solids handling and
broodstock systems when comparing managed production with an autonomous fishery.

Report both edible output per unit feed and edible output per unit of integrated
standing biomass, alongside peak oxygen demand. Separate natural prey from
manufactured feed and retain their production costs. Complete-cohort budgets are
needed before claiming that a caviar line has better food efficiency than a
meat-only harvest. No sturgeon tonnage, universal FCR or kg O2/kg product is assigned
from the present literature screen.

## 7. Meat, caviar and a distinctive lunar cuisine

### Flesh quality and preparations

Sturgeon should be evaluated as a meat fish in its own right. A comparison of
Siberian sturgeon and green-sturgeon-derived hybrids found differences in tissue
composition and color; the backcross did not preserve the parental hybrid's pink
color, and fillet yield was reported against gutted, deheaded carcass mass
[ST15]. These results justify measuring the actual cross and cut. They provide
no basis for assuming that all derivatives combine their parents' preferred
traits or for converting a carcass percentage directly into live-fish yield.

| Product trial | Proposed preparation | Traits to measure |
|---|---|---|
| Smaller freshwater sturgeon | Roasted or steamed portions; grilled whole fish where size permits | Flesh firmness, juiciness, skin/scute removal, portion labor and cooked retention |
| Larger Siberian or beluga-derived fish | Steaks, skewers, hot-smoked fillets and gently poached portions | Fat distribution, connective tissue, flaking, smoke compatibility and oxidation during storage |
| Richer belly and other fatty cuts | Plain grilled comparison, followed by a glazed kabayaki-style preparation | Rendered-fat losses, glaze balance and whether the cut remains succulent; label it as sturgeon |
| Raw-flesh candidate | Standardized frozen/thawed slices only after product qualification | Slice integrity, chew, fat melting, drip loss, flavor and consumer preference |
| Roe | Separate fresh-roe cooking and cured-caviar trials | Egg maturity, size, membrane texture, salt uptake, aroma, oxidation and processing loss |
| Recoverable trimmings | Broth, cooked minced products or other demonstrated uses | Actual recovery, flavor and food suitability; keep uneaten material out of edible-yield totals |

These are culinary proposals. The first tasting should compare plain preparations
before adding smoke, glaze or strong seasonings. Measure muscle lipid by body
region, liver/visceral fat separately, collagen-related texture, free amino acids,
nucleotides and oxidation. Use blinded sensory panels to establish whether a
chemical difference is desirable. A high whole-body fat fraction cannot establish
rich fillets, and a larger fish need not produce a superior raw slice.

### Caviar quality and reproductive scheduling

Caviar has an especially credible route to distinction. A study of eight products
from six sturgeon species associated beluga caviar with gamma-glutamyl peptides
and differentiated products by texture and flavor, including buttery, nutty and
briny notes. Processing also affected the measured profiles [ST17]. This supports
testing a broad sensory space, while the mixed products and treatments limit
attribution to ancestry alone.

Roe does not necessarily track the feeding response of muscle. A 20-sample white
sturgeon experiment found only small effects of its two dietary lipid treatments
on caviar fatty acids [ST16]. Work on Kaluga–Amur hybrid caviar also links
phospholipids and microbial differences with flavor [ST18]. Accordingly, compare
family, maturation, diet and processing under controlled conditions. Neither a
feed change nor a microbial association is a recipe for a superior product.

Keep reproductive eggs and food roe distinct. The FAO Siberian account's 8–14%
of female body mass concerns collected ovulated eggs; it is not a validated
finished-caviar yield. The same account describes only a fraction of a broodstock
cohort maturing each year [ST01]. Before annualizing output, measure female
maturity, interharvest interval, mortality, reproductive reserve, rejected eggs
and processing recovery. Evaluate terminal harvest and repeated nonterminal roe
collection as separate systems. Any induced ovulation or handling belongs in the
latter system's intervention and welfare account; retaining a live female does
not establish autonomous reproduction or equivalent egg texture.

For an ecosystem fishery, harvesting mature females or their eggs reduces the
potential contribution to recruitment. Model both effects when setting harvest
and reserve fractions. A population with abundant large adults can still be
recruitment-limited, and a large one-time caviar harvest says little about its
replacement rate. This is especially consequential for long-lived beluga.

### Edible yield and joint products

Use weighed lots and compatible denominators:

```
harvested live mass = recovered raw roe + prepared raw meat + all other material
cooked meat = prepared raw meat × measured cooking retention
finished caviar = recovered raw roe − processing losses + retained added salt
```

The first balance partitions a harvested fish; a living female retained after roe
collection stays in the standing-biomass account. Record absorbed cooking oil,
glaze, salt and other additions separately so that recipe mass is not reported
as fish production. Carcass, skin-on fillet, skinless fillet and cooked-portion
yield are different measurements. Grade rejections and discarded batches count
as losses even if laboratory portions taste good.

For a terminal caviar harvest, meat and roe are coproducts of the same lifetime
inputs. Report both physical outputs and a transparent allocation method where
separate product costs are required. Assigning all feed to roe and then treating
meat as an independently costless production route would distort comparisons.
For repeated harvest, include maintenance between collections and the eventual
fate of the female. The present source set does not support a common live-to-cooked
percentage across sterlet, bester, Siberian sturgeon and beluga; pilot slaughter
and cooking records are an explicit next input.

### Where lunar distinctiveness could arise

The strongest candidate is a recognizable combination of **local ancestry, food
web, cut, harvest phase and preparation**: smaller river sturgeon with a particular
benthic diet; richer beluga-derived cuts; or roe from a defined regional lineage.
The long lunar cycle could influence prey and tissue reserves, but this remains
a hypothesis. Sample the same families across phases with matched diet and
handling controls. An effect that disappears under a common diet can still be a
useful regional food-web effect, without requiring novel lunar biochemistry.

Sturgeon raw flesh and lightly cured roe need separate qualification. Rapid
freezing must be part of a validated process where parasite control requires it;
freezing speed alone specifies neither an adequate treatment nor protection from
chemical contaminants or every microbial hazard. Cooking, smoking, curing and
refrigerated packaging also have product-specific controls [ST19]. These issues
belong in the existing [culinary qualification work](culinary_evaluation.md),
alongside sensory tests. They do not confine the entire sturgeon fishery to raw
products or to closed tanks.

## 8. Selection priorities beyond temperature

The programme follows the author's preference for Earth founders, lineage
evaluation and intensive selective breeding informed by genetic analysis.
Engineering remains available for a demonstrated limitation. Preserve reference
families and genetic diversity while measuring the following objectives together:

| Objective | Evaluation | Tradeoff to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Autonomous recruitment | Spontaneous spawning, viable offspring, nursery survival and return to reproduction | Excellent hatchery performance with poor foraging, movement or habitat spawning |
| Meat production | Edible cuts per lifetime feed input, growth, health and cooked retention | Faster body growth with low edible recovery or excessive visceral fat |
| Caviar production | Age and size at first usable roe, intervals, quality, fertility and survival | Earlier roe production with small or poor-textured eggs, reduced longevity or compromised replacement |
| Environmental fit | Full thermal history, oxygen demand, ionic tolerance and performance in fluctuating conditions | Acute survival accompanied by poor growth, chronic stress or reproductive failure |
| Culinary quality | Regional muscle fat, texture, taste, oxidation and storage performance | Improved chemistry scores with no sensory benefit or shorter useful storage |
| Ecological compatibility | Prey use, competition, movement and recruitment across cohorts | High culture productivity coupled to excessive prey demand or spread |

Use replicated families split across environments to separate inherited
differences from acclimation, parental condition and tank effects. Genomic
prediction needs such phenotype records and validation in held-out families.
Family differences in lake sturgeon provide a reason to investigate selection,
not a heritability estimate for lunar beluga [ST08]. Early biomarkers should be
validated against later fertility before replacing full reproductive follow-up.

Bester's fertility is useful, but hybrid quality needs examination across
generations. A study using second-generation bester found that egg maturation
state was associated with hatching performance and spontaneous triploidy [ST09].
Record ploidy, cross direction and family contribution rather than classifying
all hybrids as either sterile or uniformly reliable. Select derivatives for
their measured phenotype, with ecological autonomy assessed independently of
managed reproduction. Stable backcross lines may be valuable even if they lose
some of pure beluga's size or culinary attributes.

## 9. Research sequence and decision criteria

1. **Choose founder families and real candidate habitats.** Assemble sterlet,
   Siberian and documented bester families, with pure beluga as a separate large-
   fish track. Obtain ancestry, age/sex, diet, health and full reproductive
   histories. Refresh site environments against the pending geography/climate
   work and identify connected spawning–nursery–feeding networks.
2. **Measure growth, diet, oxygen and food quality together.** Compare actual
   chemistry and fluctuating exposures with source-stock conditions. Follow
   intake, mortality, fed oxygen demand, edible fat and plain cooked products;
   reserve qualified raw-product testing as an additional comparison. Build
   cohort accounts from these observations.
3. **Test complete reproduction.** Track maturation history, gamete quality,
   embryonic development, first feeding and viable descendants. Report every
   reproductive intervention. Maintain comparisons with cooler habitat where
   physically available, rather than interpreting juvenile heat tolerance as a
   whole-life result.
4. **Evaluate ecological recruitment and harvest.** Use bounded habitat trials
   to measure prey, benthos, competitors, dispersal and locally born descendants.
   Test whether replacement persists when hatchery supplementation stops and
   under a proposed harvest. Long-lived species require long follow-up.
5. **Select distinct food and ecological lines where results justify it.** Advance
   families on combined performance; retain pure references and an adequate
   breeding population. Assess meat, caviar and optional hatchery services as
   separate outputs within a common resource account.

This sequence can establish useful sturgeon foods before every ecological goal is
resolved. Sterlet and Siberian sturgeon provide the clearest freshwater starting
points; bester provides a documented route into beluga-derived lineages. Pure
beluga remains attractive for its food and ecological roles, with a larger
commitment to prey, habitat and delayed replacement. A functioning regional
sturgeon fishery would be a substantial outcome even if the hottest open waters
remain outside its range.
