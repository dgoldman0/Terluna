# Jade perch and eel in lunar food systems

4 November 2026 · Distilled follow-up to commit
`e7b0292843dfd38cf40d6e13399b38441e9f770d` on `domain/food-systems`.

Jade perch is the leading development candidate for distinctive raw fish; eel is
a promising cooked-food candidate for suitable freshwater waterways. These are
research priorities. The proposed tightly controlled raw-fish habitat is an
initial route to reliable qualification, **not a boundary around Terluna's usable
seas, lakes or fisheries**. Qualified wild catch remains part of the programme.

This note consolidates the discussion following the initial
[aquatic-food study](aquatic_novelty.md). It retains its
[environmental basis](aquatic_biomes.md), Earth founders and breeding-first
approach. No new biological trials, harvest capacities or safety outcomes are
claimed, and no numerical food-system assumptions change.

## Culinary priorities

The proposed **lunar butterperch** is a selected jade-perch lineage combining H1
(cyclic food supply and edible fat) with H3 (carbonate-associated food webs).
Diet-responsive muscle fat makes *Scortum barcoo* a useful experimental founder
[S1]. Its raw eating quality, reef use and response to lunar forcing remain to be
tested. The name describes a desired food, rather than an established taxon.

| Candidate | Development role | Desired outcome or comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Jade perch | First raw-quality development line | Pale, cohesive loin with clean sweetness and savory depth; a richer, silky belly; useful intermediate cuts |
| Tilapia under moderate alkaline conditions | Parallel H2 flavor-chemistry experiment | Determine whether water chemistry yields a desirable sensory change within healthy growth conditions |
| Barramundi | White-flesh comparator | Benchmark texture and product quality, retaining its separate saline hatchery requirement |
| Japanese eel | Dedicated cooked-food line | Actual unagi, including grilled and steamed-and-grilled preparations, with reliable juvenile supply |

For butterperch, loin would be evaluated as thin sashimi or nigiri, belly as
richer nigiri, and intermediate cuts as sashimi or lightly cured products after
the relevant raw qualification. Fine fat distribution, clean aroma, slice
integrity and a pleasant finish are selection objectives. Maximizing total fat
or omega-3 content alone would be an inadequate objective.

H1 could make richness vary with a repeatable harvest phase. That requires a
sequence from light to producers, prey and edible fish reserves; food-web lags
may shift or erase the effect. No peak phase has been identified. H3 could supply
regional diets that change flesh composition. Carbonate minerals do not by
themselves impart a buttery taste, and eating sweet shellfish does not establish
sweet flesh. The proposed novelty lies in the combination of lineage, food web,
harvest timing and cuisine; some successful products may also be reproducible on
Earth.

## Jade perch fat storage and processing

Jade perch has distinct abdominal reserves and edible-muscle lipids. Analyses
also identify substantial fatty acids in skin and head tissues; one comparison
found the highest omega-3 concentrations in the head and skin [S2]. These tissues
need separate measurements. Abdominal fat around the organs is not the same
product as belly muscle, and whole-fish composition cannot substitute for the
composition of a served cut.

Song et al.'s 60-day juvenile feeding trial found greater muscle lipid in the
higher-fat diet groups [S1]. Its roughly 24–28% muscle-lipid figures are on a
**dry-mass basis**. Multiplying each group's lipid fraction by its reported
dry-matter fraction gives approximately **7.2–9.8 g fat per 100 g fresh muscle**
(derived from group means). This is a trial-specific illustration, not an adult
fillet specification. Bulk lipid measurements also do not establish fine
marbling or distinguish fat between muscle bundles from droplets within cells.

The general teleost pathway is digestion of dietary lipids, intestinal uptake
and rebuilding, followed by storage in lipid droplets or transport in
lipoproteins to other tissues. Model-fish experiments directly demonstrate
intestinal digestion, rebuilding and export [S6]. Triglycerides provide energy
reserves; other lipids contribute to membranes. Stored fatty acids can be
released and oxidized for energy. This supplies a physiological framework for
jade perch, whose detailed tissue allocation and turnover still need measurement.

Its genome contains fatty-acid desaturase and elongase genes: machinery for
introducing double bonds and lengthening chains [S3]. Sequence identity alone
does not quantify conversion efficiency. Feeding evidence is more directly
useful: Van Hoestenberghe et al. reported production of long-chain omega-3 and
omega-6 fatty acids from dietary precursors, while showing strong dependence on
the feed supplied [S4].

- Linseed-oil-fed fish had the lowest fillet long-chain omega-3 content among
  treatments and modestly lower weight gain than the fish-oil group.
- A Schizochytrium–linseed mixture supported growth comparable to fish oil and
  efficient DHA deposition.
- Four weeks of that finishing diet shifted fillet composition toward the
  fish-oil comparison without lowering the reported sensory properties;
  continuous feeding for fourteen weeks lowered overall sensory scores.

Those sensory results do not establish raw-sushi performance. They show why diet
duration and composition must be tested together, rather than treating a higher
omega-3 measurement as a flavor improvement. A finishing diet can alter flesh
chemistry; it does not erase infections acquired earlier in life.

Fasting research reports changes in jade-perch energy metabolism and antioxidant
defenses [S5]. It does not establish which depot is depleted first or a useful
monthly cycle of belly richness. The lunar night should therefore be represented
by measured prey availability, light and oxygen, rather than imposed starvation.

## Raw preparation across the aquatic landscape

The author noted that the most tightly controlled proposal occupies only a
limited part of the total aquatic landscape. The research programme should
retain separate questions: which habitats support desirable fish, which catches
can meet a defined raw-product standard after processing, and which supply
cooked foods. A recommendation for an initial controlled trial does not exclude
other waters from later raw qualification or from food production generally.

| Proposed environment | Potential advantage | Qualification that remains |
|---|---|---|
| Clear, nutrient-poor freshwater coast | Low nutrient inputs reduce one driver of harmful blooms [S10] | Local productive margins, microbial exposure and toxins still require assessment; clarity is not a safety measurement |
| Carbonate habitat with invertebrate prey | Deliberate species choices may interrupt particular parasite cycles [S11] | Some prey or resident hosts can sustain transmission; community richness alone predicts neither safety nor danger |
| Moderate alkaline water or extreme soda lake | Chemistry might exclude specific parasites or hosts | No general raw-safety advantage is established; pH near 8.3 permits many pathogens, and cyanotoxins occur in Earth alkaline habitats [S9, S12] |
| Cooler highland water | May reduce growth of some warm-adapted microbes | The supplementary roughly 17–21°C conditions remain compatible with microbial growth; fish performance and the full thermal cycle matter [S9] |
| Controlled freshwater grow-out | Greatest control over feed, incoming water, introductions and waste | Maintain controls through the life cycle; some freshwater parasites penetrate fish through the skin [S7] |

These are hypotheses about relative control, not measured rankings of lunar
fishery safety. The long light cycle and low gravity currently provide no
demonstrated sanitation benefit. Phase-dependent blooms, prey exposure and
oxygen stress could instead determine when monitoring and harvest restrictions
are most useful.

Validated parasite-control freezing can support raw use of an otherwise suitable
catch. Rapid freezing must include the required temperature and holding time
for the product; speed alone does not establish parasite destruction [S7].
It does not reliably sterilize fish or remove accumulated natural toxins [S8].
Site management, hygienic harvest and handling, and the cold chain retain
separate roles.

Host selection offers a concrete ecological opportunity. Some freshwater flukes
require suitable snails before their larvae infect fish [S11]. A deliberately
assembled community could lack a necessary link even after a parasite arrives.
Continuing Earth–Terluna exchange makes this a monitored condition, not a claim
of permanent exclusion. Processed feeds can reduce exposure through infected
prey, but feed control alone does not address skin-penetrating parasites [S7].

For the first butterperch trials, controlled carbonate-water grow-out and feeds
representing local fatty-acid sources offer a practical comparison. Whether such
feeds reproduce the full flavor of a natural food web remains testable. In
parallel, retain open-water site assessments and the earlier objective of
Earth-comparable sushi safety for qualified wild catch. No area fraction or
fishery-wide exclusion follows from this discussion.

## Unagi in freshwater waterways

The author's proposal that eel could fit silty freshwater waterways has a useful
Earth basis. A Japanese-eel telemetry experiment observed both use of rock
refuges and burrowing into sand–mud sediment [S13]. This favors investigating
sheltered channels, vegetated margins, lake edges and managed ponds with a mosaic
of refuges and feeding areas. Eel habitat need not be confined to clear carbonate
shelves, and silt is only one part of the habitat case.

Juvenile Japanese eels were nocturnal in a temperature experiment and grew more
quickly at 20 and 25°C than at 15°C [S14]. This supports testing warm freshwater
grow-out with shelter during bright periods. It does not validate uninterrupted
feeding throughout a lunar night, or the warmest sea conditions. Sediment type,
oxygen at occupied depths, prey supply, water chemistry and chronic temperature
must be assessed together. Muddy habitat does not imply that anoxic sediment or
contaminated water is acceptable.

The larger mismatch for existing founders is **recruitment**. Terluna's fresh
seas do not supply the marine reproductive environment of Anguilla eels.
Japanese eel has completed
its life cycle in captivity, and a pedigree study found heritable variation in
larval metamorphic traits; its larval culture used seawater [S15]. The practical
initial route is therefore a saline reproductive hatchery supplying
juveniles for suitable freshwater grow-out. Managed stocking could extend that
grow-out beyond enclosed farms if ecological and resource assessments support
it. Hatchery recruitment supplies a baseline while breeding explores alternative
reproductive routes.

The author explicitly proposed breeding eels to use a different spawning
location and potentially remove the saline-hatchery requirement. Retain three
routes in the programme:

| Route | What must change or be supplied |
|---|---|
| Saline hatchery and freshwater grow-out | Supply existing maturation and early-life requirements in a controlled facility; establish reference families |
| Local spawning grounds | Select or condition spawning cues, migration and site use for suitable local waters, potentially including a managed brackish lake; provide viable egg and larval habitat |
| Complete freshwater reproduction | Select for successful fertilization, embryo development, feeding larvae, metamorphosis and fertile offspring under the target freshwater chemistry |

Changing spawning geography and changing early-life physiology are distinct
objectives. There is already some flexibility in larval conditions: one trial
found better growth and survival in half-strength seawater than full seawater,
while larvae in freshwater and 10% seawater died in the short comparison [S16].
An egg/embryo study found increased developmental abnormalities at some lower
salinities [S17]. These are stage-specific husbandry results. They do not yet
demonstrate an inherited response to selection or a freshwater life cycle.

A gradual reduction in salinity requirements could be useful before full
freshwater reproduction is achieved. Test family variation and reproductive
success across generations, retaining the founder reference and normal health
and development. The target is Terluna's actual ion mixture: diluted chloride
seawater and its calcium-bicarbonate fresh seas are chemically different media.

This fits the breeding-first programme: evaluate existing eel species and
lineages, improve hatchery survival and development, then select for growth,
health and cooked product quality. Compare the cost and output of the initial
hatchery with the prospects for locally reproducing lineages. Adult freshwater
fit alone cannot establish a self-sustaining wild population; that outcome needs
successful reproduction and recruitment in the chosen habitat.

Unagi also has a well-defined cooked culinary destination. The relevant targets
include skin and fillet integrity, tenderness after steaming or grilling, fat
retention, boning effort and reheating quality. Cooking changes the parasite and
bacterial control options while source controls remain necessary for toxins and
chemical contaminants. Actual eel retains the unagi identity; catfish or
jade-perch alternatives remain labelled by species and as kabayaki-style.

## Next comparisons

1. Compare jade-perch families and diets by served cut: fat distribution, fatty
   acids, texture, oxidation, freeze–thaw performance and blinded sensory quality.
   Separate inherited traits from feeding effects and phase-dependent responses.
2. Assess raw-product qualification across representative open and managed
   habitats. Record host communities, contamination pathways, bloom/toxin
   exposure and processing access without restricting the entire programme to
   the easiest initial trial setting.
3. Develop an eel habitat-and-hatchery dossier linking juvenile supply to
   freshwater grow-out, ecological effects, feed and oxygen budgets, and cooked
   yield. Compare the initial saline route with selection for local spawning and
   progressively lower early-life salinity requirements, including a possible
   complete freshwater life cycle.

   Developed in the [eel dossier](eel_habitat_hatchery.md), with reproducible
   scenario accounts. The author's subsequent clarification makes a complete,
   self-sustaining freshwater life cycle the **preferred Gaean endpoint**.
   Saline hatcheries serve the transition; completing the cycle in controlled
   freshwater tanks is an intermediate milestone toward autonomous recruitment.
