# Eels on Terluna: habitats, hatcheries and cooked yield

5 November 2026 · `domain/food-systems` · evidence synthesis and scenario accounts

**The preferred endpoint is an eel population that completes its freshwater
life cycle and recruits autonomously within the established lunar ecosystem.**
This follows the author's Gaean objective: the species should be able to renew
itself without indispensable captive spawning, larval rearing or repeated human
or automated stocking. Hatcheries may accelerate establishment, support breeding
and supplement fisheries; routine controlled reproduction should not remain a
condition of the species' persistence.

The initial route with the strongest evidence is a saline reproductive system
supplying freshwater grow-out. It is a transitional production and research
route. Local spawning and reduced early-life salinity are distinct development
goals toward the preferred endpoint. Complete freshwater reproduction remains
unproven: adult tolerance does not establish successful eggs, feeding larvae and
fertile descendants. Its priority is a design choice; feasibility must be tested.

Japanese eel (*Anguilla japonica*) is the reference founder because the evidence
below connects reproduction, larvae, juvenile husbandry and unagi preparation.
Other *Anguilla* founders can enter comparative trials, with their own life-stage
data and sourcing assessment. This dossier does not transfer Japanese-eel
performance to another species or presume unrestricted collection of wild seed.
Establish traceable captive founder families and retain reference lines.

The initial production network connects hatcheries, nurseries and freshwater
habitats. The intended mature arrangement includes self-renewing populations
and fisheries, with hatcheries serving optional supplementary roles. Silty
channels could contribute, alongside
rocky refuges, managed ponds and lake margins. The limiting questions are juvenile
supply, prey or feed, oxygen near the animals, nutrient export and recruitment.
The area of freshwater alone cannot answer them.

## 1. Habitats and the production network

The inherited water-chemistry proposal has approximately 0.14 g/kg dissolved salts
and pH 8.3, dominated by calcium and bicarbonate. The unresolved magnesium floor
matters especially for larval work. These waters are chemically different from
diluted seawater. Preserve actual ion concentrations, alkalinity and pH when
designing comparisons; a conductivity or salinity number alone is insufficient.

Earlier sea-oxygen forcing used basin mean temperatures around 25–32.5°C. These
are contextual values, not updated site assignments under the latest climate
and reorientation. Supplementary hydrosphere work includes cooler high lakes
and stronger pond cycling. The existing oxygen study already brackets basin
mixing: it does not establish oxygen at a particular eel refuge. Use that work
to select candidate regions, then measure or model the occupied habitat.

| Habitat | Proposed eel role | Conditions to establish before assigning production |
|---|---|---|
| Silty freshwater channels and connected backwaters | Extensive grow-out; sheltered feeding territories | Oxygen through the full light/temperature cycle, accessible prey, tolerable sediment oxygen demand, connectivity and harvest access |
| Gravel/rock margins with crevices | Refuges within rivers, ponds or lake shores | Interstitial flow, usable gaps, cleaning or natural renewal, stocking density and prey competition |
| Vegetated lake margins and freshwater sea shores | Dispersed fishery or managed grow-out | Littoral food-web production, predation effects, recruitment source and local oxygen minima; deep-basin averages do not set shore capacity |
| Managed ponds or partly enclosed channels | Habitat-rich grow-out with measured feed and recoverable stock | Refuge layout, escape control, solids management, aeration and a way to distinguish natural prey from supplied feed |
| Recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS) | Nursery, family comparisons, dependable juvenile supply and controlled finishing | Filtration, oxygen transfer, CO2 removal, ammonia/nitrite control, system reliability and separate quarantine units |
| Cooler upland water | Temperature comparator or refuge from excessive warmth | Actual growth, annual production timing and energy cost; cool-water occurrence alone does not establish superior economics |
| Persistently hypoxic deep water or extreme soda water | No initial production allocation | Direct life-stage evidence and a viable food/oxygen balance would be required to reconsider |

In an eight-eel pond experiment, Japanese eels used rock refuges and also burrowed
in sand–mud; filling the refuge gaps removed their use [E01]. A juvenile experiment
found nocturnal activity and better growth at 20/25°C than 15°C [E02]. These support
testing sheltered freshwater habitats. Neither establishes an advantage in
anoxic mud, tolerance of the warmest lunar basins, or a month-long feeding rhythm.
Provide daytime shelter under the long lunar illumination cycle and measure
feeding activity across the whole cycle; darkness does not automatically mean
no available food.

The initial network keeps reproductive water separate from grow-out water. A
central or regional hatchery supplies several nurseries; nurseries produce robust,
traceable juveniles for ponds, channels or qualified open-water fisheries. Parallel
broodstock and batch capacity reduce dependence on one successful spawning event.
Transfers require health assessment and gradual, stage-qualified acclimation.

## 2. Four reproductive routes

Here **local spawning** means reproduction at a nearby, repeatable site under
specified cues. It may still use saline water and assisted maturation. **Local
natural spawning** additionally requires successful maturation and spawning
without that assistance. **A freshwater life cycle** requires all stages,
including production of reproductively competent descendants, to succeed in a
defined freshwater composition. **Autonomous freshwater recruitment** further
requires the established habitat and food web to supply the cues, larval food,
transport and refuges needed for population replacement without routine hatchery
intervention. Completing a freshwater cycle in tanks is an intermediate milestone
toward that preferred endpoint.

| Route | What it supplies | Development requirement | Main resource/ecological consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| R0. Initial saline hatchery → freshwater grow-out | Captive-produced glass eels for freshwater nursery/grow-out | Transfer and stabilize established reproductive/larval methods; qualify acclimation and juvenile health | Salt, specialized larval feed and hatchery operation remain concentrated at a small part of the network |
| R1. Selection for a local spawning site | More dependable local broodstock reproduction, potentially in an artificial saline lake or tank | Separate maturation cues, spawning behavior, fertilization and viable offspring; distinguish assisted from unassisted success | Could reduce reproductive handling and migration dependence while retaining saline early stages |
| R2. Progressively lower early-life salinity | Stage-specific reductions in salt requirement | Determine each stage's performance curve, then test inherited improvement with intact reproductive competence | May reduce salt inventory and discharge; a small highly saline egg unit could coexist with larger brackish larval units |
| R3. Complete freshwater life cycle, followed by autonomous recruitment — preferred endpoint | Self-renewing freshwater populations; optional hatcheries for breeding and supplemental production | Fertilization, normal embryos, larval food and survival, metamorphosis, adult fertility and repeated population replacement in the established ecosystem | Removes obligatory saline stages and reproductive intervention if achieved; establishment and spread become part of the intended ecological design |

R1 and R2 can proceed in parallel. Changing spawning location does not require
waiting for a freshwater embryo, and lowering larval salinity does not prove
local natural spawning. R3 is the preferred destination, with no justified generation
count or probability of success assigned here. Conventional breeding may fail
to reach it if the required variation is absent or correlated costs are too high.
The author's sequence remains founder selection, lineage evaluation and intensive
selective breeding first; genetic engineering is a later option for a demonstrated
barrier. An autonomously recruiting local saline population could be a fallback
that also reduces dependence on controlled reproduction, provided its spawning
water, larval ecology and freshwater recruitment persist without batch-by-batch
intervention. It would still fall short of the preferred fully freshwater route.

### What the Earth evidence already permits

- **Captive completion is established.** Nomura et al. describe the earlier
  achievement of a captive life cycle and study genetic variation in size and
  timing at metamorphosis. Their rearing methods use seawater [E04]. This provides
  a breeding platform, without demonstrating freshwater reproduction.
- **Juvenile production has been reproduced across facilities.** A 2026 study
  reports 560, 2,269 and 1,347 juveniles at three recipient facilities using the
  transferred tank technique [E08]. Those are reported production counts, not
  annual capacities, guaranteed batch yields or evidence of lunar operation.
- **Some larval stages already benefit from lower salinity.** In a 2009 study,
  half-strength seawater produced 18.2% survival after two months versus 8.2% in
  full-strength seawater. In a separate short, unfed comparison, larvae in fresh
  water and 10% seawater all died within six days [E05]. The two-month percentages
  cannot be substituted for survival from egg to glass eel.
- **Eggs and early embryos have different constraints.** A 24–42 psu exposure
  study found developmental differences and suggested approximately 34–35 psu
  for its early-stage conditions [E06]. None of those treatments tested fresh
  water. Hatch success alone also missed later deformity outcomes.
- **Ions matter individually.** A 2026 unfed-larva study starting five days after
  hatch found best survival-activity indices around 13–16 psu and investigated
  individual elemental requirements [E07, with correction]. That short assay
  cannot establish a feeding-larva formulation or a complete life cycle.

These findings support a **saline egg/early-embryo stage followed by qualified
lower-salinity larval rearing** as an early comparison. They do not establish one
universal salinity schedule. Low-gravity effects on egg suspension, larvae,
feed-particle distribution, tank circulation and gas exchange require validation
before transferring Earth tank geometry or stocking density.

### Stage-by-stage gates

| Stage | Initial management | Evidence required to advance a lower-salinity or local-spawning line |
|---|---|---|
| Broodstock and maturation | Separate reference families and reproductive conditioning | Repeated fertile gametes; cue response and assistance recorded; ancestry and reproductive contribution retained |
| Fertilization and embryos | Qualified saline reproductive water | Fertilization, buoyancy/suspension, normal development and hatch; avoid selecting on hatch count alone |
| Yolk-sac larvae | Stage-specific water and handling | Normal jaw/organ development, survival and readiness to feed |
| Feeding leptocephali | Specialized larval feeding and controlled water | Ingestion, growth, sustained survival, deformities and microbial/water-quality load through the entire stage |
| Metamorphosis and glass eel | Measure completion and condition | Functional juveniles, timing distribution and successful freshwater transfer |
| Nursery and grow-out | Freshwater feed training, grading and habitat trials | Survival, feed conversion, health, tissue quality and absence of costly correlated responses |
| Reproductive descendants | Return retained siblings to broodstock trials | Fertile offspring in the same target water across repeated generations; quantify failure as well as successes |

Use split-family comparisons across salinity and ion treatments, with replicated
tanks and tank effects accounted for. Preserve an unselected reference line and
test selected descendants in a common environment. Estimate family-by-environment
effects before treating favorable family performance as heritable adaptation.
Genomic parentage and breeding-value prediction can preserve diversity and reveal
tradeoffs, but cannot supply missing phenotypes. The reported metamorphic-trait
heritability [E04] is not a freshwater-tolerance heritability estimate.

Compare progressively lower salinity only after a stage performs adequately at
the preceding condition. Track maturation, normal development, larval duration,
feed use, post-transfer survival and cooked quality together. Retaining fertile
descendants is essential; an early-survival gain that prevents later reproduction
does not advance R3.

## 3. Juvenile supply linked to harvest

For live harvest mass H, mean harvest individual mass m, grow-out survival s_g
and nursery survival s_n:

$$N_{harvest}=H/m,\qquad N_{glass}=N_{harvest}/(s_gs_n).$$

If s_l is normal-hatchling-to-glass survival, h is normal hatch per fertilized egg
and f is fertilization success:

$$N_{eggs}=N_{glass}/(s_lhf).$$

The reference assumes 250 g harvest fish, 10 g nursery exit, 0.2 g glass eels,
90% grow-out survival, 80% nursery survival, 1% normal-hatchling-to-glass survival,
60% normal hatch and 70% fertilization. **These are accounting inputs awaiting
measurement.** Expected counts are fractional internally; operational lots must
be rounded up and given a separately justified contingency.

| Stage | Expected count per 1,000 kg live harvest | Associated live biomass |
|---|---:|---:|
| Eggs before fertilization | 1,322,751 | Not calculated |
| Fertilized eggs | 925,926 | Not calculated |
| Normal hatchlings | 555,556 | Not calculated |
| Glass eels entering nursery | 5,556 | 1.11 kg |
| Juveniles entering grow-out | 4,444 | 44.44 kg |
| Harvested eels | 4,000 | 1,000 kg |

Holding later stages fixed, larva-to-glass survival of 0.1%, 1% and 5% requires
approximately **13.23 million, 1.32 million and 264,550 eggs**, respectively.
These scenarios expose the bottleneck; they are not a forecast of selection gain.
Changing this early survival changes hatchery requirements without changing the
post-glass feed account for the same delivered juveniles.

For a throughput illustration, assume 1,000 glass eels per successful batch,
80% successful batches, 270 occupied larval days plus 30 turnaround days. That
gives 974 expected glass eels per occupied batch position per 365.25-day year.
Supplying 1,000 kg live harvest annually needs 5.70 such positions, rounded to six
before redundancy. The batch output, cycle time and success probability have
**not** been demonstrated together. A position represents capacity to rear a
batch; it is not a specified tank size. Failed batches may consume feed, water
and time, so mean output alone cannot price juveniles.

Grow-out duration and the biomass trajectory are still missing. Annual harvest,
number of fish present and maximum simultaneous biomass must remain separate in
facility planning. Broodstock replacements and juveniles retained for breeding
are additional to the food-harvest counts above.

## 4. Feed and nutrient accounts

Larval and grow-out diets are separate development problems. A palatable juvenile
pellet does not solve leptocephalus feeding. Record larval feed dry matter,
ingestion, waste, water quality and survival per batch; compare locally producible
ingredients only after they support full development. Avoid defining a hatchery
around an assumed unlimited supply of a particular animal-derived ingredient.

For juveniles and grow-out fish, test digestible protein, amino-acid balance,
lipids, feed stability and acceptance alongside resource costs. A 2025 Japanese-eel
experiment compared paste and extruded commercial diets under recirculating and
flow-through conditions. Pellet treatments performed favorably, but diet
composition differed and paste intake was uncertain [E09]. It supports a
controlled comparison, rather than a universal pellet effect or a borrowed FCR.

Define **economic dry feed conversion ratio** as dry feed supplied divided by
net stage biomass gain. This boundary includes lost feed and the effects of
mortality. Do not add another mortality feed multiplier. FCR and survival must
eventually be measured together; keeping FCR fixed while changing mortality is
only an arithmetic sensitivity.

$$F_{nursery}=FCR_n(B_{nursery\ exit}-B_{glass}),$$
$$F_{growout}=FCR_g(H-B_{nursery\ exit}).$$

With nursery FCR 1.2 and grow-out FCR 1.5, the example needs **52 kg nursery feed
plus 1,433 kg grow-out feed**, or **1,485 kg dry feed per tonne harvested**.
Pre-glass feed is outside this total. Wet paste and dry pellets must be compared
on measured dry matter; their as-fed masses are not interchangeable.

An illustrative nutrient balance assumes dry feed containing 48% crude protein
and 1% phosphorus, wet whole fish containing 18% protein and 0.4% phosphorus, and
a protein-to-nitrogen factor of 6.25. Entry and exit fish use the same composition
for this screen; actual stage-specific samples must replace it.

| Element, kg per 1,000 kg live harvest | Feed input | Glass-eel input | Retained in harvested whole fish | Nonretained balance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Nitrogen | 114.07 | 0.03 | 28.80 | 85.31 |
| Phosphorus | 14.85 | 0.004 | 4.00 | 10.86 |

The residual comprises dissolved excretion, feces, uneaten feed and mortalities.
It is not all discharged water. Separate captured solids, recovered nutrients,
water exports and gaseous nitrogen losses when designing treatment. Nitrification
changes nitrogen form; it does not remove the nitrogen from the whole system.
Processing later divides the harvested nutrients between food, coproducts and
losses; harvested-whole-fish retention is not human intake.

In open habitats, replace the feed account with measured prey consumption and
prey renewal. Natural prey still has a nutrient and ecological cost. Count a fish
or crustacean eaten by an eel once, rather than also assigning it to direct human
harvest. Imported feed can increase eel production while exporting oxygen demand
and nutrients to a receiving habitat. The wider phosphorus budget remains a
constraint on both cultured feed and wild food webs.

## 5. Oxygen, water flow and operating load

Oxygen capacity follows **fish held and feed supplied on the demanding day**.
It cannot be inferred from tonnes harvested per year. A young-eel experiment at
23.4–25°C found poorer growth and feed performance below approximately 40% oxygen
saturation, with comparable performance from 60% to above 100% in its conditions
[E03]. These saturation percentages do not define a universal concentration
threshold. Resting oxygen consumption also varies with size [E15]; fed, active
fish and warm periods require their own measurements.

The standing-stock screen deliberately keeps three demands separate:

$$O_{fish}=Bq(24)/10^6,$$
$$O_{nitrification}=4.57\,F_d\,a\,r,$$
$$O_{other}=F_d\,b,\qquad O_{total}=O_{fish}+O_{nitrification}+O_{other}.$$

Here B is standing biomass in kg; q is fish respiration in mg O2/kg/h; F_d is dry
feed in kg/day; a is kg total-ammonia nitrogen (TAN-N) per kg feed; r is the
fraction nitrified; and b is other biological oxygen demand per kg feed. The
other term excludes fish and nitrifiers. The 4.57 oxygen coefficient and 7.14 kg
CaCO3-equivalent alkalinity per kg N nitrified are oxidation-only stoichiometric
values [E10]. The other coefficients below are assumptions. Basal nitrogen
excretion and delayed decomposition are not resolved by the feed-proportional
terms; they cannot predict demand during fasting or following a system upset.

| Quantity | Reference assumption or calculated requirement |
|---|---:|
| Fish held | 1,000 kg |
| Dry feed supplied | 10 kg/day |
| Fish respiration | 200 mg O2/kg/h |
| Fish oxygen demand | 4.80 kg O2/day |
| TAN-N generation, at 0.03 kg N/kg feed | 0.30 kg N/day |
| Nitrification oxygen, all that TAN nitrified | 1.37 kg O2/day |
| Other biological demand, at 0.20 kg O2/kg feed | 2.00 kg O2/day |
| Total demand | 8.17 kg O2/day |
| Capacity with assumed factor 1.5 | 12.26 kg O2/day, or 0.511 kg/h |
| Nitrification alkalinity consumption | 2.14 kg CaCO3-equivalent/day |

The sensitivity cases use respiration of 100/200/300 mg/kg/h, feed of 5/10/15
kg/day and other demand of 0.1/0.2/0.3 kg O2/kg feed at the same one-tonne stock.
Their capacity requirements are **5.38, 12.26 and 20.63 kg O2/day**. These are
paired examples, not a confidence interval or a demonstrated safe design envelope.
Do not apply this one-tonne-stock example as the daily budget of the one-tonne-
annual-harvest example.

For a whole production cycle, integrate fish biomass and respiration through
time and add feed-related and separately measured hatchery demands:

$$O_{cycle}=\int B(t)q(t)\,dt/10^6
 +\sum_t F_t[4.57a_tr_t+b_t]+O_{hatchery},$$

where time in the first term is hours and each feed term is the dry feed supplied
over its corresponding interval. This is the link needed to report oxygen per
kg cooked fish. No cycle total is assigned until duration, changing fish size,
temperature, feed schedule and hatchery demand are known.

As a hydraulic illustration, choose inlet DO 7 mg/L and an outlet floor of
5 mg/L. These are **design assumptions awaiting qualification**, including at
the actual temperature, pressure and water chemistry. Carrying the reference
capacity entirely in flowing water with that 2 mg/L drop requires about
**255 m³/h**. Recirculation can reoxygenate water, so this is not necessarily new
water withdrawal. The same demand still has to enter through a transfer device.

A connected 100 m³ water inventory contains only **0.20 kg O2 above that floor**:
about **23.5 minutes** at the assumed capacity demand with perfect mixing. This
is an inventory calculation, not a guaranteed outage survival time. Local dead
zones, interrupted circulation and uneven fish distribution can invalidate the
well-mixed approximation. Oxygen delivery and circulation need independent
backup appropriate to measured response times.

Lower gravity does not reduce the biochemical oxygen requirement by six. Test
bubble transfer, water circulation, solids settling and gas removal under the
actual pressure and gravity. Convert transferred oxygen to electrical energy
using measured kg O2/kWh, and add pumping, filtration and temperature control.
No defensible installed power follows from oxygen mass alone.

At the proposed pH near 8.3, compare TAN with its unionized ammonia fraction
under the actual temperature and ions. Higher pH and temperature increase that
fraction [E14]. Track nitrite, alkalinity, CO2 and sediment oxygen demand as well
as water-column DO. A refuge can be physically attractive while its porewater
or boundary layer is unsuitable.

## 6. What lower salinity saves—and what it does not yet establish

For a separate **100 m³ water inventory**, assume density 1,000 kg/m³ and loss
of 1 m³/day as liquid discharge, with no salt recovery. Approximate salt accounts
are:

| Salt concentration used for accounting | Salt in inventory | Makeup replacing liquid losses |
|---|---:|---:|
| 35 g/kg | 3,500 kg | 35 kg/day |
| 17.5 g/kg | 1,750 kg | 17.5 kg/day |
| 13 g/kg | 1,300 kg | 13 kg/day |
| 0.14 g/kg | 14 kg | 0.14 kg/day |

This water inventory is not the volume of a 1,000-juvenile batch tank. The
0.14 g/kg row is a **physical comparison only**; no egg, larval or reproductive
success is assigned to it. Practical-salinity units from experiments and mass
concentrations here are not interchangeable ion prescriptions. Density and
composition would be refined in a facility design.

Evaporation removes water while retaining salts. The table concerns liquid
losses, including purge and cleaning water. Recovery lowers makeup requirements
but adds treatment and energy costs. A stage-separated hatchery may capture much
of the benefit by retaining a small saline egg unit and operating later larvae
at qualified lower salinity, even without reaching R3.

Reduced salt use does not automatically improve overall efficiency. Slower
development, more deformities, poorer feeding or greater batch failure can
outweigh it. Compare salt, water, feed, oxygen, energy and labor **per healthy
glass eel delivered and per kg cooked fish**, including failed batches.

## 7. Ecological effects and recruitment

| Mechanism | Initial saline-dependent stock | Locally reproducing freshwater stock | Measurement needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Juvenile recruitment | Managed supply can be counted and adjusted | Recruitment may continue outside hatcheries | Parentage, juvenile abundance, reproductive replacement and spatial spread |
| Predation and competition | Released or escaped grow-out fish still affect prey and other consumers | Self-recruitment may sustain those effects after stocking ends | Diet, prey renewal, competing consumers and before/after habitat comparisons |
| Habitat use | Refuge demand, crowding and local sediment disturbance | Wider occupancy may become possible | Sediment condition, refuge occupancy, benthic communities and local oxygen |
| Nutrient loading | Supplied feed and waste depend on managed biomass | Wild food-web redistribution adds to farm-associated loads | Full N/P balances, captured solids, receiving-water response and harvest removal |
| Disease exchange | Movement of eggs, larvae or juveniles connects facilities and habitats | Sustained populations can maintain pathogen/parasite cycles | Founder health, intermediate hosts, batch histories and surveillance |
| Saline effluent | Localized hatchery discharge needs management | Could diminish with lower-salinity stages | Discharge ions, receiving-water dilution and recovery costs |

An eel need not breed locally to alter an ecosystem. Conversely, a tank-grown
freshwater life cycle does not establish a self-sustaining field population:
spawning cues, larval food, dispersal, survival and reproductive replacement all
have to align. Measure both possibilities explicitly. A self-recruiting population
is a separate ecological decision from producing juveniles in a freshwater tank.

For the Gaean endpoint, measure **locally born daughters reaching reproduction
per breeding female**, including mortality and the proposed harvest. Replacement
requires an expectation of at least one at the population density being tested;
a stable population tends toward replacement, rather than unlimited recruitment.
Keep parentage and immigration records so stocking or arrivals cannot masquerade
as local recruitment. The captive survival assumptions above cannot be assigned
to this field life table.

Demonstrate multiple cohorts with no assisted spawning, supplied larval diet or
supplementary stocking. Verify that the habitat's food web supplies the larval
and juvenile stages through the lunar cycle and other expected variability.
Evaluate recovery after a poor recruitment interval and retain enough breeding
stock under harvest. This is the test of independence from routine reproductive
control; a single successful freshwater hatch is far short of it.

Newly recruited American eels have been found carrying the swimbladder parasite
*Anguillicoloides crassus* [E11]. This illustrates a live-stock movement pathway;
it is an eel-health/ecological example, not a claim about a human foodborne
parasite. Founder screening and continuing surveillance remain relevant under
the project's assumption of recurring exchange with Earth.

Start habitat trials in recoverable managed units. Quantify predation and nutrient
effects before expanding stocking. Keeping Earth ancestry preserves biological
continuity, but selective breeding for freshwater reproduction could increase
establishment potential. Compare ecological effects of each selected line with
its founder; ancestry alone cannot settle that question.

## 8. Cooked products, yield and breeding value

The culinary target is **actual eel for cooked unagi**, with separate tests for
shirayaki, steamed-then-grilled kabayaki and direct grilling. The Japanese
agriculture ministry describes both the steamed Kanto sequence and grilling
without steaming in the Kansai sequence [E12]. This supports contrasting
preparations; it does not provide a live-to-cooked conversion factor.

Measure live weight, prepared skin-on deboned cut, cooked fish before sauce,
rendered fat, cooking losses and other coproducts separately. In the reference:

$$M_{cooked}=H\,y_{cut}\,y_{cook}=1{,}000(0.65)(0.80)=520\ \mathrm{kg}.$$

| Joint scenario | Grow-out dry FCR | Prepared cut/live | Cooked fish/prepared cut | Cooked fish per tonne live | Post-glass dry feed/kg cooked fish |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Efficient example | 1.2 | 75% | 85% | 637.5 kg | 1.88 kg |
| Reference example | 1.5 | 65% | 80% | 520 kg | 2.86 kg |
| Higher-resource example | 2.0 | 55% | 70% | 385 kg | 5.10 kg |

Nursery FCR remains 1.2 in all three. The paired assumptions illustrate possible
resource consequences; they are not measured correlations, confidence limits or
promised breeding outcomes. No reliable species-and-process-specific conversion
was established from the inspected sources, so these yields must be replaced
with weighed processing trials.

The reference tonne partitions into 650 kg prepared raw cut and 350 kg other
material; cooking yields 520 kg fish and 130 kg mass loss. That is 5,200 portions
of 100 g cooked fish, or approximately 130 g from each assumed 250 g eel.
Sauce, rice and other meal ingredients are additional. The 350 kg remainder is
not automatically edible or recoverable. Track suitable coproducts and their
processing costs before assigning nutrient, feed or economic credits.

Select for **served quality and usable yield** alongside growth and fertility:
skin tenderness, bone removal, texture, flesh thickness, lipid distribution,
desirable aroma, oxidation resistance and retained juiciness. Excess fat that
renders away may increase feed cost without increasing the valued serving.
Use matched age/size and handling controls, blinded tasting, and both sauced and
unsauced preparations so glaze does not hide the lineage or habitat effect.

Regional prey, husbandry and harvest timing could create distinctive lunar
unagi traditions. A direct low-gravity flavor advantage is unestablished. The
lunar-cycle fat-storage hypothesis should be tested across full cycles rather
than assumed from nocturnal behavior.

Cooked production broadens eligible culinary uses. Source-water toxins and
chemical contamination still require control; freezing and cooking address
particular hazards and cannot be treated as universal cleanup [E13]. Validate
the thermal process for the actual cut and product, then control handling after
cooking. This dossier does not qualify any lot for raw consumption.

## 9. Development decisions and evidence gates

1. **Establish the reference chain.** Acquire traceable founder families; reproduce
   saline hatchery success; deliver healthy glass eels to measured freshwater
   nursery and grow-out trials. Record all failed batches and the full input
   boundary. Demonstrate repeated supply before assigning a fishery capacity.
2. **Separate the reproductive improvements.** Compare local maturation/spawning
   cues independently from stage-specific salinity. Retain saline reference
   families and test lower-salinity larvae before proposing a freshwater embryo.
   Advance a line on functional, fertile descendants and overall resource use.
3. **Compare habitats with the same juveniles.** Allocate sibling groups among a
   controlled system, a managed silty channel and a structured pond/littoral
   habitat. Measure survival, feed or prey use, local oxygen, N/P outputs and
   recoverability across the lunar cycle. Do not extrapolate trial density across
   an entire basin.
4. **Weigh the plate.** Pair those groups with standardized culinary trials.
   Replace assumed cut and cooking yields, estimate feed and oxygen per served
   kg, and preserve fertility and health in the breeding objective.
5. **Reach the preferred autonomous freshwater endpoint.** First demonstrate
   repeated complete generations in the specified water, with assistance
   explicitly recorded. Then test whether an established, bounded habitat
   supports unassisted maturation, spawning, larval feeding, metamorphosis and
   reproductive replacement without supplementary stocking. Assess effects on
   the wider food web and recruitment under harvest. Use those results to assess
   expansion into connected habitats.

The immediate work can support cooked eel production while developing the
preferred self-renewing freshwater population. The saline hatchery supplies a
reference and a transition. Eliminating obligatory reproductive intervention is
a central success criterion for the mature Gaean arrangement.

## Reproduction of the scenario accounts

From the repository root:

```sh
python -m research.studies.food_systems.eel_budget
python -m pytest research/studies/food_systems/test_eel_budget.py -q
```

The generated product embeds the assumptions and hashes of its producer, inputs
and source register, records the shared year-length constant, and pins the
environmental manifest as context. Tests check arithmetic, conservation,
boundaries and reproducibility. They do not validate the biological assumptions.
