# Construction, external payments and monetary capacity

31 October 2026. **Monetary institutions can help a lunar economy use available productive capacity and survive financial interruptions. Their contribution to construction finance depends on the resources being purchased, the denomination and ownership of claims, and who accepts the associated risks.** The [institutional comparison](institutions.md) identifies alternative arrangements. This account connects those choices to the existing Earth–Open Moon–array comparison, including suppliers elsewhere in the Solar System. Its accounting identities are general; the development paths and institutional applications are conditional.

## An import programme and a domestic economy develop together

The existing [construction and product comparison](../world_economy/comparison.md) places the central initial lunar construction bill at **83.67 quadrillion U**, the array bill at **3.33 quadrillion U**, and imported lunar water and gases at **78.79 quadrillion U**. These are highly uncertain engineering-price scenarios. The combined initial bill equals about **96.66 years of the reference future Earth's gross product**, spread illustratively over five hundred years. Construction-period renewal and operations have additional funding requirements. The source industries, transport and capture architecture remain major unresolved physical dependencies.

These quantities explain why the monetary problem cannot be reduced to an early settlement's payment system. A bank financing local builders can create purchasing power that reaches idle machinery, available materials and contractors. A lender financing atmospheric imports instead depends on enormous source-side extraction, processing and transport operations. Some of those suppliers could accept lunar currency or claims on future lunar output; others could demand outside settlement money, equity or delivery in kind. The contract determines which financial obligation is created. Neither the lender's location nor the purchaser's currency determines where the real production occurs.

At early settlement, public commitments, sponsors and external investors may finance an external deficit while domestic production remains small. That deficit can be a purposeful investment phase rather than a sign of immediate failure. Its viability depends on what is built, subsequent income or transfers, and the distribution of risk. As lunar housing, food production, maintenance, manufacturing and services expand, a larger fraction of spending could mobilize domestic supply. Monetary autonomy could then help prevent a financial contraction from interrupting otherwise viable production. This is an economic benefit worth testing, even though it supplies no numerical discount on the inherited construction bill.

The same future technology applies to Earth, the array and the Moon. Advanced automation might reduce many costs and alter labor income, but it does not give lunar institutions a unique ability to create credit or evaluate projects. Distinctive physical opportunities, institutional reliability and counterparties' willingness to hold claims must carry the argument for a lunar advantage.

## Trace the current account and its financing separately

For a lunar territorial account, let **CA** be the current-account balance, **KA** capital transfers and transactions in non-produced nonfinancial assets, and **FA** net acquisition of external financial assets minus net incurrence of external liabilities. With complete consistent recording:

\[
CA = X-M+\text{net primary income}+\text{net current transfers},\qquad CA+KA=FA.
\]

New borrowing or equity investment therefore finances a deficit through the financial account. Interest and investment income enter the current account; repayment of principal is a financial transaction. A current grant and a capital grant belong in different accounts. Changes in the net international investment position (NIIP), the territory’s external financial assets less its external liabilities, additionally include exchange-rate and asset-price revaluations and other-volume changes:

\[
\Delta NIIP=CA+KA+\text{valuation changes}+\text{other-volume changes}.
\]

These conventions follow the ECB's BPM6-based [general principles of external accounts](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/pubbydate/2025/html/ecb.bopiipbook202507.en.html). Statistical discrepancies would need a separate entry in measured data. The reference examples below have a closed accounting boundary and no residual discrepancies.

The lunar economy could receive outside money in several economically different ways. Selling a programme to an Earth audience supplies an export. Selling a bond to an Earth investor creates a liability. Selling shares brings equity finance and transfers claims on future returns. Selling an existing financial asset supplies cash while reducing another claim. These transactions can all support an immediate import payment, but their effects on income, ownership and future obligations differ. A useful development account must also track gross funding: an adequate net current-account position can coexist with a large maturing debt that cannot be rolled over.

Exports likewise do not automatically accumulate at the central bank. A manufacturer or venue initially earns the receipts. It might retain outside deposits, pay its own suppliers or sell the currency through a bank. The monetary authority gains reserves only through an actual transaction or a specified legal arrangement. Tax receipts, private foreign assets and official reserves cannot be treated as interchangeable pools of public construction money.

The wider boundary matters. Transfers among Earth, Moon and array cancel when their accounts are consolidated, but a payment to a source economy elsewhere does not. The three-world comparison can describe mutual trade without claiming that these three territories contain every supplier, creditor or settlement issuer. A future pan-solar account needs the remaining economies explicitly represented.

## What the reference transactions establish

The [ledger](ledger.py) records each financial instrument as both its holder's asset and its issuer's liability. Each event also checks the resulting change in net worth, and the examples retain the identities of institutions and currencies. Amounts are arbitrary normalized units, with no conversion to the quadrillion-U construction estimates. L denotes a hypothetical lunar currency and S an externally issued settlement currency. U is a valuation unit, never an additional currency issuer.

In the external-liquidity example the lunar central bank starts with **25 S**, while an importer requests **28 S** of Earth equipment. Domestic lending and central-bank credit make L available, but even another **50 L** of bank reserves leaves the missing **3 S** unavailable. The model first completes an import of 25 S. A subsequent **6 S** service sale to the array gives a lunar bank external assets and its customer a deposit; those assets become official reserves only when the central bank purchases them. The remaining 3 S import can then settle, leaving the Moon with **3 S** of external settlement assets.

Across this sequence lunar goods imports total 28 S and service exports total 6 S. With no other current items or capital transfers, its **−22 S current account** exactly matches the reduction of net external financial assets from 25 S to 3 S. Earth records +28 and the array −6. The Earth equipment is an existing produced asset transferred to a lunar owner: it is an export/import, but the sale alone establishes no new Earth production. The accounts do not derive territorial GDP from these cash flows.

A separate comparison gives the same Earth investor a claim of **10 L** or **10 S** at initial parity. When L falls to **0.7 U**, the L debt's value to the creditor falls from 10 U to 7 U; the matching lunar external position improves by 3 U through valuation. No export has occurred. The S debt still has a value of 10 U, but its debtor now needs about **14.286 L** to obtain repayment currency. These counterfactuals hold real-asset valuations in U and nominal contract faces fixed while changing the value of L. Actual depreciation would also affect import prices, demand, asset valuations and subsequent lending terms, none of which is estimated here.

## Sovereign borrowing and central-bank purchases

Public borrowing must specify the issuer, repayment currency, creditor residence, maturity, indexation and guarantee. A lunar household can hold S debt; an Earth institution can hold L debt. Domestic and foreign ownership determine which claims cancel when lunar accounts are consolidated. Denomination determines a different exposure: how changes in exchange value affect the promised payment. Domestic claims still matter for distribution, taxation and bank solvency even when they cancel in the aggregate.

The fiscal reference starts with **120 L of private bank deposits**, 20 L of bank reserves and 20 L of Treasury bonds held by the central bank. The Treasury then issues 10 L and makes a 10 L transfer. A nonbank buyer pays from its deposit, temporarily reducing private deposits to 110 L; the transfer restores them to 120 L. A commercial-bank buyer uses reserves without first reducing a private deposit; the transfer takes deposits to 130 L. Neither result is a forecast of the spending response. The treatment of bank reserves and the Treasury’s account follows the [Federal Reserve’s liability-account explanation](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/bst_frliabilities.htm); no future lunar legal operating framework is inferred.

A subsequent central-bank purchase of that bond also depends on its seller. Buying from a nonbank adds the seller's deposit and its bank's reserves. Buying from a bank replaces the bank's bond with reserves without directly creating a customer's deposit. In the two complete reference paths, both finish with 130 L of deposits and 30 L of reserves. Treasury bonds total **30 L** in both: their ownership changes, while the fiscal transfer is the event increasing the private sector's net financial claims on the public sector. The model distinguishes these transactions rather than treating all public purchases as equivalent injections.

A central bank purchasing development debt can affect yields, maturity exposure and credit availability. Consolidating it with the Treasury reveals a replacement of one public liability with another, rather than cancellation of every public obligation. The [CBO’s analysis of quantitative easing](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/58457) describes the implications of this change in public liabilities. Interest paid to the central bank may return through remittances, but reserve remuneration, operating costs, losses and remittance rules determine the net fiscal result. The value of this intervention lies in its financing and stabilization effects; purchases do not constitute a second sale of construction output.

For an issuer of its own currency, the capacity to settle nominal domestic obligations changes default and liquidity risks. It does not fix a real interest rate for centuries or guarantee that additional spending will buy the same quantity of materials. If suppliers are fully occupied or imported inputs become scarce, easier financing may raise prices or weaken the currency before increasing real output. If facilities and inputs are available but spending has collapsed, it may instead help restore production. A useful monetary model must distinguish those cases.

## Debt burdens remain conditional on financing and ownership

The inherited [annual accounts](../world_economy/annual_accounts.md) already distinguish depreciation, income paid to nonresidents and principal payments. In the patient financing case, half the construction bill is borrowed, construction and repayment each span five hundred years, and the assumed real rate is 0.1%. With the specified creditor ownership, the lunar net-export requirement reaches about **8.22% of lunar GWP** when there is no new borrowing, asset sale, transfer or other compensating flow. The staged case requires about **9.91%** under its different terms. These are constrained cash-account outcomes, not market-clearing forecasts or monetary-policy recommendations.

A monetary architecture could change access to lenders, refinancing risk, denomination and willingness to hold long-duration claims. It could also support activity during shocks and thereby affect the income available for payments. Those effects would require a new behavioral analysis; the ledger neither adds them to world product nor declares the old debt burden solved. Continued external borrowing might smooth payments while increasing later obligations. Grants and agreed debt forgiveness shift losses to other parties. Depreciation can redistribute the real value of nominal claims, with consequences for prices, portfolios and future financing. Each mechanism needs its own counterparty and account.

The construction horizon also changes what a suitable liability would look like. A source industry delivering nitrogen now needs compensation before much of the finished world’s income exists. Long nominal L debt can transfer inflation and currency risk to its holders, who may demand compensation or refuse it. S debt avoids L exchange-rate risk for an S-based creditor, while S inflation still affects its real value and the Moon’s repayment requirement remains tied to outside currency. Indexing payments to imported resources preserves the corresponding purchasing-power claim while exposing the lunar budget to source prices. Short-term borrowing repeatedly rolled over leaves a centuries-long project vulnerable to funding interruptions even if its eventual benefits exceed its costs. Equity, capital grants, staged procurement commitments and long debt distribute that timing and risk differently; none is a free substitute for payment. The next financing comparison should identify which parties can bear each risk and how essential construction continues if they withdraw.

Patient capital could plausibly include institutions or residents who value the Moon's long development horizon, but their willingness to accept particular returns remains an assumption. Early sports, tourism and industry could make smaller projects more financeable and strengthen confidence through demonstrated activity. Their aggregate contribution to construction requires measured scale and retained cash after operating costs, renewal, taxes and existing claims. The venue model cannot establish that contribution by itself.

## Reserves, essential services and the price system

A reserve policy needs scenarios for lost export income, interrupted external funding, maturing debt and residents seeking outside assets. The IMF's [reserve-adequacy discussion](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2015/09/28/04/53/sopol040711b) supports examining several outflow channels rather than relying on import months alone. Its historical ratios are not lunar targets. A lunar calculation would additionally separate ordinary purchases from essential equipment and services, identify which payments can be delayed, and test correlated bank and public-sector needs.

Available official reserves must be distinguished from pledged backing assets, illiquid investments and private foreign holdings. The same asset cannot fund unrestricted construction expenditure and remain available for a conversion guarantee. Committed external lines can provide contingent liquidity if their currency, triggers and maturity match the need; a line expiring during the stress offers different protection from an unencumbered reserve. The cost of holding reserves is an investment and insurance question, not evidence that all reserves are wasted capacity.

Financial cover also needs an operational counterpart. Spare equipment or stored feedstocks can sustain work through a transport interruption; reserves can pay a functioning supplier through an income interruption. Array protection, power and computing services require continuity arrangements appropriate to the actual service. Cash, replacement hardware, delivery capability and authority to keep essential systems operating address different failures. A financial claim on an array operator does not itself provide a replacement shield.

Price measurement must reflect that economy. A lunar consumption index, a construction-cost index and an external import-price index answer different questions. Imported machine prices could jump while many domestic service prices remain stable. A monetary authority would need to assess subsequent substitution, income effects and persistent price-setting behavior rather than infer one general inflation rate from the engineering bill. The common real U used in the world-product comparison supplies none of these nominal indices.

The next quantitative step is therefore a sequence of dated sector accounts linking domestic capacity, imports, public revenue, banks, contracts and external positions. It needs evidence or explicit scenarios for import shares, invoice currencies, maturities, ownership, spending responses and joint shocks. The present study supplies the institutional alternatives and accounting controls for that work. It leaves policy rates, reserve targets, inflation paths and a preferred currency regime unselected.
