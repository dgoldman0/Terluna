# Inflation, deflation and currency pressure on U across nine portfolios

U remains the project's general economic unit, with the monetary transition considering either one currency or a basket of U-related currencies. Its service area here is the planned off-Earth economic zone through enclosed development, including its industrial, source, power and transport operations. Earth customers and funders are external counterparties. The Open Moon, planetary array and subsequent outer Solar System buildout are excluded.

**An export-rich U economy can experience local price increases and external appreciation pressure at the same time.** The modeled portfolios earn much of their income from worldwide services. Spending part of that income on domestic goods raises prices when supply responds slowly, while customers' and suppliers' desire to hold U can support its external value. Issuance that meets additional balance demand can accommodate adoption without that monetary contraction. Partial reserves support conversion and payment continuity, and their funding reduces the distributions available for spending.

This study estimates the size and type of those pressures under explicit policy experiments. It extends the [domestic price analysis](../complex_returns/prices.md) and [U trade transition](trade_transition.md), preserving their financial baselines. The estimates below are conditional calculations for research and institutional planning. They are neither observed U behavior nor calibrated annual inflation or exchange-rate forecasts.

## What the numerical comparison assumes

Each experiment follows a ten-year transition ending at elapsed year 30, 100, 300 or 800 of the existing industrial financial paths. These are separate dated experiments: a transition at year 100 starts at year 90, rather than repeating adoption within one monetary history. The runner reproduces all 27 financial paths and uses their annual receipts, distributions, investment orders and installed capacity. It produces 108 portfolio/market/date records, of which 73 have continuing funded paths. The 35 records whose financial paths have already failed receive no sustained price estimate.

The reference domestic basket combines the producer's resident consumption, imputed civic services and housing use with domestic capital formation, multiplied by installed scale. It excludes exports, imported capital and intermediate inputs already embodied in final uses. It is a broad domestic final-use index rather than a household consumer-price index. The inherited illustrative 30 USD2041 per accounting U valuation supplies source amounts, not an exchange rate; all monetary stocks below are expressed in equivalent source purchasing power. A common redenomination changes none of the indices.

| Input | Reference experiment | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| U settlement adoption | 25% to 85% over ten years | A scenario, distinct from how many prices are genuinely set in U |
| Customer transaction holdings | Seven days of U-settled annual receipts | Holdings are a stock; annual export turnover is a flow |
| Domestic transaction holdings | 90 days of planned annual domestic expenditure | Fixed-turnover transmission; 30-day customer holdings supply a separate sensitivity |
| Extra domestic spending | Rises from zero to 1% of distributions remaining after reserve contributions | All stakeholders' distributions are eligible; no assumption that all receipts belong to residents |
| Earned reserve funding | Lesser of 20% of positive available operating cash and actual distributions | The ten-year fund-building experiment preserves every original expansion order and operating buffer |
| Initial reserve capital | Additional funding equal to 5% of original seed | The original seed remains fully allocated; total initial funding becomes 1.05 times seed |
| Domestic supply | Elasticity one; zero or 5% additional supply by year ten | Sensitivity assumptions, without an engineering qualification of that response |
| Issuance for additional outside balances | 90%, 100% or 110% of the stock increase | Under-, full- and over-accommodation, respectively |
| External conversion quote | Held at its reference value in the controlled experiment | No USD peg or constant external quote is adopted as policy |

The 5% supply improvement is a **total ten-year shift**, not 5% annual productivity growth. The incremental spending shares and supply shift ramp linearly; actual spending also follows the changing payouts and orders. Reserve funding applies to actual positive payouts; it can fall below the 20% operating-cash target. Prices, supplier costs, financing and export demand remain at the inherited reference values in the financial counterfactual. Their joint response requires a subsequent integrated simulation. The extra supply is a productivity/capacity sensitivity: no separate retrofit bill or engineering proof of its availability is included.

## Price estimates by portfolio

The table uses the strong worldwide-demand case and the transition ending at year 100. Each percentage is the domestic price-level difference at the end of the ten years, relative to that date's unshocked domestic basket. It is **not an annual rate**. The issuance-shortfall and issuance-excess columns also include the 5% supply improvement.

| Portfolio | Reserve build, existing supply | With 5% extra supply | 10% issuance shortfall | 10% issuance excess | Additional outside U balances, billion source-value equivalent |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Raw-resource proxy | +4.40% | +1.88% | -5.60% | +8.85% | 11.25 |
| Commodity refined | +3.86% | +1.36% | -5.11% | +7.43% | 9.78 |
| Specialty metals | +4.06% | +1.55% | -5.02% | +7.72% | 9.96 |
| Premium lunar products | +4.99% | +2.46% | -4.79% | +9.23% | 7.84 |
| Propellant and logistics | +4.41% | +1.89% | -5.00% | +8.35% | 11.53 |
| Computing and digital | +4.46% | +1.94% | -5.39% | +8.78% | 11.35 |
| Worldwide services | +4.51% | +1.99% | -5.44% | +8.92% | 11.25 |
| Diversified | +4.29% | +1.78% | -4.93% | +8.06% | 10.34 |
| Earlier manufacturing mix | +3.52% | +1.02% | -4.33% | +6.10% | 7.90 |

With full monetary accommodation and the supply improvement, these cases end **1.02–2.46%** above their reference domestic prices. Their ten-year geometric equivalents are **0.10–0.24% per year**, assuming the reference price level itself is constant. Annual movements within the experiment vary with investment and payout timing, so those equivalents are not ten identical annual changes. Without the extra supply shift, prices end **3.52–4.99%** higher. If reserve funding has already been completed and payouts are no longer withheld, the supply-improvement cases instead end **1.92–3.67%** higher.

The additional outside transaction balances amount to **7.84–11.53 billion USD2041-equivalent**. These are U balance requirements valued using the source accounts, not new dollar income or nominal U amounts. A 10% issuance shortfall is only 0.78–1.15 billion in those same values, yet removes **10.3–14.2%** of the modeled domestic transaction stock. At the controlled reference quote and unchanged turnover, the resulting domestic price levels are **4.33–5.60% lower** than the original counterfactual. Over-accommodating by 10% instead produces **6.10–9.23% higher** prices. The symmetry of the monetary-stock shock does not make percentage price changes symmetric.

Increasing domestic spending to 5% of post-reserve distributions produces **13.73–19.97%** higher prices even with the 5% supply improvement and neutral issuance. This is a spending and supply problem. Restricting currency issuance enough to suppress it would also impede payments unless turnover, fiscal saving or real supply adjusted. Conversely, raising customer holding periods from seven to 30 days makes a 10% issuance shortfall produce **24.53–36.09%** lower domestic prices in the fixed-quote experiment. That large sensitivity makes measurement of actual holding behavior essential; it is not evidence that such deflation is likely.

## The monetary mechanism and its external interpretation

Let B_t be reference domestic final use, D_t actual distributions, A_t available operating cash and I_t expansion orders. A reserve contribution R_t = min(0.2 max(A_t,0), D_t) leaves D_t − R_t for recipients. With the chosen ramp f_t from zero to one, planned domestic nominal expenditure at the reference quote is

    N*_t = B_t + f_t s_D (D_t − R_t) + f_t l I_t.

Here s_D is the incremental local-spending share and l the assumed local share of expansion orders. The main table uses s_D = 0.01 and l = 0. The construction sensitivity uses l = 0.1; it assumes disbursement in the order year, so it is not a measured local procurement schedule. Ordinary consumption and maintenance already included in B_t are not added again.

With k = 90/365 years of holdings, the domestic transaction stock supporting the spending plan is L*_t = k N*_t. This counterfactual already accommodates domestic payment needs. Customer holdings are F_t = s_t X_t tau/365, where X_t is annual export receipts, s_t the U settlement share and tau the customer holding period. Additional outside holdings are

    H_t = F_t − F_0 − J_t,

where J_t is any reduction in outside dealers' existing inventories. The main experiment holds dealer target inventories unchanged, J_t = 0. The dealer-buffer sensitivity instead funds an opening inventory of twice opening customer holdings and transfers half the additional customer requirement from it. Every year must leave nonnegative dealer inventories. Customers buying existing U from an Earth dealer do not thereby drain domestic balances.

If issuance supplies nu H_t, the remaining domestic stock is L_t = L*_t + (nu − 1) H_t. Fixed turnover gives expenditure N_t = L_t/k. Domestic supply responds as Q_t = (1+a_t) P_t^epsilon, so market clearing gives

    P_t = [N_t / (B_t (1+a_t))]^(1/(1+epsilon)).
    pi_t = P_t/P_(t−1) − 1.

The model rejects nonpositive domestic balances and depleted dealer inventories instead of returning prices for an impossible transfer. Issuance meeting customer demand adds U claims against acquired external settlement assets; an excess purchase converts additional privately held external assets into domestic U balances. The adjustment of L*_t assumes domestic payment/credit accommodation, with corresponding issuer or bank claims. None of these transactions is earned export income, investor profit or additional productive capital. The funded reserve pool remains a distinct account from assets acquired against new monetary liabilities.

At nu = 1, additional foreign holdings are fully supplied and do not mechanically increase domestic expenditure. At nu < 1, the remaining customer acquisition is supplied by domestic holders in the controlled experiment. It is therefore a **required domestic balance transfer**, not an unfilled exchange order. If domestic holders and dealers will not sell that amount at the reference quote, external appreciation pressure replaces the assumed transfer; a floating market needs an asset-demand and dealer-capacity model to determine the new quote. At nu > 1, excess balances similarly create depreciation pressure if holders seek outside assets rather than the assumed domestic spending.

The numerical domestic-price columns and that floating interpretation are alternatives. Their exact price changes must not be asserted simultaneously. Research on currency pricing shows why a label change on invoices cannot settle transmission: prices that remain tied to outside purchasing power behave differently from prices held fixed in U. The [IMF's external-adjustment analysis](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/esr/2019/english/ch2.pdf) motivates this distinction. The [BIS analysis of intermediary constraints](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-942-fx-policy-when-financial-markets-are-imperfect) supports treating asset-market absorption as a separate mechanism. Neither source supplies a lunar elasticity.

## How the type of pressure differs

The price index captures demand pressure on domestic final uses, supply-led price declines and the specified monetary-stock transmission. Imported cost pressure is assessed separately. A further channel is **asset-price inflation**: international saving in U bonds, business equity or property can increase claim prices before much of the money reaches ordinary consumption. Collateral values and credit can subsequently transmit that effect to spending and rents. This calculation does not price those assets or estimate long-term portfolio demand for U. Supplier working balances and U used as collateral are also additional to the customer transaction balances quantified here; worldwide use could therefore create substantially more balance demand than the seven-day case.

| Portfolio | Main domestic channel | External and competition exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Raw-resource proxy | Shared service earnings spent on domestic goods; extraction and transport bottlenecks | Commodity price-taking limits the ability to keep a higher U price after appreciation; the raw route remains an engineering proxy |
| Commodity refined | Service-driven spending plus materials and equipment costs | Thin commodity margins and outside-priced inputs make adverse terms of trade consequential |
| Specialty metals | Domestic spending and scarce skilled production | Qualification and differentiated contracts may support margins, while substitution and buyer budgets limit pricing power |
| Premium lunar products | Relatively concentrated earnings and qualified production capacity | Genuine lunar advantages can sustain premium demand; failed qualification or displaced orders reverse that support |
| Propellant and logistics | Mission demand and constrained delivery capacity | Refueling locations, delivery windows and Earth launch substitutes govern pricing; receipts can be uneven |
| Computing and digital | Electricity, thermal rejection and service-worker demand | Competing Earth capacity constrains U prices; digital receipts can change faster than installed facilities |
| Worldwide services | Rights, research and professional-service earnings spent on a small domestic supply base | International audiences and institutions can support U holdings without local travel; churn, rights renewal and institutional budgets create reversal risk |
| Diversified | Shared workers, infrastructure and customer budgets spread several pressures | Sector diversity helps only to the extent that demand and inputs differ; common worldwide services retain correlated exposure |
| Earlier manufacturing mix | Materials income and manufacturing demands alongside services | Stronger industrial contribution depends on the inherited premium price assumption and qualified buyers |

At year 100, worldwide sports, entertainment, licensing, research and professional services supply roughly **91–100% of the strong cases' receipts**. Consequently, most portfolio differences concern the costs, timing and scale around that common earnings base. This does not establish that the industrial choices have identical mature prospects. It does establish that a monetary ranking which ignored non-material exports would misrepresent these particular scenarios.

Domestic scarcity and wage pressure can coexist with cheaper imported goods following appreciation. With genuine U pricing, appreciation can also make exports more expensive to external buyers. Commodity and computing competition may pass much of that adjustment back into producer margins; distinctive entertainment, research or qualified materials can behave differently. Contract and demand data are needed before assigning portfolio-specific exchange-rate elasticities.

A separate import-cost sensitivity gives the magnitude without assuming an exchange-rate forecast. A 10% rise in U's external value reduces outside-priced costs in U by 9.09%; a 10% decline raises them by 11.11%. If imported costs make up 10–30% of the domestic final-use basket and 25–75% of that cost change passes through, the corresponding price effects are **−0.23% to −2.05%**, or **+0.28% to +2.50%**, respectively. These illustrative shares are not inferred from total industrial imports, which also support exports. They are not added to the fixed-quote table. [BIS evidence on pass-through](https://www.bis.org/publications/working-paper-690-nonlinear-state-and-shock-dependence-exchange-rate-pass-through-prices) supports variation by economic conditions and shock, rather than a universal coefficient.

## Reserves, reversals and a U-related currency basket

An additional reserve contribution equal to 5% of the original seed requires approximately **128–139 billion USD2041** of extra initial funding across the nine portfolios. Calling it 5% of total new funding instead would require original seed divided by 0.95. During the strong year-100 windows, the earned contributions total **551–772 billion USD2041 over ten years**. They come from released distributions, preserving the original expansion orders and operating buffers, but reducing immediate investor returns. Existing net-present-value results therefore remain the original no-new-reserve counterfactual; the reserve assets and their ownership are not a new profit.

Those sums are large relative to the modeled customer transaction balances. A prudent target would depend on essential payments, short-term foreign liabilities, credible conversion facilities and simultaneous withdrawals, rather than collecting 20% of earnings indefinitely. The study tests a finite ten-year fund-building period and a separately completed-reserve case. Reserve accumulation does not permanently remove spending pressure after contributions stop. Assets previously allocated to operating buffers cannot also back a new guarantee without reconciliation.

The reversal experiment cuts export receipts by 25%, holds first-year costs fixed and shortens customer holdings from seven days to one. At year 100 in the strong cases, customers release **9.9–14.6 billion USD2041-equivalent of U balances**. All nine still cover the original orders by reducing distributions in this immediate stress. The reversal keeps the same counterfactual policy that adjusts domestic transaction balances to planned spending; it also tests retirement of returned outside balances. With full retirement of returned U, the lost domestic payout spending lowers prices **2.23–2.99% from their pre-shock levels**. Retiring only 90% leaves a monetary overhang that raises those prices **4.21–5.60%**, despite the income loss. With no retirement, the model produces much larger increases of **50–64%**. That last result assumes domestic holders finance the conversion with outside assets and keep the acquired U at unchanged turnover; it is a severe controlled stress, not a likely floating-market outcome.

For full retirement the issuer supplies external assets; any unretired transfer instead requires private external assets at the maintained quote. The initial reserve supplement alone exceeds this isolated customer release, if it remains liquid and uncommitted. The saved account also adds the selected window’s funded contributions; it assumes the initial supplement has survived unchanged and omits any earlier earned accumulation, returns, losses or withdrawals. That diagnostic does not reset the initial capital requirement at every horizon. Coverage of that one stock does not establish coverage of all bank deposits, debt, investor exits, import payments, collateral calls or reserve losses. In weaker financial cases the shock can exhaust distributions and leave an operating/order funding gap; those receive no funded reversal-price result. The model never silently rescues their failed capital paths.

The aggregate calculation applies directly to a single U. For a basket of U-related currencies, it assumes internal reference conversions and coordinated aggregate liquidity throughout each controlled experiment. If constituent exchange values move, a price denominated in constituent u_i must first be converted: its basket-U price is the local price multiplied by the basket-U value of one u_i. Domestic consumption weights then aggregate those converted prices. They are distinct from the quantities defining the currency basket. Aggregate balance sufficiency does not assure that each participating issuer can settle its own obligations, and common export exposure supplies no automatic diversification. Earth reserve currencies remain reserve assets and external payment instruments, not constituents defining U.

## Timescales and interpretation

The neutral-issuance, reserve-building, 5%-supply experiments give these end-of-window price-level ranges across the nine portfolios:

| Window ending in elapsed year | Middle-market domestic price change | Strong-market domestic price change |
|---|---:|---:|
| 30 | -2.41% to -1.45% | -2.41% to +3.80% |
| 100 | -2.41% to -1.69% | +1.02% to +2.46% |
| 300 | -2.41% to -1.68% | -0.22% to +0.79% |
| 800 | -2.41% to -1.68% | -1.71% to +0.20% |

These ranges compare different funded economic stages and each stage's own counterfactual. They do not describe repeated inflation accumulating over 800 years. The year-30 range is especially sensitive to lumpy investment commitments: an endpoint with no distribution also has no extra distribution-funded demand that year, even if payouts occurred earlier. The saved annual paths retain that timing; the earlier price study's decade-average inputs answer a different question.

Middle-market cases commonly show modest **supply-led deflation** under these assumptions because the chosen extra output exceeds incremental payout spending. Eight limited-market paths fail in years 13–18; the remaining propellant case fails in year 47. Failure and financing distress cannot be recast as a benign low-inflation equilibrium. No surviving path reaches the architectural population cap by year 800, so these results do not qualify a sustained economy at that cap. At a hypothetical stable scale, fixed adoption, holdings and balanced issuance generate no continuing inflation merely because annual exports remain large. Persistent inflation would require continuing expenditure growth relative to supply, changing turnover, monetary imbalance or imported cost shocks.

The supported policy inference is to **accommodate measured growth in U holdings while directing earned income and investment toward expanding constrained domestic supply**. Outside adoption can support U's external purchasing power, and the relevant monetary risk is issuing too little during growth as well as too much during reversal. Reserve funding can moderate expenditure and support conversion while the pool is built. The outstanding empirical work is to measure balance holding periods, domestic spending propensities, supplier currencies, constituent clearing obligations and qualified supply responses, then let currency prices and export demand feed back into the financial paths.
