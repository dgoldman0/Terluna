# How people live: the Moon's day, setting by setting

**9 October 2026.** The human paper, *Living on an Open Moon*, asks how people could organize homes, movement, work,
gathering, care and cultural life in a world with extended light cycles, distinctive built biomes and a usable vertical
dimension ([ensemble plan](../../../ensemble/planning/Open_Moon_Ensemble_Outline_plan-01.md), section 5). This study is the
fifth step of the order of work the author approved for the people and the sky. It follows each setting of the
[ways-of-living study](../ways_of_living/README.md), under that study's names, through the Moon's 708.7-hour cycle. It
computes the physical situation people would live in from the project's products, then lays out what that situation opens.
The outline's eight sections give its order.

Numbers marked *screen* come from [run.py](run.py) and its product [results/daily_life.json](results/daily_life.json);
*derived* marks arithmetic in the text on cited values. Every source is in [sources.json](sources.json) with what was read.
Each section gives the physical situation first and the human possibilities after it, under their own headings.

## Findings

1. **Every setting holds 29½ Earth days inside one lunar day, and its latitude and side decide how they divide.** At the
   summit metropolis the Sun is up for 354 hours, about 15 sleeps. Practical dusk follows for 82 hours (3.4 sleeps), then
   190 hours of night below dusk's level (7.9 sleeps), 118 of them below a tenth of a lux, and a dawn that mirrors the dusk
   (*screen*). The far side's low latitudes, out to the limbs, are the Moon's deep-dark belt: poleward of 30.4° the clear
   night never falls below 0.1 lux, and poleward of 48.5° it never falls to practical dusk's 2.98 lux. A town at 60°
   keeps 25 lux all night, one at 75° 485 lux (*screen*).
2. **The Earth holds the near side's nights at dusk's level.** Under a high Earth the night keeps 4.1–4.9 lux at its
   darkest from the equator to 45°, above practical dusk throughout (*screen*). By southern Mare Nubium, with the Earth 61°
   up, the darkest hour keeps 3.1 lux. Over Tranquility Base's harbour the Earth holds 145 of the 191 night hours above the
   level, and the rest stays above 2.4 lux. Toward the limb the Earth sinks and its light with it: 0.42 lux by Russell and
   0.06 lux at the Smythii headland (*screen*). The study's light matches main's corrected per-place Earthlight within 0.2%
   at all seven of its places.
3. **People keep a 24-hour day indoors; the outdoor cycle cannot set their clocks.** The human pacemaker runs at 24.18
   hours on average (Czeisler et al. 1999). Weak light entrains it to a 24.0-hour day and to neither 23.5 nor 24.6 hours
   (Wright et al. 2001), and timed light near 450 lux stretches that to 23.5–24.65 hours (Scheer et al. 2007). The lunar
   day is 29 times longer. Homes need dark bedrooms through the 354-hour day and daytime light through the night. Lighting
   the decent-living floor of 23.8 m² a person to the daytime recommendation of Brown et al. (2022) for 16 waking hours
   a day through one night takes 11–45 kWh a person, 2–7% of the night's energy at Europe's level (*screen*).
4. **Outdoors, the day's light lasts a day and a half past sunset.** After an equatorial sunset the open ground keeps 250
   lux, the daytime recommendation's level, for 36 hours and day vision for 47 hours. It falls below the evening's 10 lux
   after 68 hours and below the sleep environment's 1 lux after 94 hours (*screen*). At 30° each comes a sixth to a
   quarter later.
5. **The dusk is a shared time 82–137 hours long that moves at a cyclist's pace.** Sunset crosses the equator westward at
   15.4 km/h, with a practical-dusk band 1,260 km wide behind it; at 45° it moves at 10.9 km/h in a band 1,490 km wide
   (*screen*). No equatorial place lies more than 2,730 km, 5.2 hours by winged liner, from a dusk or a dawn (*screen*).
   The afternoon's storms tail off through the dusk's first day, and the evening's cloud thins away from the equator
   (*screen*).
6. **Height buys sun, and a sky town chooses its day by its height and latitude.** A park deck 3.1 km up sees 13.5
   more hours of sun each cycle at the equator, a sky town at 10 km 24 more (*screen*). Above about 8 km the air
   superrotates, so a town drifting with it lives a day shorter than the lunar day, the shorter the higher it floats and
   the nearer the poles: a median of 22 days at 10 km, 17 at 20 km and 9 at 40 km over the equator, and 17, 10 and 4 days
   at 60° (*screen*, from the zonal-wind product). Near the ground a drifting town lives a day of about 34 days with a
   long evening, 11.5 days of it from sunset to midnight. The wind itself holds a town at one hour only in the lowest
   5 km at 40–75°, from the late afternoon into the night, in 18–31% of 3-day means and up to 66% at the best hour
   (*screen*).
7. **The open-air heights breathe like Earth's mountain towns.** With 17.5% oxygen, the summit's ground holds the oxygen
   of 1,860 m on Earth. The top of the parks for everyone matches 2,330 m, the residents' parks 3,420 m and the high
   gardens 4,190 m; a sky town at 10 km breathes like 1,610 m and a lake town on Korolev's shore like 1,270 m (*screen*).
8. **At a sixth of Earth's gravity a fall is slow and gentle until it is long.** A 3 m fall lands like half a metre on
   Earth and a 10 m fall like 1.6 m, but a long fall ends at 17.9 m/s, like 16 m on Earth (*screen*). A round canopy lands
   a person at 4 m/s. Stopping a wheelchair or a bicycle from 5 m/s takes 12.8 m against 2.1 m on Earth (*screen*, with an
   assumed friction of 0.6).
9. **No human has grown up below Earth's gravity, and none has lived in partial gravity for more than three days.** The
   longest stay on the Moon is 75 hours (NSSDCA, Apollo 17). Adults in orbit lose 0.9–1.5% of their spine and hip bone
   a month (Lang et al. 2004) and recover it only in part (Sibonga et al. 2007; Gabel et al. 2022). Mice kept at 1/6 g
   on the ISS held their soleus muscle for a month (Hayashi et al. 2023), while mice bearing a fifth of their weight lost
   bone as fast as unloaded ones (Ellman et al. 2013). No mammal has been born in space (Proshchina et al. 2021).
10. **A room at Earth's gravity on the Moon is a 55 m wheel at 4 rpm, its floor banked 80.5°.** The same spin gives Earth's
    gravity at 55.9 m in orbit; at 2 rpm the radii are 220 and 224 m (*screen*). How much time in such a room keeps a body
    ready for Earth is unknown (Clément et al. 2015).
11. **Earth is a conversation with a 2.6-second echo; the array and the whole Moon talk in real time.** Light's round trip
    to Earth takes 2.4–2.7 s, twelve times conversation's mean gap of 0.21 s (Stivers et al. 2009). To the array it takes
    0.13 s and across the Moon 18 ms (*screen*). Earth is 2.1–5.0 days away by transfer, the array 6–14 hours (*screen*;
    provisioning).
12. **The resource operation's crews work beyond conversation's reach.** From the main belt light takes 14–31 minutes each
    way and a least-energy transfer 1.3 years; among the Centaurs 1.9–2.2 hours and 11 years; in the Kuiper belt
    5.4–5.7 hours and 46 years (*screen*, at representative distances).
13. **One air and one water hold everyone, and the winds gather drifting towns onto shared routes.** The Moon's
    2.84×10¹⁸ kg of air is 284,000 t for each of the shared case's 10 billion lunar residents. Its seas and lakes hold
    1.07 million m³ for each, and its rivers bring each 724 m³ a year (*screen*). Each terawatt used anywhere on the Moon
    puts 0.026 W/m² on all of it (provisioning). Towns that drift at one height gather onto shared tracks, near the ground
    within months and over the poles within years at 40–60 km (*screen*, from the zonal-wind product).

## 1. Many ordinary days within one lunar day

### The physical situation

**The cycle.** Noon to noon takes 708.73 hours, 29.53 Earth days, and the Sun moves 0.508° an hour (*screen*). With the
Sun in the Moon's equatorial plane, every place has 354.4 hours of sun. The Moon's equator tilts 1.54° to the ecliptic,
which gives the high latitudes a small year: at 60° the day runs 344–365 hours through it, at 75° 332–377 and at 85°
284–425 (*screen*).

| 15° | 85 h | 184 h | 105 h | 0.0057 lux | 4.1 lux | 184 |

The register gives 82 hours at the equator, 98 at 30° and 48.6° for the never-dark latitude. The calendar's transfer
product, read by its linear rule, puts the level with the Sun 41.5° down where the finer solution gave 41.4°; the
difference moves an equatorial dusk by a quarter of an hour and the never-dark latitude to 48.5°. The sea-appearance
study's dated month of 2038, with the libration and the real distances, gives its three high-Earth coasts 2.3–2.9 lux at
the darkest, where the mean geometry gives the near side 3.1–4.9 lux.

**The body's clock.** The human pacemaker's intrinsic period averages 24.18 hours, within about eight minutes either way
across young and old (Czeisler et al. 1999). In about 1.5 lux of waking light five of six people entrained to a 24.0-hour
day and none to 23.5 or 24.6 hours (Wright et al. 2001). About 450 lux timed to late or early waking hours entrained people
to the Mars sol of 24.65 hours and to 23.5 hours (Scheer et al. 2007). The 708.7-hour cycle lies far outside any of these.
Adults need 7 or more hours of sleep (Watson et al. 2015); school children 9–12 hours and teenagers 8–10 (Paruthi et al.
2016). People wintering at the poles show delayed rhythms and poorer sleep, which morning and evening bright light
restores (Arendt 2012, from small groups).

**Light against the clock's recommendations.** Brown et al. (2022) recommend at least 250 lux of melanopic light at the
eye by day, at most 10 lux in the three hours before bed and at most 1 lux for sleep. Outdoor horizontal light set beside
those levels (*screen*, a comparison of different quantities):

| Place | Night hours at or above 250 lux | Night hours below 10 lux | Night hours below 1 lux |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Summit | 73 | 217 | 165 |
| Ingenii coast, 34° S | 88 | 184 | 106 |
| Southern Mare Nubium | 80 | 194 | 0 |
| Polar plain, 75° N | 354 | 0 | 0 |

**Indoor light.** Lighting 23.8 m² to 250–500 lux with LED troffers at 126 lm/W (Pattison et al. 2022), for 16 waking
hours a day through one night, takes 11–45 kWh a person, against the 650 kWh of final energy a person at Europe's level
draws through a night (*screen*; provisioning). One added 40-minute outdoor class a school day cut first-graders' myopia
over three years from 39.5% to 30.4% (He et al. 2015); through the long night children's outdoor time falls in dusk light
or darker.

**Phases apart.** The places sit at different hours of the same cycle. Southern Mare Nubium runs 283 hours ahead of the
summit, the Procellarum coast 164 hours ahead and the Ingenii coast 76 hours behind; Korolev's shore keeps the summit's
hour within half an hour (*screen*). A winged liner crosses those differences in 3–8 hours.

### What it opens

A household on the Open Moon can keep an ordinary 24-hour day for as long as it likes, with dark bedrooms through the
long day and bright rooms through the long night. Inside that frame each place has its own month. On the far side's
equator two weeks of sun give way to three and a half days of dusk and eight days of deepening night, so the month itself
becomes a calendar of outdoor seasons. Under the Earth the night stays an evening lit at 3–5 lux that never fully
darkens. Poleward of 48° it is a long low twilight.

Many households could keep a local hour shared with their town, some the hour of relatives on Earth, and some could let
their waking day drift a little with the work they do. Farming follows the Sun: the biosphere's day fruit is set at
sunrise and ripe at sunset (biosphere canopy model), so harvest and its work fall in the dusk. Indoor work and study can
run at any hour. The decent-living basket takes an 18-hour paid week and a "good life" 46 hours (McElroy & O'Neill 2025),
which leaves most waking hours free in every phase of the month. Travel between phases can be part of ordinary life: a few
hours' flight takes a family from the far side's night to an earthlit evening by Nubium, or into a sunrise.

Children would spend the night half of every month in dusk light or darker outdoors. The myopia trial measured time
outdoors; whether bright indoor light serves children's eyes as well as outdoor daylight is open, for the biosphere
paper's human evidence and for study before settlement.

### The physical situation

**How the dusk falls** (*screen*): hours after sunset at which the Sun's sky on level ground falls to each level.

At the equator the light is 4,660 lux at sunset, 2,190 after 12 hours, 820 after a day, 75 after two days and 7 after
three (*screen*). Under a nearside Earth the night stays above practical dusk's level, so there the dusk ends as the
light passes from the sunset sky to the Earth.

**Weather through the dusk.** Over the cloud-resolving equatorial ring's land the afternoon's storms rain 0.39 mm/h
around ten hours after sunset, 0.17 around thirty, 0.09 around fifty and 0.03 around seventy; rain spells last 3 hours at
the median and 12 at the 90th percentile, and the land is wet 5.7% of the time (*screen*, from the sky-fleet product).
Cloud fills 28% of equatorial evening columns in the first 12 hours after sunset and 18% after three days; 15–30° from
the equator it fills 1–2%, 30–45° under 1%, and the highland box stays clear (*screen*, from the cloud-twilight study). In
the design run land air at dusk averages 21 °C at the equator and 20 °C at 45°, between the day's 22–24 °C and the
night's 19–20 °C, under cloud covering 41% of the sky at the equator, 28% at 30°, 17% at 45° and 8% at 75° (*screen*).
The climate's comfort over land stays the range between the GCM and CM1 that the register carries.

**A town that drifts near the ground lives a long evening.** A passive town at 2.5 km lives a day of 34 days on average
over ten years: 11.5 of them between sunset and midnight, 7.3 from midnight to sunrise, 5.3 in the morning and 9.7 in
the afternoon, where a fixed place spends 7.4 days in each quarter (*screen*, from the zonal-wind product's routes).

**Gathering and travel.** A dusk-long gathering spreads its crowd over the dusk. If a million people come during an
equatorial dusk and each stays four hours, 12,200 arrive an hour and 49,000 are there at once, a third to a half of one
sky-ferry line's 24,200–36,300 people an hour (*screen*, attendance and stay assumed). A dusk spans 3.4 sleeps at the
equator and 4.1 at 30°.

### What it opens

The dusk is long enough to be lived in. A gathering can run through it with people coming at their own waking hours,
quieter observances in other places, and the light fading over days toward the first stars. On the equatorial ring the
storms tail off in the dusk's first day and the later dusk is dry, so a waterfront or basin rim facing west needs shelter
for the first evening's showers and little after. Communities at different longitudes meet the dusk at different
times, and travelling with it, or to it, is a few hours' flight. Each gathering place needs sleep close by in dark, quiet
rooms, transit sized to a crowd spread through the dusk, and lighting that leaves the night's organisms their dark. On
the far side's equator that dark is the deepest on the Moon. A town that floats low lives an evening of eleven and a
half days and could carry a gathering through all of it. The forms these gatherings take, and their names, belong to
the people who make them.

### What it opens

The air is a second ground. People live on the land and on decks kilometres up, walk out on high parks, glide home in
the afternoon, and ride ferries between districts and sky boats to the high gardens. Height is also time: a deck keeps
the sun for hours after the street below has entered the dusk, and a sky town chooses its day by its height and
latitude. Near the ground it lives a month with a long evening; at 10 km three weeks with the sun lingering a little
past noon; at 15–20 km eleven to thirteen days on average with a long morning; at 40 km nine days over the equator and
four at 60°. Low over the middle and high latitudes
the wind itself can hold a town in the late afternoon and evening for days at a time. As the author put it, "a sky town
chooses its day by choosing its height and route… it's a complex map. And that's fine." A roaming town's shadow passes
over the land below in minutes, which leaves the ground under the sky's routes its light.

## 4. Living with different biomes

### A wetland-edge town on Korolev's shore

**The physical situation.** The town sits 84 km south of the summit on a lake of 140,700 km², up to 6.7 km deep, with
its water 7.8 km above the sea and 3.7 km below the summit (summit tower). Its air is 1.03 atm at a mean 16 °C with the
oxygen of 1,270 m on Earth (*screen*). Its light is the far side's equatorial cycle: 82 hours of dusk, 191 hours of night
and 119 hours below a tenth of a lux (*screen*). The summit's highland rains 3–6 m a year (metropolis brief). The air holds
no OH, so methane lives for centuries in it (joint synthesis; provisioning), and the town's wetlands and wastewater works
need designs that keep their methane out of the air. The metropolis passes about 44 kt of phosphorus a year through its
food and sewage (provisioning), and its wastewater is one of the Moon's main phosphorus return paths.

**What it opens.** A day there could start on boardwalks over the wetland, run through work in the wetland's care, in
treating the city's water and returning its nutrients to the farms, or in the city 35 minutes away by sky boat, and end
on the water as the dusk comes down over the lake. Food could come from the rain belt's farms and the lake, and gliders
from the port's rings reach the shore. The dark night here is among the deepest
on the Moon, so a town that keeps its light low gives the lake's night life, and its own people, the stars.

### A canopy-connected district at a forest edge

**The physical situation.** The megaforest's trees stand 100–500 m tall (megaforest wind study). Under the canopy model's
stand (leaf area index 5), 7.4% of the noon light reaches the floor, about 6,500 lux, and 3.8% of the low Sun's and the
twilight's diffuse light (*screen*, photosynthetic photons standing in for illuminance). So the floor reaches practical
dusk's level 48 hours after an equatorial sunset and leaves day vision after 11, against 82 and 47 hours in the open
(*screen*). Climbing 300 m through the layers is the work of 50 m on Earth (*derived*). A fall from 100 m ends at 14 m/s,
like 10 m on Earth (*screen*), so routes through the crowns carry nets, rails or canopies. A micro glider launched from a
300 m crown glides 4.5 km (*derived*).

**What it opens.** People could live at the forest's edge on their own supports beside the trees, with stations at the
layers and walkways through them, and leave the trees and their productive and detrital layers to the forest. Its work
could be the forest's care, its fruit harvest at sunset and the study of its layers. The floor's day is a green shade;
its dusk ends after two days, and its night is the forest's. Irrigation, waste and care for the forest run on the ground
and on lifts; arrival by air is a short glide from the crowns.

### An aerial neighbourhood or campus near 10 km

**The physical situation.** The inhabited-volume study's reference envelope, 500 m in radius at 10 km, carries 5,750
residents at 50 t each and 402,000 m² of floor at the metropolis brief's 70 m² a person (*screen*). Its air is 0.99 atm at
14 °C with the oxygen of 1,610 m on Earth (*screen*). The design run's 3-day winds at 10 km have a median of 2.6 m/s and a
99th percentile of 5.5; the cloud-resolving ring's 3-hourly winds reach 11.9 m/s (sky ships). Its people see 24 more
hours of sun each cycle than the ground below, and a canopy brings them down in 42 minutes (*screen*; sky fleet).

Drifting with the wind, the neighbourhood lives a day of 21.9 days over the equator (16.7–35.6 at the 10th and 90th
percentiles), 21.2 at 30° and 17.3 at 60° (*screen*). A passive route at 10 km averages 21.3 days a day over ten years,
spends 4.9–5.9 days in each quarter of it and lingers a little past noon; the Sun is up 54% of its time, about 11.4 days
a day (*derived*), and it gathers 1.09 times the light of fixed places at its latitudes. Its routes spend 46% of years
5–10 within 20° of the equator and 11% poleward of 70°, and keep 32–52 km from their nearest neighbour after one to ten
years (*screen*, from the zonal-wind product). The wind at 10 km holds the hour in under 1% of 3-day means within 40° of
the equator, so keeping one place in the day takes power: 2.7–10 kW a resident for the unstreamlined sphere, and 6.9 kW
held against a steady 5 m/s (inhabited volume).

**What it opens.** A resident of a neighbourhood drifting at 10 km over the equator lives about 21 Earth days to the
town's day: some eleven and a half days of sun lingering a little past noon, an evening and first half of the night of
five days, and five days from midnight to dawn. The light outside changes about a third faster than on the ground, while
the home keeps its 24-hour day. Over the years the neighbourhood drifts between the equator and the high latitudes,
meets other towns on shared currents, and comes down to the ground for errands by sky boat. Its work could be research
that needs the height, such as the atmosphere's science or astronomy, or any work done at a desk. Its people need the
ground's services within reach: care, schools and food, carried up by boats and ferries.

## 5. Homes and mobility across bodies and generations

### What the evidence covers

- **Time on the Moon.** The longest human stay was 75 hours, with 22 hours outside (NSSDCA, Apollo 17). There are no
  human data for living in partial gravity beyond that.
- **Bone and muscle in microgravity.** Crews on 4–6-month flights lose 0.9% of their spine's and 1.4–1.5% of their hip's
  bone mineral a month (Lang et al. 2004). Half the loss returns within nine months at every site (Sibonga et al. 2007),
  and a year after return the tibia's strength and density are still 0.9–2.1% down, with 9 of 17 astronauts short of full
  recovery (Gabel et al. 2022).
- **Partial gravity in mammals.** Mice kept at 1/6 g for about a month on the ISS held their soleus muscle's mass and
  fibre size, which microgravity cut by 17% and about 40%, but not its fibre type (Hayashi et al. 2023). Mice bearing 20%,
  40% or 70% of their weight for three weeks lost bone and muscle in proportion to the unloading, and at 20% lost bone as
  fast as without load (Ellman et al. 2013). Lunar gravity is 16.6% of Earth's.
- **Development.** No mammal has been born in space, and no vertebrate has completed its life cycle there (Proshchina et
  al. 2021; Ronca 2003). Rat pups flown from their 14th day for 16 days kept a changed way of righting themselves through
  the four months they were followed; after 9 days the change passed (Walton et al. 2005). On Earth, loading during growth
  builds bone: women racket players who started at or before menarche gained two to four times the playing-arm bone of
  those who started more than 15 years after it (Kannus et al. 1995).
- **Moving.** People switch from walking to running at 1.98 m/s in Earth's gravity (Kram et al. 1997) and at 1.42 m/s in
  lunar gravity in parabolic flight (De Witt et al. 2014). Below 0.4 g the Earth rule of a constant Froude number fails.
- **Artificial gravity.** The level, duration and frequency needed to keep a body healthy are unknown (Clément et al.
  2015). Thirty minutes a day at 1 g during 60 days of bed rest lessened some losses of muscle function and none of aerobic
  capacity (Kramer et al. 2021). Residents tolerate at least 4 rpm (Globus & Hall 2017, as the provisioning population
  study cites).
- **Returning to Earth.** After 9–14-day flights 9 of 14 crew could not complete a 10-minute stand test (Buckey et al.
  1996); about 20% of astronauts show orthostatic intolerance after short flights and 5 of 6 did after 129–190-day flights
  (Meck et al. 2001).

### The physical situation

Lunar gravity is 1.624 m/s², 0.166 of Earth's (*screen*). A fall from a 1 m bunk takes 1.1 s and lands at 1.8 m/s, like
16 cm on Earth; from 3 m it lands like 0.5 m and from 10 m like 1.6 m; from 30 m like 4.3 m (*screen*, with drag). A long
fall ends at 17.9 m/s (sky fleet). A jump with the same push goes six times as high (*derived*). Braking on a dry path
holds at most 0.97 m/s², so a wheelchair or bicycle needs 12.8 m to stop from 5 m/s and every turn is six times as wide
(*screen*, friction 0.6). A surface room at Earth's gravity spins on 881 m at 1 rpm, 220 m at 2 rpm and 55 m at 4 rpm,
with its floor banked 80.5° toward the axis; in orbit the radii are 894, 224 and 56 m (*screen*).

### What it opens

Daily life at a sixth of the weight makes carrying, lifting and climbing easy for most people and falls far gentler,
while long falls and fast stops stay dangerous. Access then rests on design: lifts and ramps everywhere, seated transit
with gentle acceleration, wide turning paths, rails and soft ground where children play, and canopies at every rim.
Recreational flight is one way to move among several, and a ferry, a lift and a level path reach every home. People with
limited strength may find a sixth of the weight freeing, and people who move on wheels need the long stopping distances
and gentle slopes built in; how disabled people fare in partial gravity has not been studied.

Children growing up at a sixth of Earth's gravity are the largest open question. The evidence says bone and muscle answer
to load, that loading during growth builds bone for life, and that a month at 1/6 g keeps some muscle in mice. It says
nothing yet about a whole childhood. The possibilities range from play and sport that load the skeleton hard, through
daily hours in spinning rooms, to childhoods spent partly on Earth or in the array's wheels at Earth's gravity. Which of
these families choose, and what they cost a child, waits on research the outline lists for before settlement.

Older residents may find the Moon kinder: falls are slower and land softer, and loads are light, while bone kept under
a sixth of the load may thin as it does in orbit. Visitors from Earth arrive with Earth's bones; crews in orbit lose about
1% of hip and spine bone a month, and the rate at a sixth of the gravity is unmeasured. Anyone returning to Earth after
years, and above all anyone born on the Moon, faces a weight six times their own and the reconditioning that crews need
after long flights. A wheel at Earth's gravity, at home or in the array, is where that preparation could happen.

## 6. Work and affluence

### The physical situation

- **Energy.** Affluence is 2–6 kW of primary energy a person, centred on Europe's 3.7 (provisioning). A person draws
  353–1,511 kWh of final energy through each night, 650 at Europe's level, and daylight-level home lighting is 2–7% of it
  (*screen*).
- **Computing.** The array's computing answers in 0.13 s, inside conversation's gap, and Earth's in 2.56 s (provisioning).
- **The array.** It is 14.1 hours away on a least-energy transfer, 7.3 on a faster ellipse and 5.9 on a parabola. Weekly
  commuting by rocket takes 18–46 kW a person, a rotation each lunar cycle 4.2–11 kW and every other cycle 2.1–5.4 kW
  (provisioning). Its residents live behind 990 t of shield each, in wheels that give Earth's gravity at 56 m and 4 rpm
  and the Moon's at 9.3 m (provisioning).
- **The resource operation's crews** work 14 minutes to 5.7 hours of light from the Moon and 1.3–46 years of
  least-energy travel (*screen*, at 2.7, 15 and 40 AU for the main belt, the Centaurs and the Kuiper belt).
- **Travel time.** People spend about 1.1 hours a day travelling (Schafer & Victor 2000, as the provisioning population
  study cites), and the array is half a day away.

### What it opens

Work on the Moon can be short in hours and well equipped: the economy is rich in computing and energy, and a decent life
takes an 18-hour paid week. Work that follows the Sun (farming, building outdoors, guiding) fits the month's shape; work
indoors fits any hour. The array's workers could live in rotations, a lunar cycle on the Moon and one aloft, and the
tinkerers who work at its industry most of the time live there, with families who may divide their time between the
wheels and the Moon. The resource operation's crews, light-minutes to light-hours away and years from home by least
energy, could live at their sources as communities of their own.

## 7. Communities that share one air and one water

### The physical situation

The Moon's air, 2.84×10¹⁸ kg, is one body that every community breathes, 284,000 t a person among 10 billion lunar
residents; its seas and lakes hold 1.07 million m³ a person and its rivers bring 724 m³ a person a year (*screen*). The air
holds no OH, so what any community releases stays for centuries unless soils, rain or escape take it (joint synthesis).
Each terawatt used on the Moon warms all of it by 0.017–0.026 K (provisioning). The night's darkness, the flight routes
and the shared heat budget likewise belong to everyone.

The winds gather towns that drift at one height onto shared tracks. At 2.5 km routes released 300 km apart come within
12 km of a neighbour in a month, and within half a year every route runs within one model cell of another; at 40 km
they close from 210 km apart after a month to 80 km after a year and 7 km after ten, over the poles; at 30 km they keep
117 km apart after ten years (*screen*, from the zonal-wind product).

### What it opens

Decisions about water, light at night, flight routes and heat reach well beyond the community that makes them, and
the drifting towns that the winds bring onto one track share their passing places, meetings and storm routes. Reasoning
from people and communities, those who live with a decision's effects need a voice in it, those who maintain the shared
air and water need their work seen and shared fairly, and everyone needs access to essential services and freedom to move
between communities. The Moon's future nations descend from all of Earth's nations and form as their own lunar nations;
the coordination they build across scales, from a lake town's wetland to the whole Moon's air, is theirs to shape.

## 8. Ties with Earth

### The physical situation

- **Talking.** Light takes 1.21–1.35 s each way to Earth (1.28 at the mean distance), a round trip of 2.4–2.7 s, twelve
  of conversation's 0.21 s gaps (*screen*; Stivers et al. 2009).
- **Travel.** A transfer from the Moon's distance to Earth takes 5.0 days at least energy, 2.6 days on an ellipse reaching
  twice the Moon's distance and 2.1 days on a parabola (*screen*, Earth's gravity alone). The dense air ends launch from
  the ground, so the ascent leaves from altitude, by tether, elevator or rocket (provisioning).
- **The Earth in the sky.** From the near side the Earth stands fixed, 1.8–2.0° across, new near local noon and full near
  local midnight, and its face turns once every 24.84 hours (*screen*). It stands 61° up by Nubium, 66° over Tranquility,
  12° by Russell and on the horizon at Smythii; the summit, Korolev, the Ingenii coast and the highland box never see it
  (*screen*).

### What it opens

Families divided between Earth and the Moon can talk every day, with a pause of two and a half seconds in every
exchange. The near side lives under the Earth: its turning face is a 24.8-hour clock in the
sky, close to the body's own, and its full disk lights the night. The far side lives without it and keeps the darkest
nights and the radio quiet. A trip to Earth takes days in space, and for people raised at a sixth of the weight, the
preparation section 5 describes.

## Evidence and boundaries

The light is clear-sky light on level ground from the calendar's solved column in the joint synthesis's mean geometry:
no libration (which moves the Earth by up to about 8° in each place's sky), refraction, terrain horizon, haze or cloud.
Heights above the ground change the horizon here and leave the twilight's light curve at the ground's. The air is the
design run's global mean by height; the winds, drifting days and routes are its 3-day means at about 170 km, with
storms, gusts and turbulence averaged out, for towns that hold their height and do not steer. Comfort over land carries
the climate work's range between the GCM and CM1. The circadian levels are defined at the eye in melanopic units and are
set beside horizontal photopic light only as a reference. The gathering, lighting and traction figures rest on stated
assumptions. The human evidence covers adults in orbit, 75 hours on the Moon, and animals; it does not cover a life, a
childhood or a pregnancy at a sixth of Earth's gravity. The social possibilities are choices open to the people who will
live there.
