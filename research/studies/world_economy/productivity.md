# Production on Earth, Open Moon and array

This proposal compares annual production on a successfully settled Moon with production on a future Earth using the same mature technology. Its reader is the project researcher; the sector weights, relative outputs, future price scale and depreciation sensitivities are assumptions for examination, not empirical estimates or accepted design decisions. Moon proper has 8 billion residents, Earth 9 billion, and the separately reported array 5 billion. Other colonies remain outside these totals.

The physical evidence supports substantial economic activity and distinctive comparative advantages on the Moon. It does not identify a unique GDP. The defensible first result is therefore a conditional comparison around parity: equal value added per resident gives Moon/Earth = 8/9 = 0.889, and the Moon needs 12.5% greater value added per resident to match Earth's total. The sector screen gives approximately 0.7–1.1 Earth products for the Moon and 0.4–0.8 for the separately accounted array. This interval describes selected scenarios; its endpoints are not statistical bounds.

## Sector proposal and its limits

The weights partition future Earth's gross value added at a common reference price basis. The ratios measure realised lunar value added per resident relative to Earth's corresponding sector. They jointly represent sector scale, deployment, demand and productive efficiency; calling them labour-productivity multipliers would be misleading. Every row is an assumption informed qualitatively by the project's physical studies.

The implementation must assign each activity to one row. Food processing belongs with food here and is excluded from other manufacturing; hotels belong with visitor services and are excluded from ordinary housing. Repair services must receive the same treatment on all three worlds. The numbers carried to four decimals expose the arithmetic; the narrative should round them to about 0.74, 0.93 and 1.12, or more broadly 0.7–1.1.

**Food and biological products.** The Moon's productive vegetation and specialised aerophyte products support agricultural possibilities. Whole-biome net primary production is not crop yield, and crop yield is only one part of food value added. The accepted food blend also depends on manufactured food, power and nutrient return. Earth receives the same automation, fermentation, crop improvement and recycling technology. The current 301 K design has not yet supplied a final coupled weather-and-optics solution, so older crop and hydrology products remain provisional inputs to this comparison.

**Materials, construction and housing.** Reduced structural weight and extensive aerial habitation create valuable possibilities, but structural material does not scale simply as gravity. Wind loads, stiffness, envelopes, gas replacement and residential services remain. Heavy industry is assigned to orbit; lunar GDP should not absorb all its production. Newly built and maintained housing provides services, while the quantity of construction required to keep it habitable is not by itself evidence of superior welfare. Aerophyte living area is a conditional supplement, with engineered floors and full residential support still unresolved.

**Utilities and transport.** Beamed power is abundant in physical potential, with delivery, receiver, resilience and heat-management requirements. Its production is assigned to the array and lunar distribution to the Moon. The sky-fleet model gives wing energy per kilometre near one-sixth Earth's under matched conditions. If energy were 5%, 15% or 25% of total transport-service cost, this alone would reduce unit cost 4.2%, 12.5% or 20.8%, permitting roughly 1.04, 1.14 or 1.26 times the service at unchanged spending. Those energy cost shares are sensitivity assumptions. Buoyant lift per cubic metre is unchanged; only the weight-driven part of its structure becomes lighter. These mechanisms support moderate transport advantages, not a sixfold GDP multiplier.

**Digital and professional services.** Proximity to orbital computing favours some interactive work; hardware, renewal, latency and demand still constrain what is supplied. Earth can deploy the same technology and own orbital assets. Solar power or operations per second cannot be multiplied by today's service prices to obtain future GDP. Public and social services likewise have no demonstrated blanket lunar efficiency advantage. The comparison assumes the health, institutions and reliability needed for mature settlement; it is conditional on that successful state rather than an estimate of its probability.

**Leisure and visitor services.** Flight, inhabited sky, novel ecology and lunar landscapes are differentiated offerings. The upper case requires people to demand and pay for them at scale, within access and conservation limits. A scarcity premium raises nominal prices; it does not automatically represent more real services. Free ecological experiences can improve life without receiving a GDP price.

The main weakness of this screen is that judgement sets every realised-output ratio. The table organises the uncertainty and makes its consequences testable; it does not derive economics from the physical inventory. Sensitivity to sector composition is smaller within the trial baskets: a material-weighted basket [6,20,17,8,12,12,15,10]% gives Moon/Earth 0.716/0.920/1.111; a service-weighted basket [2,8,12,4,10,30,24,10]% gives 0.749/0.938/1.117. Neither test establishes the scenario range as likely.

## Future Earth, prices and the accounting bridge

Future Earth should share the Moon's mature automation, medicine, manufacturing, clean power, agricultural technology and access to orbital services. Earth's inherited atmosphere and ecosystem are valuable assets, but its cities, equipment and infrastructure still depreciate. Earth should not be charged a speculative disaster burden merely to favour the Moon. Any future climate repair or adaptation scenario belongs in an explicit, symmetric cost ledger.

Use Earth annual product = 100 for the principal result. A secondary common reference-dollar scale may illustrate future Earth product of 50,000, 100,000 or 250,000 per resident per year. At 9 billion residents these imply 450, 900 or 2,250 trillion reference dollars annually. They are normalisations or income scenarios, not forecasts for a named year. They should not be labelled measured future PPPs: new products and qualities have no observed future comparison basket.

There are two different uses of this scale. If every quantity is specified in Earth-product units, changing the dollar label only changes the display. If physical capital and maintenance retain fixed reference-dollar unit costs while annual product per resident changes, the scale is an economic income assumption: moving from 50,000 to 250,000 reduces the same fixed capital or debt burden relative to output by a factor of five. Preserve that distinction in sensitivity reporting. Transfers and debt payments require a common tradable numeraire or an explicit real-exchange-rate assumption; PPP purchasing power is not itself a transferable currency.

The sector sum is GVA at basic prices. GDP at market prices adds net taxes on products. If t_w denotes net product taxes divided by GDP, GDP_w = GVA_w/(1-t_w). The common-tax treatment t_M = t_E makes the GVA ratio equal the GDP ratio. Otherwise report the factor (1-t_E)/(1-t_M), rather than silently calling GVA GDP. Do not treat all taxes on production and income as this bridge.

## Ordinary depreciation

Use a common ordinary-capital CFC sensitivity of 10%, 15% and 25% of gross product for all three worlds before adding clearly non-overlapping special assets. These are illustrative future assumptions. They allow durable capital at one end and substantial equipment, computing and intellectual-property renewal at the other. Automation may raise capital intensity; engineered longevity may lower depreciation. Neither effect has been quantified for the mature project.

A present-day scale check is available: the original BEA September 2026 annual update reports US 2025 GDP of 30,861.3 billion dollars and CFC of 5,055.5 billion, giving 16.38%. This observed national ratio supports the order of magnitude, not a future global coefficient. Where the project has asset costs and service lives, itemised CFC should replace the corresponding ordinary allowance. It must not be added to a percentage that already includes the same buildings, power equipment, computers or vehicles.

Distinguish CFC from routine maintenance, current intermediate inputs and debt principal. A broad GDP haircut for operating costs followed by another full operating-cost deduction double counts. For special lunar costs, specify whether their resource use is already reflected in realised sector GVA, their capital replacement enters CFC, or the supplementary account measures essential uses of net output. The latter can explain discretionary availability but should not be relabelled conventional net product.

## Array territory, ownership and trade

For the separate array, aggregate realised value added per resident of 0.75, 1.0 or 1.5 times future Earth's can be used as explicit scenarios. They do not claim that a particular power capacity has been sold. Combining these with the Moon cases gives Moon-plus-array / Earth = 1.152/1.488/1.951. This comparison has 13 billion versus 9 billion residents; equal per-resident performance alone gives 1.444. Report the 8-billion Moon result alongside it.

The array coefficients already represent realised production serving residents, investment and external customers. Do not add a second export bonus. A later market model can limit sales by domestic demand plus external purchases and calculate value added after intermediate inputs. If Earth replaces domestic computing with array imports, Earth's competing production may change; a large export expansion cannot simply be added while all Earth sectors remain fixed. The present aggregate screen is a conditional allocation of production, not a trade equilibrium or an estimate of the incremental benefit of building the array.

Earth residents may own array or lunar assets, and Moon residents may own array assets. Those ownership flows alter residents' income without relocating territorial production. An association between the Moon and its protective array therefore establishes neither exclusive lunar ownership nor exclusive lunar access to the array's industry.
