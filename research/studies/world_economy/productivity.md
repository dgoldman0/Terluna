# Production on Earth, Open Moon and array

This scenario study compares annual production on a settled Moon with production on a future Earth that has the same mature technology. The Moon has 8 billion residents, Earth 9 billion, and the separately reported array 5 billion; the colonies sit outside these totals. The sector weights, relative outputs, price scale and depreciation shares are assumptions laid out for examination, each informed qualitatively by the project's physical studies.

**The result is a comparison around parity.** Equal value added per resident would give Moon/Earth = 8/9 = 0.889, and the Moon needs 12.5% more value added per resident to match Earth's total. The sector scenarios give 0.74, 0.93 or 1.12 Earth products for the Moon and 0.42, 0.56 or 0.83 for the array, a span of selected scenarios. The physical evidence supports substantial activity and distinctive comparative advantages on the Moon, and the scenarios turn them into output ratios by judgement, sector by sector.

## The sector scenarios

The weights partition future Earth's gross value added at a common reference price basis. Each ratio is the Moon's realised value added per resident relative to Earth's in the same sector, so it combines sector scale, deployment, demand and productive efficiency in one number; it measures realised output, and labour productivity is one of its parts.

Each activity belongs to one row: food processing sits with food, hotels with visitor services, and repair services are treated alike on all three worlds. The four-decimal values carry the arithmetic, rounding to about 0.74, 0.93 and 1.12.

**Food and biological products.** The Moon's productive vegetation and specialised aerophyte products open agricultural possibilities. Whole-biome net primary production differs from crop yield, and crop yield is one part of food value added; the accepted food blend also rests on manufactured food, power and nutrient return. Earth has the same automation, fermentation, crop improvement and recycling. The older crop and hydrology products stand in until the 301 K design run supplies its weather and light.

**Materials, construction and housing.** Reduced structural weight and extensive aerial habitation create valuable possibilities, while structural material follows wind loads, stiffness, envelopes, gas replacement and residential services as well as gravity. Heavy industry sits in orbit, in the array's account. Newly built and maintained housing provides services, and the amount of construction needed to keep a place habitable measures its cost more than its welfare. Aerophyte living area is a conditional supplement, with engineered floors and full residential support to come.

**Utilities and transport.** Beamed power is abundant in physical potential, with delivery, receivers, resilience and heat management as its requirements; its production is the array's and lunar distribution the Moon's. The sky-fleet model gives wing energy per kilometre near a sixth of Earth's under matched conditions. If energy were 5%, 15% or 25% of transport-service cost, that alone would cut unit cost 4.2%, 12.5% or 20.8%, roughly 1.04, 1.14 or 1.26 times the service for the same spending, with the energy shares as sensitivity assumptions. Buoyant lift per cubic metre is unchanged, and only the weight-driven part of a buoyant structure gets lighter. These mechanisms support moderate transport advantages, of the order of the table's ratios.

**Digital and professional services.** Proximity to orbital computing favours some interactive work, within the limits of hardware, renewal, latency and demand. Earth can deploy the same technology and own orbital assets, and solar power or operations per second times today's service prices would overstate future output. Collective and social services get no blanket lunar efficiency gain. The comparison describes a successful mature settlement with the health, institutions and reliability that implies.

**Leisure and visitor services.** Flight, an inhabited sky, novel ecology and lunar landscapes are differentiated offerings. The upper case needs people to want and pay for them at scale, within access and conservation limits. A scarcity premium raises nominal prices without raising real services, and free ecological experiences improve life outside the measured product.

The scenarios' main limit is that judgement sets every realised-output ratio: the table organises the uncertainty and makes its consequences testable, and the physical inventory constrains costs rather than output. The sector mix moves the results little. A material-weighted basket of [6, 20, 17, 8, 12, 12, 15, 10]% gives Moon/Earth 0.716, 0.920 and 1.111, and a service-weighted basket of [2, 8, 12, 4, 10, 30, 24, 10]% gives 0.749, 0.938 and 1.117.

## Future Earth, prices and the accounting bridge

Future Earth shares the Moon's mature automation, medicine, manufacturing, clean power, agricultural technology and access to orbital services. Its inherited atmosphere and ecosystem are valuable assets, and its cities, equipment and infrastructure still depreciate. Any future climate repair or adaptation on Earth belongs in an explicit, symmetric cost ledger, with no speculative disaster burden charged to Earth alone.

Earth's annual product is 100 in the principal result. A common reference scale illustrates future Earth's product at 50,000, 100,000 or 250,000 per resident a year, 450, 900 or 2,250 trillion annually at 9 billion residents: normalisations and income scenarios for no named year, kept apart from measured future PPPs, since new products and qualities have no observed future basket.

The scale works in two ways. Where every quantity is in Earth-product units, changing its label changes only the display. Where physical capital and maintenance keep fixed reference unit costs while product per resident changes, the scale is an income assumption: moving from 50,000 to 250,000 cuts the same fixed capital or debt burden relative to output fivefold. The sensitivity reporting keeps the two apart. Transfers and debt payments need a common tradable numeraire or an explicit real exchange rate, because PPP purchasing power is no transferable currency.

The sector sum is GVA at basic prices, and GDP at market prices adds net taxes on products: with t_w the net product taxes divided by GDP, GDP_w = GVA_w/(1 − t_w). The common treatment t_M = t_E makes the GVA ratio equal the GDP ratio; otherwise the factor (1 − t_E)/(1 − t_M) is reported. The bridge uses product taxes alone.

## Ordinary depreciation

A common ordinary-capital CFC sensitivity of 10%, 15% and 25% of gross product applies on all three worlds before the non-overlapping special assets are added. The range spans durable capital at one end and heavy renewal of equipment, computing and intellectual property at the other; automation may raise capital intensity and engineered longevity lower depreciation, neither yet quantified for the mature project.

A present-day scale check: BEA's September 2026 annual update gives US 2025 GDP of 30,861.3 billion dollars and CFC of 5,055.5 billion, 16.38%, the order of magnitude of the central share for one national economy. Where the project has asset costs and service lives, itemised CFC replaces the corresponding part of the ordinary allowance, so buildings, power equipment, computers and vehicles are counted once.

CFC is distinct from routine maintenance, current intermediate inputs and debt principal, and a broad GDP haircut for operating costs followed by a second full operating deduction would count them twice. Each special lunar cost is placed once: in realised sector GVA, in CFC through its capital replacement, or in the supplementary account of essential uses of net output, which shows discretionary availability apart from conventional net product.

## Array territory, ownership and trade

The array's aggregate realised value added per resident is 0.75, 1.0 or 1.5 times future Earth's, as scenarios of realised production; no particular power capacity is assumed sold. With the Moon's cases these give Moon-plus-array / Earth = 1.152, 1.488 and 1.951, for 13 billion residents against 9, where equal output per resident alone gives 1.444; the 8-billion Moon result stands beside them.

The array coefficients already include production for residents, investment and outside customers, so no second export bonus is added. A later market model can limit sales by domestic demand and outside purchases and compute value added after intermediate inputs. If Earth replaces domestic computing with array imports, Earth's competing production changes too, so a large export expansion moves both sides of the account. The present screen allocates production; a trade equilibrium and the incremental benefit of building the array are separate questions.

Earth residents may own array or lunar assets, and Moon residents array assets, and those ownership flows change residents' income while territorial production stays where it is. The Moon's association with its protective array gives it neither exclusive ownership of the array nor exclusive access to its industry.
