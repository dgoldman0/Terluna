# Industrial construction through the atmospheric transition

31 October 2026. The strongest early industrial opportunity is to make useful construction stock and assemblies from local feedstock, combining a fixed working surface, low weight loads and access to ambient vacuum. Large tanks, trusses, machinery and selected coated components could benefit from avoiding a chamber around the entire workpiece. The opportunity is conditional on a deliberately preserved vacuum interval, qualified processes and customers. After atmospheric loading, lunar factories retain local materials, foundations and low gravity, while vacuum operations require chambers, local pumping or relocation.

An enclosed settlement need not put every industrial operation under one pressure roof. Occupied rooms, chemical preparation and clean assembly can operate at 1–1.2 atmospheres while robots conduct suitable hot or vacuum work outside. Interfaces for materials, maintenance, cooling and contamination control join the two environments. This arrangement offers a plausible route from enclosed communities to an enduring construction industry; its value depends on which equipment and operations it actually simplifies.

The comparison throughout is with equally advanced Earth and array industry. Earth can optimize chambers, automation and alternative processes. Array factories already have ambient vacuum, can work in microgravity and can rotate equipment that needs directed acceleration. Lunar gravity and vacuum therefore support particular location advantages, rather than a general manufacturing premium. The [economic assessment](industrial_transition.md) treats those advantages through equipment, operating and transition accounts. Selected casting and spin-casting hypotheses have a separate [manufacturing investigation](manufacturing.md).

## Candidate products and competing routes

| Candidate | Potential lunar advantage | Present evidence | Strong competing route |
|---|---|---|---|
| Selected filled castings and encapsulation compounds in sealed facilities | Enough buoyancy to remove bubbles while reducing filler settling during a constrained cure | A calculated finite-gravity window; no qualified lunar product | Faster cure, degassing and rheology control on Earth; rotating array process equipment |
| Large spin-cast or cured parabolic blanks and moulds | Fixed curvature at lower rotation speed and rotating load; gravity supplies the axial liquid shape | Hydrostatic scaling and related liquid-mirror component work | Earth spin casting and finishing; orbital centrifuges or actively shaped liquids |
| Welded tanks, pressure-vessel sections, trusses and machinery | Large workpieces in ambient vacuum; ground fixtures and smaller weight loads | Established terrestrial electron-beam welding; lunar factory economics untested | Earth local vacuum heads and other joining methods; array assembly near its customers |
| Wire-fed additive parts, repairs and fittings | Flexible addition of material, repair and reduced waste; local supply of blanks | Terrestrial and microgravity electron-beam deposition demonstrated | Casting, rolling, extrusion and welding for bulk shapes; orbital additive repair |
| Selected refined metals and compounds | Low ambient backpressure, local feedstock and a drainage direction | Mineral experiments and thermochemical calculations; no complete lunar plant | Earth vacuum furnaces; orbital processing with condensers and appropriate flow control |
| Glass fibres, cast glass and ceramic stock | Controlled dry processing; local bulk feed; reduced support weight | Ground simulant experiments, including vacuum-drawn fibres | The same controlled atmosphere on Earth; orbital drawing with mechanical take-up |
| Coated optics, metallized foil and thin-film substrates | Large coating paths and workpieces without a surrounding chamber | Terrestrial coating practice and regolith-simulant component demonstrations | Orbital coating beside fleet demand; Earth continuous coaters with confined processing zones |

These candidates serve different markets and have different scales. Standard stock can supply local construction and orbital customers; custom repairs depend on nearby installed equipment; precision optics depend on specification, finishing and transport. A successful laboratory process establishes a starting point for qualification, while a production programme also needs yield, equipment availability, material flows and orders.

The sealed comparison remains useful after the open atmosphere exists. The earlier [casting calculation](manufacturing.md) places one illustrative 100-second bubble-clearance and filler-settling process in a 1.12–2.05 m/s² operating interval. Its benefit requires a real filling or curing constraint: Earth can otherwise reproduce the same displacement by shortening the mobile interval or changing viscosity. The spin-casting calculation retains the desired curvature at 40.7% of Earth's angular speed, with 16.6% of its rotational load scale. These are product-specific hypotheses with continuing low-gravity relevance; actual prices and yields remain unestablished.

A 1–1.2-atmosphere workshop also need not expose every process to ordinary air. Its furnaces and chemical systems can use controlled gases or local vacuum, as comparable Earth and array factories do. Changing ambient pressure changes bubble formation, evaporation and chemistry, so a sealed-process result cannot simply receive an additional vacuum benefit without a new process calculation.

## The vacuum interval ends far before breathable air

Pressure requirements differ by process. TWI's thick-section electron-beam comparison uses roughly **0.1 Pa** around the workpiece. Special guns with differential pumping can operate around **100 Pa**, with selected variants described at **100–1,000 Pa**. The gun still needs its own protected vacuum. These are equipment-specific conditions, not a universal boundary between useful and unusable lunar vacuum. [TWI process development](https://www.twi-global.com/technical-knowledge/published-papers/reduced-pressure-electron-beam-welding-in-the-power-generation-industry); [pressure comparison](https://www.twi-global.com/technical-knowledge/faqs/faq-what-is-electron-beam-welding-at-reduced-pressure).

The committed air inventory is **3.14135×10¹⁸ kg**, inherited from the historical spherical atmosphere at **121,590 Pa** and 288 K. This screen retains that identity while the 301 K design develops. Shared lunar radius and gravity give the thin-column relation `p=Mg/A`, corresponding to **2.33542×10¹³ kg per pascal**. Applied to the full inventory, it gives 134,509 Pa because it neglects atmospheric extension. For globally spherical hydrostatic gas above the surface, that expression is an upper pressure bound at fixed mass.

An alternative holds the historical column's pressure-to-mass ratio fixed, using `p=p_final M/M_final`. Its mass per pascal is 10.625% larger. Neither relation predicts the evolving temperature, early exosphere or pressure near a release point. They establish how small the relevant gas inventories are.

| Pressure | Fraction of final mass, historical-column scaling | Equivalent time with uniform retained-gas loading over 500 years |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0.0001 Pa | 8.22×10⁻¹⁰ | 13 seconds |
| 0.01 Pa | 8.22×10⁻⁸ | 21.6 minutes |
| 0.1 Pa | 8.22×10⁻⁷ | 3.60 hours |
| 1 Pa | 8.22×10⁻⁶ | 1.50 days |
| 100 Pa | 0.000822 | 0.411 years |
| 1,000 Pa | 0.00822 | 4.11 years |

The corresponding thin-column times are about 9.6% shorter. These inventory clocks assume that 199 million kilograms of gas per second remain in the atmosphere. Their very short initial intervals demonstrate why a mature loading rate cannot accompany centuries of ambient vacuum. They do not predict a globally mixed atmosphere appearing in seconds: an actual programme would ramp its activity, and gas would initially form nonuniform plumes and a changing exosphere.

The [industrial architecture](../../../engineering/reference/industrial_architecture/RECOVERED_INDUSTRIAL_ARCHITECTURE.md) already stages gas and water accumulation after industrial expansion and anticipates changes to mass drivers, radiators and exposed materials. The standing overall construction horizon is 500 years; the release curve remains unresolved. Illustrative deferrals of 10, 50 or 100 years, followed by uniform loading to year 500, reach 0.1 Pa after another **3.53, 3.24 or 2.88 hours** respectively. Reaching 100 Pa takes another **0.403, 0.370 or 0.329 years**. Deferral provides the long interval; subsequent atmospheric loading provides very little additional high-vacuum operating time.

Preserving that interval requires control of all gas sources. Exposed water imports, exhaust, habitat leakage and processing vapours matter alongside planned nitrogen and oxygen delivery. Surface adsorption, cold trapping and escape distinguish delivered material from retained air. Local contamination can also invalidate a process before a global inventory threshold is reached. Primary studies of anthropogenic lunar gas support these local concerns, without modelling planetary construction. [Killen, Sprague and Farrell, 2024](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240014015/downloads/KillenTemporarySTI.pdf?attachment=true). Postponing release also extends some enclosure costs, delays open-air benefits and raises the later delivery rate; a 100-year deferral requires 25% faster loading to preserve completion at year 500.

## Pressure containment and construction in low gravity

At a specified pressure difference, low gravity does not reduce the force on a wall: `F=Δp A`. An interior at 1–1.2 atmospheres against vacuum imposes **101.3–121.6 kN/m²**. This remains a principal early construction load even where roofs, floors and equipment weigh much less than on Earth.

For an ideal thin spherical shell, thickness `t=Δp r/(2σ)` and shell mass per enclosed volume `m/V=3ρΔp/(2σ)`. A long cylindrical barrel requires twice the spherical thickness at the same radius, with `m/V=2ρΔp/σ`; its ends remain additional. [Roylance, MIT, equations 1–3](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/3-11-mechanics-of-materials-fall-1999/082fcd712adffa589a670f9f8dfdd49a_MIT3_11F99_pv.pdf). Using **chosen** density 2,700 kg/m³ and allowable stress 100 MPa:

| Pressure against vacuum | Sphere shell mass / enclosed volume | Cylinder barrel mass / barrel volume | Sphere thickness at radius 25 m / 100 m |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 101,325 Pa | 4.10 kg/m³ | 5.47 kg/m³ | 12.67 / 50.66 mm |
| 121,590 Pa | 4.92 kg/m³ | 6.57 kg/m³ | 15.20 / 60.80 mm |

These pressure-only screens omit joints, penetrations, liners, floors, shielding, foundations, thermal control and degradation allowances. A lava tube provides existing rock and shielding, but its sealing and pressure-load path require site-specific assessment. An open-bottom dome supported against uplift solely by ballast needs `Δp/g`: at 1.2 atmospheres, **74.9 tonnes/m²**, equivalent to 41.6 m at an assumed density of 1,800 kg/m³. A fully closed hull balances its pressure forces internally; the dome's ballast requirement must not be assigned to every vessel.

When local internal and external pressures match, the static pressure-difference term disappears, leaving the other structural requirements. Matching is a condition, not an automatic consequence of atmospheric completion. A sealed 101.3-kPa enclosure surrounded by 121.6-kPa air experiences 20.3 kPa of external pressure. An evacuated industrial chamber in that air carries the full external pressure and must resist buckling.

Classical elastic theory for a perfect sphere gives `p_cr=2E(t/r)²/√[3(1−ν²)]`. With chosen `E=70 GPa`, `ν=0.33` and 121,590 Pa outside, a 25-m-radius sphere reaches that condition at 29.79 mm thickness. An illustrative reduction of capacity to 20% raises the corresponding thickness to 66.61 mm. Neither is a safe design: imperfections, supports, ports and nonlinear response govern practical capacity. [Hutchinson and Thompson, 2017](https://groups.seas.harvard.edu/hutchinson/papers/2017-7-PerfectSpheres.pdf). An early dust shelter therefore cannot simply be relabelled as a later vacuum chamber.

Weight relief offers separate benefits. A tonne weighs **1.624 kN** on the Moon versus **9.807 kN** under standard Earth gravity; raising it 10 m ideally takes 16.2 rather than 98.1 kJ. Accelerating it at 1 m/s² still requires 1 kN. Cranes, fixtures and foundations can benefit from reduced weight while retaining inertia, stopping, stiffness and stability demands. Weight-derived friction and restoring moments also decline. At equal gas composition, pressure and temperature, buoyancy supports the same mass per cubic metre; its force and the supported object's weight both scale with gravity.

For the same beam section, material and support type under self-weight alone, preserving **absolute sag** permits **1.57 times the Earth span**, while preserving **bending stress** permits **2.46 times**. These follow from deflection proportional to `gL⁴` and stress proportional to `gL²`. They are alternative constraints. Pressure, machinery, vibration, thermal strain and other loads prevent using either ratio as a whole-building cost multiplier. An orbital factory avoids self-weight but must still react machining forces and control relative motion; only processes requiring effective gravity need rotating machinery.

## Large fabrication: chambers, throughput and process control

Ambient vacuum is most attractive where a large chamber would otherwise surround a large workpiece. Avoiding that enclosure can simplify geometry and loading, but the factory still needs fixtures, dust control, clean surfaces, vapour capture, electron-gun protection, cooling and inspection. Vacuum removes convective cooling; coolant loops, conduction and radiators must reject process heat.

Pump energy alone gives an incomplete valuation. Reversible isothermal evacuation of a rigid 150-m³ chamber from 101,325 Pa to 0.1 Pa, exhausting back to ambient pressure, requires at least **15.20 MJ, or 4.22 kWh**. Actual pumps incur losses and continuing gas loads. Equipment capital and occupied production time can matter more than this thermodynamic minimum.

TWI reports 70 minutes to pump a 150-m³ chamber to 0.1 Pa and 16 minutes to reach 1,000 Pa. For a repeatedly vented chamber with 120 minutes of useful processing per batch, removing those pumpdowns raises ideal capacity by **1.583 or 1.133 times**. With 480-minute processing, the factors fall to **1.146 or 1.033**. Loading and other work remain additional. These are batch-scheduling bounds, not factory-wide gains. [TWI chamber example](https://www.twi-global.com/technical-knowledge/faqs/faq-what-is-electron-beam-welding-at-reduced-pressure).

Optimized Earth offers a stronger comparator than a giant batch chamber in many applications. TWI demonstrated a mobile local head reaching below 100 Pa in less than ten seconds and maintaining pressure while traversing steel plate. Local seals, load locks and continuous production can remove much of the supposed lunar advantage. The same methods could support later lunar factories inside ordinary-pressure buildings. [Punshon and Sanderson, 2007, section 2.2](https://www.twi-global.com/technical-knowledge/published-papers/eb-welding-of-large-components-without-a-vacuum-chamber-april-2007).

Electron-beam additive manufacture remains a useful candidate for repairs, fittings and adding detail to simpler stock. NASA's microgravity demonstrations establish that finite gravity is not indispensable: useful deposition occurred, while incorrect wire positioning could release molten droplets. Process control matters alongside environment. [Hafley, Taminger and Bird, 2007](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20070005152/downloads/20070005152.pdf).

The terrestrial demonstrations reported **330–2,500 cm³/hour** deposition. At an assumed aluminium density of 2,700 kg/m³, that is **0.891–6.75 kg/hour**. At 70% duty, the higher rate deposits 41.42 tonnes per head annually, so a one-million-tonne target requires **24,144 integer heads**, before rejection and finishing. This historical scale diagnostic neither caps future technology nor specifies a sensible future plant. It shows why bulk construction should compare casting, rolling, extrusion and automated joining alongside selective additive production. [Taminger and Hafley, 2003](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20040042496/downloads/20040042496.pdf).

Partial gravity can change larger molten pools without materially changing every small one. For chosen liquid density 2,400 kg/m³ and surface tension 0.9 N/m, capillary length is **15.20 mm on the Moon and 6.18 mm on Earth**. Bond number, which compares gravity with surface tension, is 0.0390 versus 0.2354 at 3 mm and 1.73 versus 10.46 at 20 mm. A 0.2-m head supplies 780 rather than 4,707 Pa. These identify useful experimental scales; wetting, recoil pressure, heat flow and imposed motion still determine whether quality improves.

## Materials that retain value after the atmosphere arrives

Refining benefits from low vapour backpressure only when heating, chemical separation and collection also work. Preliminary mineral-pyrolysis research describes high-temperature dissociation and possible condensed products, while later simulant work combines measured sintering behaviour with thermochemical evaporation estimates. Neither supplies a demonstrated steady lunar plant yield. Species partial pressures, shrinking reactive area, condensate purity and recovery govern useful output. [Senior, author abstract](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19920033574); [Shaw et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/min13010079). Finite feed composition also limits extraction: useful potassium and other nutrients should enter conserved inventories, rather than being treated as disposable process exhaust.

Glass and coatings offer concrete component precedents. Freundlich and colleagues produced smooth simulant-glass substrates and transparent films composed mainly of silicon oxide; these support further substrate and coating work, while semiconductor purity, complete devices and packaging remain separate requirements. [Freundlich et al., 2005](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20050110155/downloads/20050110155.pdf). A ground study of vacuum-drawn simulated-regolith fibres reports stronger fibres than its air-drawn control. Its retrieved abstract and introduction support investigating atmosphere-controlled manufacture, but do not establish a lunar-gravity optimum or long-term humid-service strength. [Shi et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ceramint.2025.10.337).

Protected fibre reels, billets, standardized sections and qualified components can carry useful production into the atmospheric era. Exposed dry-strength measurements cannot serve directly as wet-environment design allowables: moisture-sensitive glass requires sealing, coatings or qualification for its intended service. Precision films and optics need protected packaging, inspection and compatible future specifications. Durable intermediate stock may consequently retain value more reliably than a large speculative inventory of finished assemblies. Source-access limits, including abstract-only and indexed-text evidence, are recorded in [industrial_sources.json](industrial_sources.json).

## Delivery and industrial continuity

Location should follow the complete product route. The existing [resource architecture](../../../resources/industry.md) places major film renewal near the fleet, lunar construction materials near their local users and appropriate bulk processing at source bodies or hubs. An early surface coating opportunity does not displace all of those relationships. Orbital customers may prefer compact refined stock followed by final fabrication in orbit, avoiding launch of delicate finished products.

Future geography determines which surface assets survive. The selected 28% standing-water atlas floods lowland mare sites, and the [land-city study](../land_cities/README.md) places many mapped tube candidates beneath future seas; groundwater also threatens tubes remaining under dry land. Long-lived factories should therefore be assessed on future dry sites, or assigned permitted waterproofing, pumping or relocation works. Early extraction and stockpiling must respect the [conservation programme](../conservation/README.md), future mineral reservations and accepted park-and-garden land uses. A useful ore body or existing industrial pad does not automatically authorize permanent industry there.

Qualified pressure hulls may be reused after air arrives, subject to their new loads, corrosion, humidity and maintenance conditions. Productive orbital assets and stock already positioned near orbital customers offer another strong route for retaining early value: they avoid the surface atmosphere's direct transformation. They still require renewal, storage protection and continuing demand. Their contribution belongs alongside surface reuse in the [transition account](industrial_transition.md), without assuming a monetary return from their mere existence.

The ideal mechanical-energy increase from a stationary lunar surface to a circular orbit at the representative 20,000-km radius is **2.699 MJ/kg**, including final orbital kinetic energy. It excludes propulsion losses, drag, equipment and capture. Reaching a chosen 2.4 km/s at constant acceleration needs **288 km at 10 m/s², 28.8 km at 100 m/s² or 2.88 km at 1,000 m/s²**. Low resting weight does not remove acceleration stress. Bulk packets and fragile optics therefore require different packaging and possibly different launch systems; atmospheric loading adds further route constraints.

A useful transition plan matches production to actual construction stages, stores durable stock, and identifies equipment that remains, gains a chamber or moves outward. Occupied facilities, utilities and qualified handling systems may survive the transition even when ambient-vacuum operations change. The lasting lunar industry can combine those assets with local demand and feedstock. Its early vacuum phase contributes value where verified process savings exceed the costs of preserving that phase, maintaining enclosures, storing output and changing factories afterward. Those terms belong in the [financial assessment](industrial_transition.md), without adding a second manufacturing premium to the three-world product accounts.
