# Sports for the first enclosed lunar community

**Concept proposals, 31 October 2026.** An early sporting programme can begin with three disciplines that use ordinary bodies, built contacts and a shared hall: a timed gate circuit, aerial figures, and a team catching game. Each makes prolonged airborne movement part of an intelligible contest. This note develops candidate rules and facilities for designers, instructors and programme planners; it supplies neither qualified equipment nor a forecast of participation or revenue.

The illustrative environment is lunar gravity, **1.2 atm, approximately 17.5% oxygen and 301 K**, matching the current comparison. An early enclosed community can select its own operating conditions; that selection would precede qualification of its activities. The mature atmospheric lock does not require every early practice room to use these illustrative conditions. Humidity, ventilation, clothing and individual response remain unspecified.

## The movement the games should use

The existing [activity product](results/distinctive_activities.json) evaluates a **vertical takeoff component of 2.5 m/s**. Without drag, the centre of mass rises **1.924 m** and returns to its launch height after **3.078 s**, compared with 0.319 m and 0.510 s on Earth. Those are trajectories at a prescribed velocity, not predictions of an athlete's effort or capability. Limb reach and starting body height must be added when considering overhead space.

As a separate illustrative calculation, adding 2 m/s horizontally gives 6.157 m of travel before returning to the same height. Total launch speed is then 3.202 m/s, not 2.5 m/s. Different landing heights, aerodynamic forces and actual propulsion change the result. An automatic sixfold increase in sporting range would therefore be an inappropriate course-design rule.

The [habitation mechanics](../../../habitation/low_gravity/README.md) provide the other essential distinction: weight falls while mass and momentum remain. Their assumed friction coefficient of 0.6 gives a 4.6 m stopping distance from 3 m/s on level ground; this is a reference calculation, not a sporting runout specification. A player cannot make a sharp terrestrial running cut merely because their body is lighter. In flight, changing posture can alter orientation and rotation rate, but does not redirect the centre of mass without an external force. Built handholds, footholds and landing structures can supply those forces; their attachments must carry the resulting inertial loads.

A **40 m × 16 m × 8 m clear activity volume** is proposed for comparing the formats. It is a planning box, not a minimum safe hall. Spectator circulation, changing rooms, pressure equipment, emergency access and maintenance require additional provision. A tube supplies geological accommodation; a supplied enclosure, independent equipment supports and an operating community make that accommodation usable. The playing apparatus should connect to its designed support structure rather than treating an airtight liner as a climbing wall.

## 1. Gate circuit: committing to a route and finishing under control

The gate circuit rewards precise changes between ground contact, supported movement and free flight. Its proposed course occupies **36 m × 12 m**, with eight metres of clear height. Six numbered stations form a one-way route through three successive bays. Each bay contains two successive transfers, each with a launch area, a visible crossing gate and a marked arrival area. Removable platforms and anchored handholds let the same equipment create different routes.

A run is valid when the athlete completes all six stations in order and finishes in the final arrival area. **Fastest valid time wins.** A missed gate requires a return to that station's launch point using the designated recovery route; the clock continues. An arrival counts after the required contacts are established inside its marked area for one second. Those contacts are declared before the round: two feet for one station, or feet plus a named handhold for another. The one-second interval defines scoring, not a physiological recovery requirement.

The first route would keep launch and landing surfaces near the same level and offer transfer distances of **two to four metres**, selected as prototype geometry. A gate lies within the planned arc rather than demanding maximum height. Later routes could add modest changes of level, a supported turn between bays, or a choice between two equally scored paths. None of those dimensions is inferred to be achievable by every entrant from the 2.5 m/s reference.

The distinctive skill is committing to a trajectory early enough to arrive where the next useful contact exists. A long flight gives spectators time to see an error develop and an athlete time to change body orientation. It does not supply a midair steering force. Launch angle, horizontal impulse and the timing of a hand contact consequently matter more than simply running harder. Requiring a controlled arrival also prevents the contest from becoming only a maximum-speed launch down the hall.

Practice begins with one transfer and a generously defined target, followed by two linked stations. Full-course rehearsals establish whether competitors can repeat trajectories and whether a missed station permits a workable reset. Competition initially uses **one athlete in the course**, with the next competitor outside its activity volume. A split-screen recording can compare two runs without introducing simultaneous traffic. Head-to-head racing would require a second independently arranged course and its own operating assessment.

The audience sees six numbered tasks, a running clock and an immediate record of each valid arrival. A view along each bay explains the route; side views show the arc. Course changes can keep a season interesting while preserving a common test of contact, flight and control.

## 2. Aerial figures: making a single jump a complete performance

Aerial figures use the additional time aloft for orientation, deliberate shapes and a composed return. A proposed practice layout places **two 8 m × 8 m activity cells** in a **20 m × 12 m floor allocation**, with eight metres of clear height. The remaining dimensions are layout allowances, not demonstrated separation distances. Each cell has a marked launch area and a larger visible landing target; competitors begin on the floor rather than a high platform.

An initial meet comprises four prescribed passes: an extended straight jump, a half-turn about the upright axis, a full upright turn, and a passage that changes from a compact shape to an extended shape. These are candidate tasks to test, not assertions of established lunar technique. Using the same tasks for all entrants makes early results interpretable before a difficulty catalogue exists.

Each pass offers **ten points**: two for completing the declared sequence, two for the required orientation at the declared judging moment, two for the specified body shape, two for landing location, and two for the finish. Task cards specify shape at the apex and orientation immediately before first landing contact; the complete flight establishes rotation count. Published judging examples define partial credit. The maximum is forty points, with tied totals sharing a placing during the introductory format. Video review distinguishes a completed orientation from a turn finished after touchdown. More ambitious rotations or off-axis elements enter a later catalogue only after their mechanics and operation have been evaluated.

Rotation begins with angular momentum imparted during contact with the floor or apparatus. Drawing limbs inward can increase rotation rate; extending them can reduce it. The athlete must manage the remaining rotation before meeting the landing surface. Low gravity gives more time for that sequence, while leaving the body's rotational inertia and the consequences of a poorly controlled contact present. A three-second flight could contain a recognizable beginning, held figure and return, rather than merely stretching the replay of an Earth jump.

Practice separates those operations: launch and return first, then orientation, then a figure introduced during a repeatable arc. A participant need not reach the reference takeoff speed to rehearse them. Instructors and participants would determine usable tasks from measured performance rather than assigning proficiency from age or presumed athletic background.

After individual routines are established, two performers could use the separate cells for a synchronized category. Its interest comes from matching apex timing, complementary shapes and simultaneous returns while each retains a separate movement volume. That offers a route from scored sport into choreography and staged entertainment without immediately requiring catches between bodies. Cameras should retain an ordinary-speed view: the audience needs to see that the interval is actual performance time.

## 3. Aerial catch: a team game built around the pass made in flight

Aerial catch gives six players a cooperative and adversarial problem. The proposed court is **18 m × 12 m**, divided at mid-length into opposing halves. Each half has three four-metre-wide lanes, one player assigned to each. Players remain within their own lane and half; only the ball crosses between them. Marked launch areas and available hand contacts make legal support surfaces clear. These rules organize movement, without establishing that unintended crossings or impacts cannot occur.

Each team's back boundary carries three target windows, one aligned with each lane. An illustrative window is one metre square with its centre three metres above the floor. A ball passing completely through a window scores **one point** for the opposing team; goals are the only source of points. A match has two six-minute periods and a two-minute interval, with the clock running through ordinary restarts. Most goals wins; ties remain draws in the introductory format. Teams exchange ends at the interval. These are proposed programming rules; suspension and apparatus reset require separate booking allowances. Opening possession is drawn by lot; the team conceding a goal restarts in a marked launch area.

If the ball reaches the floor or an out-of-bounds surface, the last team to touch it loses possession. Defenders may therefore let an inaccurate attacking throw go. Hands and forearms are permitted contacts. Deliberately targeting a player's body, or deliberately blocking with another body part, is a conduct fault that transfers possession and stops play for official review. Incidental contact elsewhere on the body makes the ball dead and the possession is replayed.

A team must involve all three players before attacking across the centre. **One internal pass must be received and passed onward during the same airborne interval**. Teammates choose their passing order and launch timing. Holding the ball beyond four seconds, or crossing a lane boundary, transfers possession; these are proposed rules, not performance or safety thresholds. Players rotate lane assignments between possessions.

Defenders intercept within their lanes. A successful catch begins their team's three-player sequence. The attacking team can change which lane receives the final throw, making the defenders decide whether to launch early to cover a high window or retain contact for a later response. A defender already airborne cannot simply run toward a different trajectory. The passing team likewise must synchronize its airborne exchange with a teammate's arc rather than treating a jumping player as a stationary target.

The initial ball would be a conspicuous, deformable prototype whose mass, size, surface and rebound behaviour are specified before trials. Its trajectories require measurement in the hall: light balls can respond strongly to drag, spin and ventilation. The human ballistic reference does not predict their range or certify an impact. Early practice therefore starts with two people exchanging from fixed positions, progresses to the airborne receive-and-release, then to three-player sequences before opposing teams are introduced.

The score is simple enough to follow immediately, while the aerial exchange supplies a visible signature. Viewers see a teammate preparing to leave the floor as the pass develops, and whether the throw arrives during the useful part of that arc. Replay can explain failed timing without changing the live rules. Restricting body movement to lanes also gives an initial game a tractable set of interactions before any later format permits unrestricted pursuit.

## Building a programme from the shared hall

The formats compete for time as well as space. A circuit competition occupies its whole layout with one active athlete. Figures can use two configured cells; the catch game uses six players in its court. Small practice stations may serve several groups concurrently only where their activity volumes, instruction and recovery arrangements have been separately established.

An illustrative programme alternates morning station practice, an afternoon block for one complete format, and selected evening recorded events. Reconfiguring and inspecting apparatus consumes time; a catch court cannot simultaneously be assumed available for circuit runs. The earlier sports-finance screen still assumes **twenty simultaneous participants, an unqualified capacity**. These proposals do not validate it, and their spectator, professional-practice and visitor uses need a common schedule before participant-hours become a useful sales capacity.

A first season could therefore start with measured circuit trials and individual figures, build a recorded series around repeat entrants, then introduce the catch game as teams demonstrate workable sequences. Visiting participants could attend instruction and defined practice sessions while a resident cohort supplies the main programme. This sequence gives the community recurring use of its apparatus and gives viewers people and contests to follow; whether either attracts sufficient demand remains an economic question.

## Where flight and water enter later

Unpowered body motion can use a short arc between built contacts. Sustained aircraft flight also needs room to maneuver, manage airflow and recover the vehicle. For illustration, a steady level banked turn obeys `r = v² / (g tan(bank angle))`: a 30-degree bank at 3 m/s gives a lunar radius of **9.60 m**, rising to **38.39 m at 6 m/s**. These are force-balance calculations, not qualified craft performance. Even a low-speed aircraft can require a different hall geometry from the proposed course. Tethered equipment trials could precede a free-flight discipline without establishing the latter's arena requirements.

Water sports add water inventory, containment, circulation and recovery provisions. The existing water-wave reference identifies interesting later possibilities, but no large tank is needed to obtain the first three disciplines' principal effect. Their early material programme consists chiefly of an occupied enclosure, reusable fixtures, playing equipment and recording capability. That makes them useful candidates for a community choosing what to develop first, while leaving the actual capital and operating requirements to a facility design.
