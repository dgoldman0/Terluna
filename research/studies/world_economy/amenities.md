# Hybrid amenities and three-world tourism

The hybrid park and garden regions carry a substantial part of ordinary lunar life as well as visitor activity. This extension gives resident and visitor demand explicit accounts, checks all six interworld travel directions and connects their trade to the [development-finance study](development.md). It attributes activity within the existing economic reference.

## Hybrid regions, local life and production

The connected-travel scenario attributes **24.23 trillion U a year** of direct lunar value added to interworld tourism, including **2.23 trillion U** of transport services, and **35.25 trillion U** to residents' local amenities. An assumed half of local amenity production and half of inbound destination services are associated with the hybrid regions, giving **28.62 trillion U** of annual value added attributed to those regions. These amounts describe portions of existing production: the local and destination services fit within the reference economy's **104 trillion U** leisure allocation, while transport remains in its own sector. They do not increase the reference GWP or NWP.

All monetary amounts use the study's common real unit U and its close-parity production case, with annual GWP of 900 trillion U on future Earth, 839.2 trillion U on the Moon and 500 trillion U in the array. The three worlds share mature technology and have nine, eight and five billion residents respectively. Prices and spending patterns are assumptions expressed in that common unit.

The hybrid allocation gives this activity a substantial physical setting. Under the accepted land-use compromise, heavily manicured megaparks and great gardens occupy **4.68 million km²**, or **585 m² for each of the Moon's eight billion residents** before visitors. They are intended for people living on the ground and aloft as well as for visitors. Small shops, boutiques, food services, restrooms and places to rest accompany the gardens; farms, grazing, homesteads and towns remain outside their allocation. The area is 20% of dry land, with the preferred half-wild case and the separate half-wild-plus-hybrid comparison retained in the [distributions study](../distributions/README.md).

Their economic role extends through ordinary life. Residents can spend an afternoon among shaded planting, meet friends beside a rill, eat at an automated food-service shop, or return to see the garden change through the lunar cycle. The accepted [garden programme](../../../habitation/README.md#parks-and-gardens) combines regional dry-adapted plants, silver foliage, dawn and dusk flowering, living light and night scent. Mirror pools and narrow watercourses serve suitable lowlands; stone and gravel rivers provide dry highland features. Shade structures and planting follow local microclimates. Jura Island supplies a regional example, with garden slopes facing the bay and a wild high arc and outer coast. Such varied landscapes support recurring local use, recreation and cultural activity alongside trips undertaken to experience a distinctive place.

Automation maintains the gardens and their amenities. It may allow more frequent maintenance, longer opening and extensive provision with little direct labour, but it does not determine prices, demand or profitability. The accepted 301 K climate design also leaves regional comfort and management requirements to be established: older garden and Jura climate descriptions are not simulations of comfort under the revised design. Shade, water demand, seasonal and lunar-cycle use, transport access and peak attendance require their own physical calculations.

The economic channels have different accounting treatments:

| Channel | Treatment within the existing economy |
|---|---|
| Resident recreation and amenities | Paid recreation, performances, food services and other local services contribute their producers' value added. The present local allocation includes destination amenities used on domestic trips; passenger fares remain outside this allocation. |
| Public garden services | Public or nonprofit maintenance and access services contribute recorded production even when entry is free. Public finance and user charges determine who pays; they do not create a second copy of the service. |
| Housing near attractive landscapes | Any demonstrated improvement in annual housing services belongs in the housing account. Property appreciation is an asset-price change, and no housing premium is added here. |
| Health, learning and work | Gardens may support these activities, but the screen assigns no additional productivity effect. An effect would need evidence and an explicit change to the affected sector. |
| Food gardens | Harvested products would enter food production and their processing or food services as appropriate. The accepted food-blend study excludes garden harvest; this calculation introduces no yield. |
| Wider public benefit | Access, rest, pleasure, social connection, ecological condition and freedom to use a place remain relevant outcomes. No cash value is assigned to those benefits here. |

Free entry therefore has two distinct implications. Maintained public services can already enter national accounts, generally through their costs of production, while the value people obtain beyond that measured service remains outside the GDP calculation. Purchased inputs must be separated from the garden operator's own value added before aggregating sectors. The public-service account has not been separately estimated in this screen; a later costed account should identify its existing sector allocation rather than add an imputed admission charge to every visit. [BEA, NIPA Handbook chapter 9, definitions of government production](https://www.bea.gov/sites/default/files/2019-12/Chapter-9.pdf).

## Attribution and the resources required

The local-amenity assumptions allocate expenditure equal to 4% of Earth's GWP, 7% of the Moon's and 5% of the array's. A common direct value-added share of 60% converts those purchases to **21.60, 35.25 and 15.00 trillion U** respectively. These percentages are expenditure scenarios, not observed household preferences. Visitor destination services use a 65% direct value-added share and transport 50%; the remainder consists of intermediate purchases, whose producing sectors are already present in the broader economy.

For the Moon in the connected case, destination services contribute **21.99 trillion U**. Together with local amenities they account for **57.24 trillion U** of the 104-trillion leisure row, leaving **46.76 trillion U** for its other activities. The hybrid attribution is half of each component: **17.62 trillion U** from local services and **11.00 trillion U** from inbound services. It excludes the separate transport contribution. The attribution does not establish that all associated spending occurs physically inside the parks, nor that the gardens caused all of it.

The use assumptions are similarly explicit. Fifty-two resident visits per year give **416 billion visits annually**, averaging **243 resident visits per km² per Earth-equivalent day** across the full allocation. An assumed 80% of connected-case inbound visitors use a hybrid region, or **2.86 billion visitor trips a year**. These are use scenarios. Their spatial averages cannot establish access, peak crowding or the capacity of any particular garden, harbour, aerial interchange or amenity.

The park-cost sensitivity applies alternative unit budgets to the accepted area:

| Assumed provision | Initial produced capital, trillion U | Annual operations, trillion U | Annual renewal, trillion U | Annual operating and renewal requirement, trillion U |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Sparse amenities | 9.36 | 0.468 | 0.234 | 0.702 |
| Distributed amenities | 46.80 | 2.340 | 1.560 | 3.900 |
| Intensive amenities | 234.00 | 9.360 | 11.700 | 21.060 |

These are alternative resource budgets obtained from assumed capital costs of 2, 10 and 50 U/m², operating costs of 0.1, 0.5 and 2 U/m²/year, and replacement lives of 40, 30 and 20 years. Water systems, maintenance machinery, utilities and peak capacity have not been engineered to validate them. Nor may they simply be deducted from GWP: operating purchases already enter the production accounts, and ordinary capital and depreciation allowances may include the same assets. A project cash calculation must fund any omitted requirements before assigning a financing surplus, while identifying overlaps with its existing allowances. The attributed 28.62 trillion U is value added, not park profit available to pay those bills or service debt.

## Tourism among three inhabited worlds

Travel is reciprocal among Earth, the Moon and the array. Lunar residents can visit Earth's landscapes, cultural institutions and established communities; Earth and Moon residents can visit array communities and orbital facilities; array residents can visit either planetary surface. The Moon's gardens, low-gravity recreation, inhabited sky and regional ecology give it distinctive offerings within that network. The present calculation represents all six directions without claiming that the assumed preferences have been established.

In the connected case, each world allocates 4% of its GWP to outbound travel. Earth assigns 75% of that budget to the Moon and 25% to the array; lunar residents assign 65% to Earth and 35% to the array; array residents assign 30% to Earth and 70% to the Moon. Destination stays are assumed to last 14, 21 and 10 Earth-equivalent nights on Earth, Moon and array, with destination spending of 350, 450 and 500 U per night. Access costs are 2,000 U for an Earth–Moon or Moon–array trip and 3,000 U for Earth–array. These prices, durations and allocations are scenario inputs.

| Connected-case journey | Realised trips per year, billion | Destination nights, billion | Total realised trip spending, trillion U |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Earth → Moon | 2.358 | 49.520 | 27.000 |
| Earth → array | 1.125 | 11.250 | 9.000 |
| Moon → Earth | 3.000 | 42.000 | 20.700 |
| Moon → array | 1.678 | 16.784 | 11.749 |
| Array → Earth | 0.759 | 10.633 | 6.000 |
| Array → Moon | 1.223 | 25.677 | 14.000 |

The assumed route limit of three billion annual trips binds for Moon→Earth, leaving **1.12 trillion U** of its allocated budget unspent, and that money stays with its residents, unspent. Annual destination-night limits also apply, and none binds in this case. On the Moon, **3.58 billion inbound trips** a year, about 9.8 million arrivals a day, imply an average of **206 million visitors present**, about **2.6%** of the resident population, while Earth's residents make 3.48 billion trips a year to the Moon and the array. These flows are set by each world's travel spending and are still to be sized against the transport system, the arrival ports and the register's transport rules (remote braking, capped packet energy and assigned corridors); every balance below rests on that sizing.

Tourism is identified by who consumes a service, so it cuts across industries already included in the economy. Routine local activity belongs in the local account; transport value added belongs where the transport producer operates. The model therefore assigns revenue among operators on all three worlds, including an assumed 60% array operator share on every interworld route. Those allocations describe production, not ownership of the resulting income. [ABS Tourism Satellite Account methodology, visitor definitions and relation to core national accounts](https://www.abs.gov.au/methodologies/tourism-satellite-account-methodology/2024-25).

## Visitor production and external balances

Large visitor production need not produce a large external surplus. Residents also travel outward, operators purchase imported inputs, and some transport services are supplied by another world. The connected Moon produces **24.23 trillion U** of direct tourism value added but receives only **0.24 trillion U** more than it pays through the model's tourism-related cross-border transactions. Local amenities introduce a further **9.42-trillion-U net import requirement** under the specified first-tier input shares.

| Scenario | Lunar direct tourism GVA, trillion U | Lunar tourism net external inflow, trillion U | Net inflow including local-amenity input trade, trillion U |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Balanced travel budgets | 8.57 | −4.54 | −13.96 |
| Connected travel | 24.23 | +0.24 | −9.17 |
| Strong lunar destination preference | 39.93 | +20.42 | +11.00 |
| Strong preference, doubled assumed capacities | 56.07 | +39.61 | +30.19 |

The balanced case gives each world a 2%-of-GWP outbound budget split equally between its two destinations. The strong lunar-destination case raises Earth's budget to 8% and the array's to 10%, keeps the Moon's at 4%, and directs 80% of both Earth and array outbound budgets toward the Moon. At the original route capacities, it delivers six billion lunar arrivals annually. Doubling both route and destination-night capacities permits **8.52 billion** arrivals and about **490 million** visitors present on average. Capacity expansion, demand and the required investment have not been validated.

Access cost also matters without a change in preference. Halving it in the connected case raises the Moon's tourism net inflow to **4.02 trillion U**; doubling it changes that balance to **−2.42 trillion U**. These outcomes reflect the specified budgets, trip prices, caps and operator shares, rather than a demand model that predicts how people respond to fares or congestion.

The external balances include visitor services, transport purchases and first-tier imported inputs, leaving further supply-chain imports, foreign profit transfers and a complete trade equilibrium for later. Each world's inflow is another world's outflow, so the balances close across the three-world system, and they sit inside GDP and apart from project revenues. Their financing role depends on the rest of trade, on who owns the income and on the share of saving the communities assign to construction, so the screen gives local amenities and reciprocal travel a substantial role whose contribution to construction finance turns on those arrangements.

The original patient-finance case requires 68.95 trillion U of annual lunar net exports. The strong lunar-destination scenarios supply about 30–57% of that requirement through tourism-related trade alone, or 16–44% after the modelled local-amenity input trade, and the connected case leaves a combined deficit; all three rest on the unsized travel flows above. These fractions compare with the original debt requirement, and the early contributions in [development.md](development.md) change the debt still outstanding. Visitor receipts and net trade flows reach construction only through the arrangements that direct them there.
