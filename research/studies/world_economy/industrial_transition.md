# Lunar industry through the atmospheric transition

Early lunar industry could leave an enduring productive inheritance: buildings and utilities on the Moon, equipment already working in orbit, and refined material available for later construction. Its value depends on what customers need, what competing producers charge, and how much of the original investment remains useful when atmospheric conditions change. The calculations here examine those dependencies through explicit industrial, financing and storage scenarios. They establish neither a future market nor a demonstrated lunar manufacturing advantage.

Loss of usable ambient vacuum introduces pumping and enclosure requirements; it does not mandate plant closure. The 50-year liquidation case below is a stress scenario. Continued operation, with explicit allowances for adaptation and later delivery, is a separate investment choice.

The [engineering assessment](industrial_engineering.md) develops the physical opportunities: large-workpiece processing in ambient vacuum, reduced self-weight, local feedstock and foundations, and later production inside buildings at 1–1.2 atm. It also examines Earth process controls and rotating orbital competitors. The economic comparison assigns no automatic cost reduction to lunar gravity. All monetary quantities use the study's common real unit U; billion and trillion refer to powers of ten, while Mt and Gt denote million and billion tonnes.

## Customers across the Solar System

The most plausible early bulk customers are lunar construction projects and nearby orbital infrastructure. A lunar supplier could deliver fabricated modules, protected fibres, tanks or machine components while those customers are installing capital. Orders subsequently shift toward maintenance, replacement and expansion. The reference array has a finite 75-Gt structural stock; its 4,950 Gt of passive shielding belongs to the separate bulk-material account. Treating all shielding as a market for specialized manufactured parts would greatly exaggerate demand.

Earth is a more demanding export destination because its manufacturers already serve a large local market. The present cost scenarios favour selected higher-value components over ordinary structural stock. Nearby array workshops and industrial hubs compete directly with lunar factories, and can retain ambient vacuum after the Moon acquires air. Lunar exports therefore have a potentially substantial construction interval without acquiring a permanent orbital monopoly.

Farther afield, a sensible division of work would supply Mars and major settlements with specialized machinery while developing local production of their bulk structures. Belt and outer-system sources would also compete for mineral and refined-material orders. Long routes can support valuable, compact components more readily than an unrestricted lunar bulk trade. These are design inferences about delivered costs; the numerical “colonies” buyer is only a hypothetical budget and route screen. It does not resolve a Solar System transport network, settlement sequence or regional industrial policy.

The buyer scenario uses Earth at 9 billion residents, an early array population of 1 billion and 300 million residents elsewhere. Their annual procurement budgets are respectively 90, 500 and 30 billion U. Array orders share an eligible 15-Gt portion of the final structural stock; colony orders share a separately assumed 4.5-Gt stock. These allocations preserve finite orders and spending power without treating future population as an immediate purchase commitment.

## Competitive costs and a reference factory

The factory basket comprises structural stock and welded modules, glass/ceramic products, and sealed-process specialty components. Their benchmark production costs are 5, 10 and 50 U/kg. The structural figure follows the existing array allowance; the other figures set hypothetical scenario scales. Each includes inputs, compensation, fixed operation and capital service. The comparison gives the Moon equal costs, a 10% reduction or a 25% reduction, and separately tests an orbital competitor achieving a 20% reduction. These differences are assumptions about complete production systems, not deductions from gravity or vacuum experiments.

A lunar offer must beat the cheaper delivered Earth or array alternative at equal quality. It then splits the calculated cost advantage equally between supplier and buyer. Under the 10% lunar reduction, structural modules cost 4.50 U/kg before delivery. Adding 0.25 U/kg to the array produces a 4.75-U/kg cost against the competitor's 5.05; the offered price is 4.90. Delivery to Earth instead raises lunar cost to 5.50, above Earth's 5.05. This example shows why a factory can win one destination and lose another.

The reference plant has 100 Mt/year of raw nameplate throughput. At 70% utilization and the specified product yields, it supplies 63.7 Mt/year of finished goods. Its assumed 0.5-TW electricity allocation and 70-Mt/year outbound route exceed the calculated requirements; neither establishes a qualified power or launch system. Production also consumes a finite, synthetic local-feed allowance. The reference sells 3.185 Gt over fifty years.

Development cash also pays compensation, replacement reserves, debt service and any closure escrow, then assigns half the positive residual. The initial loan amortizes over 100 years at 0.5% real interest; a required equity dividend remains unpriced. These different measures cannot substitute for one another.

The tenfold expansion beyond the reference factory buys little extra sales volume because competitive orders and budgets bind. Its assumed 38.27-trillion-U capital stock still requires operation, replacement funding and loan payments. More capacity therefore reduces value added and creates a large cash deficit. Conversely, the stronger cost advantage lets the supplier charge less while retaining more value added and cash. These comparisons size an explicitly chosen market; larger infrastructure programmes would need separately funded orders before supporting larger factories.

## Continued operation and investment recovery

The continued-operation comparison retains the same factory and economic history through year 50, then continues to year 100 with 63.7 Mt/year of sales. It includes the source model's ordinary renewals at years 40 and 80, finite feed and customer allocations, and terminal recovery at year 100. Year 100 is a comparison horizon with the source model's terminal treatment, not a pressure-predicted shutdown date. Before extra transition costs, factory investment NPV is **+633.55 billion U**, compared with **−588.73 billion U** for liquidation at year 50. Continued output at the original prices remains conditional; this comparison does not establish that atmospheric upgrades and export routes preserve those sales.

The discounted difference between the two source cases already includes the forgone year-50 liquidation proceeds. Its value at year 50 is **2.010 trillion U**. That is the combined year-50 present-value allowance for additional retrofit, operation and delivery costs before continuation becomes worse than closure. Recovering the original factory investment at the stipulated 1% real rate imposes a stricter allowance of **1.042 trillion U** at that date. The distinction matters because continued operation can improve an already-built plant's outcome while leaving its original investment unattractive.

An illustrative sensitivity charges a retrofit at the end of year 50 and additional annual costs at the ends of years 51–100. The annual stream must cover all incremental operating, maintenance, renewal, lost-production and delivery obligations outside the initial retrofit; the retrofit receives no added terminal credit. These are cost allowances, with no relation to an inferred pressure-to-price multiplier.

| Retrofit at year 50, trillion U | Extra annual cost, billion U | Factory investment NPV over 100 years, trillion U | Incremental value over closure, at year 50, trillion U |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 | +0.634 | +2.010 |
| 0.10 | 5 | +0.454 | +1.714 |
| 0.50 | 10 | +0.091 | +1.118 |
| 1.00 | 25 | −0.570 | +0.030 |

With no retrofit outlay, additional annual costs can reach **51.29 billion U/year** before continuation loses to closure, or **26.58 billion U/year** before the original investment NPV becomes negative. At the assumed sales volume these correspond to **0.805 and 0.417 U/kg**. An investment assessment must compare actual upgrade costs alongside changes in achievable prices, customer retention, throughput and freight. Neither allowance demonstrates liquidity or supplies a loan contract; the earlier financing and independent-receipts accounts require revision before treating a continuation surplus as spendable development cash.

The [continuation model](industrial_continuation.py) reads the unchanged source products and compares these choices explicitly. Pressure can trigger process upgrades, and scheduled replacement can incorporate them. No global pressure threshold is assigned as a mandatory closure date. The remaining liquidation sensitivities describe the consequences if continued operation or adaptation is uneconomic.

The negative liquidation-case NPV is material even though operations generate positive cash. In that stress scenario, capital recovery is assessed over a finite operating interval: the plant is replaced at year 40, then closes at year 50 with three-quarters of the replacement plant's book value remaining. The reference recovers only 30% of that remaining value. Annual replacement reserves and loan payments serve different obligations; project NPV instead deducts initial investment and actual replacement expenditures, excluding transfers into reserves and financing flows.

| 0 | −1.112 |

The investment breaks even at approximately **63.7% terminal recovery**, with all other reference assumptions fixed. Continued production, relocation or productive reuse could preserve more value than low-price liquidation, but require an actual use and transition budget. The closure cash account treats recovery as monetized proceeds. Retained machinery's continuation value cannot also repay the closing loan without sale, refinancing or another cash source.

Operating duration changes the result as well. Under the 10% lunar cost reduction, the 10-, 50- and 100-year cases produce NPVs of −1.828, −0.589 and +0.634 trillion U. With a 25% reduction, they produce −1.124, +1.162 and +3.185 trillion U. These are conditional operating durations, with the source cases omitting added atmospheric-transition costs. A 100-year factory life does not require 100 years of ambient process vacuum. These factory-level returns exclude the programme-wide costs of delaying atmospheric release, any interim gas storage and the additional years of settlement enclosure. At cost parity, or when the orbital competitor learns sufficiently faster, the model wins no orders. A commissioned plant then incurs losses; those cases provide a reason to withhold investment until customers and competitiveness are established.

A separate final-atmosphere cost comparison adds hypothetical enclosure and delivery costs. It supplies a later endpoint sensitivity, not a tariff change at the first loss of ambient process vacuum. In the 10% advantage case, structural exports to the array rise to 7 U/kg against the competitor's 5.05 and lose their advantage. Sealed specialty components retain a 47.10-U/kg delivered cost against 50.05. Their remaining 2.95-U/kg margin of cost advantage follows the assumed production costs; it establishes a tolerance for transition expenditure, not a demonstrated low-gravity premium or a financed retrofit.

## Programme procurement and independent receipts

The reference allocates capacity to the most profitable available orders. Almost all output goes to the array: about 63.30 Mt/year, compared with 0.40 Mt/year to colonies and none to Earth. Some Earth orders are competitive, but higher-margin array orders take the available product capacity. Larger production receipts therefore need not produce larger independent funding for lunar development.

Array construction procurement already belongs to the joint programme's expenditure. Its payments create real lunar production and supplier income; passing the same programme loan through a factory does not create another independent financing source. The model consequently assigns those purchases an independent-funding fraction of zero. Earth and colony budgets receive a fraction of one as an explicit scenario assumption, and positive cash is attributed proportionally to receipt provenance.

An alternative prioritizes independent buyers while retaining the same budgets, orders and capacity. Assigned independent cash rises from **0.122 to 1.746 billion U/year**, while total assigned cash falls from 7.993 to 7.328 billion U/year. Project NPV also falls, from −589 to −641 billion U. This trade-off separates the supplier's commercial objective from the programme's funding objective. Neither cash figure is a net gain after the initial 1.913-trillion-U equity contribution.

Local construction offers another benefit. For each 1 trillion U of programme expenditure, making 10–30% of its packages eligible for a 10–30% saving yields **10–90 billion U of gross procurement savings**. This normalized calculation precedes enclosure, adaptation and transition costs. Savings could reduce the required construction budget even without external sales, but the avoided import bill cannot also be added wholesale to GDP.

## Material reserves after the factories change

Refined feed, standardized parts and technology-specific hardware have different storage prospects. A metal or purified compound may retain useful chemistry after a finished mechanism loses compatibility with current designs. The reserve model therefore separates physical attrition from compatibility loss: incompatible material remains physically present, with any recovery process unpriced.

Every case serves the same fifty years of demand. Reserves covering zero, five, twenty or fifty years are purchased initially; later purchases cover shortages or the remaining years. The normalized acquisition cost is 1 U/kg, early positioning costs 0.1 U/kg, annual storage costs 0.002 U per opening physical kilogram, and physical attrition is 0.05% annually. These values are assumptions, including the normalization; they are not quotations for lunar film feed.

At a constant alternative delivered price of 1.5 U/kg and a 0.5% real discount rate, purchasing one unit each year costs 66.214 U in present value. The least-cost tested reserve depends on assumed compatibility decay:

| Reserve class and annual compatibility loss | Least-cost tested reserve | PV saving against later purchases, U |
|---|---:|---:|
| Durable feed: 0 | 50 years | 8.143 |
| Modular parts: 0.5% | 20 years | 4.656 |
| Rapidly changing hardware: 5% | 5 years | 0.843 |

A fifty-year reserve of the rapidly changing hardware instead costs 287.859 U, leaving substantial incompatible material. Future competition also changes the choice. With durable feed, a 1% annual price decline reduces the preferred tested reserve to twenty years at the reference discount rate; a 3% decline reduces it to five. A 1% discount rate also favours twenty years even with constant prices. At an alternative price of 1 U/kg, immediate stock accumulation loses to later purchases under the reference assumptions. Supply-interruption insurance is not valued.

The full sensitivity grid tests alternative prices of 1, 1.5 and 2 U/kg, annual real price declines of 0%, 1% and 3%, and discount rates of 0.5%, 1% and 2%. Each selects among the same four reserve lengths, rather than claiming an unrestricted optimum.

The film-renewal account gives a physical application. Gross renewal of 2.309–4.638 Gt/year with fresh fractions of 0.1–1% requires approximately **0.117–2.349 Gt initially** to cover fifty years after the assumed attrition. That mass equals **1.84–36.87 years** of the reference plant's 63.7-Mt/year finished output. This is only a mass comparison: the plant is already selling its output, and matching the feed's composition, purity and recovery loop requires separate qualification and capacity.

An orbital reserve must be exported before its launch route becomes materially more costly if it is to avoid that later transport burden. The relevant threshold depends on the launch design and rising pressure, rather than the first retained air. A lunar warehouse can serve local users, but cannot inherit the old airless delivery tariff for later exports. Early positioning, protected storage and financing all consume resources. Unsold terminal stock receives no liquidation credit in the PV comparison; its reported replacement-price reference value remains unrealized.

Production enters the economic accounts when goods are made, including additions to inventory. Their later sale draws down that inventory, so it does not count the original manufacture again. New transport, distribution or processing can add value, while changes in stored goods' prices are holding gains or losses. [BEA's inventory accounting, chapter 7, pp. 7-1–7-4](https://www.bea.gov/resources/methodologies/nipa-handbook/pdf/chapter-07.pdf). A reserve can thus provide later cash while consuming an asset produced earlier.

## Operating assets that outlast lunar production

A complete facility already operating in orbit offers a different inheritance. Per 1 trillion U of installed capital, an assumed 6% annual gross operating surplus after current expenses provides 60 billion U. Funding replacement over a 50- or 100-year useful life leaves respectively **40 or 50 billion U/year before financing**. Customers, complementary equipment and those service returns are assumptions; loose stock or unassembled components cannot earn the facility's return by themselves.

Lunar ownership could preserve resident income after a surface factory closes, while the operating facility's production remains attributed to its orbital territory. Obtaining that ownership requires equity funding or accepting shares instead of cash sales. The same equipment cannot simultaneously supply full sale proceeds and remain wholly owned by its seller, and its later services cannot be added again to an existing mature-economy estimate.

The enduring opportunity therefore lies in useful assets and avoided future work: qualified material, installed utilities, productive orbital capital, and lunar buildings or machinery that can be adapted at reasonable cost. The calculations favour patient investment only where delivered competitiveness, orders and retained value support it. They remain alternatives or detailed components of the earlier mining, factory, construction and income scenarios. Combining them requires removing overlapping production, capital and programme procurement first.

Numerical closure verifies the stated accounts and assumptions; product qualification, future demand, transport designs and empirical cost advantages remain separate work.
