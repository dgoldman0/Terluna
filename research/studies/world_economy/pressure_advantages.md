# Uses for the 1.2-atmosphere environment

The 1.2 atm atmosphere provides useful services as well as imposing pressure loads: it supports more buoyant mass in a given volume, carries heat in less volume flow, suppresses boiling and cavitation, and supplies greater force to vacuum-assisted tools. These benefits are distributed across transport, buildings, water systems and manufacturing. The most extensive economic opportunity is likely to be better operation of domestic infrastructure; selected products may also become cheaper to manufacture.

This companion to the [mature-industry assessment](mature_industry.md) isolates **1.2 versus 1 atm at matched temperature, composition and gravity** wherever possible. It retains the 301 K environmental comparison and the 1.2 atm lock. A separate composition check uses the design's 17.5% oxygen against a rounded 21% reference: both give the same dry oxygen partial pressure. Pressure-only gains must not become another credit for low gravity or for oxygen enrichment. The [runner](pressure_advantages.py), [inputs](pressure_advantages_inputs.json), [results](results/pressure_advantages.json) and [source ledger](pressure_advantages_sources.json) distinguish calculations from qualified equipment and market estimates.

## Aerial infrastructure has the broadest direct opportunity

At fixed gas composition and temperature, density rises in proportion to absolute pressure. The supported mass of an ideal buoyant envelope is `(air density − lifting-gas density) × volume`; gravity cancels because both buoyant force and weight contain it. Raising pressure from 1 to 1.2 atm therefore gives **20% more gross buoyant mass capacity per cubic metre**, with the lifting gas also at ambient pressure and otherwise matched conditions.

For the same supported mass, an envelope can instead use **16.7% less volume**. A geometrically similar envelope then has **11.4% less surface area**, potentially reducing membrane, coating and inspection requirements. These are alternative geometry comparisons, not simultaneous 20% payload and 16.7% volume gains. Envelope mass, reserve lift and attached equipment still determine useful payload. Existing aerial products already use the 1.2 atm atmosphere; this comparison identifies its contribution rather than increasing their recorded capacity. The required lifting-gas inventory does not automatically fall: the same pressure-volume product holds the same number of gas molecules at fixed temperature.

This makes the extra pressure relevant to cargo aerostats, floating platforms, sky gardens and gas-supported biological hosts. It supports the existing interest in large hosts as places for other life and human use, without establishing their structural capacity. A binary nitrogen/oxygen comparison that also changes the reference oxygen fraction gives a density rise of **19.4%**, close to the matched-composition 20%; humidity and actual temperature require local profiles.

Winged flight and rotorcraft gain a smaller increment on top of their separate low-gravity advantage. At fixed weight, wing area and lift coefficient, flight speed scales as density to the power −1/2. The pressure increment permits **8.7% lower speed**. Ideal induced hover power at fixed weight and rotor area falls by the same percentage. These calculations use the [NASA lift equation](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/lift-equation-2/) and [actuator-disk momentum balance](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/propeller-thrust/); they exclude blade profile losses and actual aircraft design. Drag and wind loads at unchanged speed also rise, so 8.7% is not a whole-flight energy saving.

The deep sky makes pressure margins spatially useful. In an ideal isothermal 301 K spherical lunar column, a 1.2-atm surface places the 1-atm pressure level about **9.84 km** above it. This illustrates how extra base pressure can extend a density-limited operating region. It is not a new climate sounding or an additional 9.84 km of habitable or ecologically productive sky; temperature, oxygen, light and winds retain their own limits.

## Air cooling can need less fan work

For the same heat carriage and air temperature rise, the required mass flow is fixed by `heat = mass flow × heat capacity × temperature rise`. Denser air supplies that mass in less volume. In the same duct, volume flow and velocity fall to 1/1.2 of their reference values. With unchanged viscosity, Reynolds number remains the same, supporting a matched resistance-coefficient comparison.

For pressure loss proportional to density times velocity squared, pressure loss also falls to 1/1.2. Fan power, volume flow times pressure loss divided by efficiency, consequently falls to **1/1.2² = 0.694**, a **30.6% saving**. The [DOE/AMCA fan-system guide](https://www1.eere.energy.gov/manufacturing/tech_assistance/pdfs/fan_sourcebook.pdf) describes the density corrections and system resistances that require this distinction between mass and volume flow.

The illustrative circuit transports 1 MW of heat with a 20 K air temperature rise, a 4 m² duct, resistance coefficient 12 and fan efficiency 65%. At matched gas properties, calculated fan demand falls from **50.9 to 35.4 kW**. Adding a prescribed 500 Pa pressure-loss floor changes the comparison to **83.5 versus 62.5 kW**, a 25.1% saving. If the duty instead requires the original volume flow, the resistance-dominated circuit needs **20% more fan power** while transporting more mass. A blanket fan-efficiency multiplier would confuse those duties.

Candidate uses include building ventilation sized by heat load, electronics cooling, industrial air heat exchangers and recirculating process air. Requirements set by contaminant capture, minimum face velocity, humidity or equipment clearance can prevent the volume-flow reduction. Heat-exchanger adequacy, refrigeration and heat rejection are separate. A 30.6% saving on fan electricity that constitutes 5% of a factory's total cost yields **1.53% overall cost saving**, before other differences; it is not a 30.6% saving on the factory or cooling plant.

Higher pressure also partly restores natural convection weakened by lunar gravity. Under matched continuum-gas properties, the pressure-only Rayleigh-number multiplier is 1.44. In regimes where heat transfer follows Rayleigh number to the power 1/4 or 1/3, this means roughly **9.5–12.9% more natural-convection heat transfer than on a 1-atm Moon**. The combined lunar-gravity/1.2-atm Rayleigh number remains only about 0.2385 of the matched Earth case. These regime-dependent scalings do not establish equipment temperatures or change the world's radiative heat budget.

## Liquid systems gain pressure margin and hotter open operation

The added **20.265 kPa** raises the liquid pressure above its vapor pressure, reducing the chance of vapor formation in a pump inlet or fast-flowing passage at otherwise identical conditions. Actual atmospheric pressure enters pump head and NPSH assessment, as the [KSB technical reference](https://www.ksb.com/en-global/centrifugal-pump-lexicon/article/atmospheric-pressure-1116788) explains.

At an assumed water density of 1,000 kg/m³, that increment equals **12.48 m of water column in lunar gravity**. At 301 K, the ideal suction-column ceiling rises from **60.1 to 72.6 m** on the Moon. The pressure margin is still only 20.265 kPa; the large height expresses low gravity, not a larger pressure benefit. Real installations must subtract flow losses, pump requirements and operating margin, and account for dissolved gas. The calculation supplies no recommended suction height.

This margin could simplify irrigation, raised gardens, water transfer, slurry handling and hot-water circuits. Longer suction lines or pumps farther above their source can be possible without another booster or a pressurized source vessel. It is not free lifting energy: the atmosphere acts on both ends of an ordinary open water system, and the pump still supplies the elevation and frictional work.

The same pressure increase raises pure water's open boiling point from **99.97°C to 105.16°C**, calculated with the existing atmosphere-domain saturation law and checked against the [IAPWS reference equation and table](https://iapws.org/technical-guidance/release/Supp-sat). Hot washing, extraction, cooking and aqueous processing can use a modestly higher temperature without adding a pressure enclosure. Higher boiling temperature does not reduce the heat needed to reach it, and no sterilization or recipe-performance claim follows from this calculation.

## Vacuum-assisted fabrication can benefit from the extra pressure

Vacuum bagging uses the atmosphere to clamp a laminate, as described in the [WEST SYSTEM manufacturing guidance](https://www.westsystem.com/instruction/epoxy-basics/vacuum-bagging/). Suction grippers, vacuum chucks and atmospheric-pressure forming use the same pressure-difference principle. With a bag at 10 kPa absolute, the available differential rises from **91.3 to 111.6 kPa**. The increment is 20.3 kN per square metre of effective area. Suitable large panels, curved skins, bonded cores and repair laminates could gain more consolidation or use smaller holding areas.

Another option is to keep the required clamping force fixed and relax the vacuum. An 80 kPa clamping requirement permits **21.3 kPa absolute inside the tool at 1 atm**, but **41.6 kPa at 1.2 atm**. In the illustrative rigid-volume, constant-pumping-speed calculation, this reduces pumpdown time by **31.2%** and reversible evacuation work by **24.3%**. These are no-load calculations for a prescribed pressure difference, not measured pump consumption; leakage, resin vapors, permeability, deformation and equipment curves remain relevant.

This is a different service from a process that needs a particular low absolute pressure. For the same absolute vacuum, the earlier extra pumping and enclosure loads remain. For clamping, the desired service is the force difference, so higher atmospheric pressure can reduce the required vacuum severity. More pressure is useful only where the material and tooling benefit: excessive compaction can crush cores or alter resin flow, and some chemistry still requires low absolute pressure for gas removal.

This provides an enduring manufacturing opportunity after atmospheric loading. A factory might use controlled vacuum for preparation and atmospheric clamping for assembly or repair. Conversion costs and qualified orders would be needed before assigning new income to an earlier industrial campaign.

## Smaller gains and the limits of composition

Gas compression benefits when the process specifies a fixed **absolute** delivery pressure. For the same gas, molar output and inlet temperature, reversible isothermal work is `R T ln(outlet pressure / inlet pressure)`. Raising inlet pressure from 1 to 1.2 atm lowers that work by **26.8% to 2 bar absolute, 8.8% to 8 bar, and 4.0% to 100 bar**. Ideal isentropic work is a separate reference, calculated from the pressure ratio as in [NASA's compressor explanation](https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/airplane/compth.html). Actual machinery, separation and drying need their own accounts. Pneumatic equipment exhausting to the higher ambient also loses expansion work; a compressor saving alone does not establish a net cycle gain.

Denser ambient air may provide useful external electrical-insulation margin. The [Schneider Electric technical note](https://www.se.com/ie/en/faqs/FA239957/) distinguishes external air insulation from sealed internal insulation. A lunar rating would require the actual gas composition, humidity, gap geometry and waveform; no 20% voltage rating or automatic spacing reduction is assigned. Wind generation has 20% more incident power at unchanged wind speed and swept area, accompanied by greater loads. Wind climate and machine limits prevent treating that as a generation forecast.

Oxygen-dependent industries receive **no automatic 20% oxygen bonus**. At the stated dry compositions, 17.5% of 1.2 atm equals 21% of 1 atm. Dissolved oxygen depends on oxygen partial pressure, temperature and salinity, as the [USGS primary guidance](https://pubs.usgs.gov/twri/twri9a6/twri9a62/twri9a6_6.2_ver3.pdf) states. Including water vapor at a saturated 301 K interface changes this ideal comparison by only about 0.6%, before gas-transfer and biological effects. Fermentation and aquaculture still need a full oxygen supply and transfer analysis. Equal oxygen partial pressure does not, however, make fire behavior identical.

Additional nitrogen supplies a plausible **material-flammability benefit** while oxygen partial pressure stays near Earth's. More diluent shares the released heat and can inhibit flame propagation for particular materials. [NASA's configuration guidance](https://standards.nasa.gov/system/files/tmp/JSC%2029353D%20Flammability%20Configuration%20Analysis%20for%20Spacecraft%20Applications.pdf) explicitly distinguishes oxygen and diluent partial pressures. This merits testing for cables, furnishings, repair polymers and other exposed materials. The outcome depends on fuel and flow: [McAllister et al.'s PMMA experiments](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/35607) found nonmonotonic ignition delays across sub-atmospheric conditions. Neither source establishes the benefit at 1.2 atm and lunar gravity. The [industrial-combustion assessment](industrial_combustion.md) examines this opportunity specifically for production. Its expanded source review includes material propagation tests near 1.2 atm, while distinguishing the separate effects of gravity, forced flow and assembly configuration. It audits the [port fire study](../port_fire/README.md) and quantifies oxygen-feed, exhaust and purge differences; no general fire-loss allowance is reduced.

At unchanged 400 ppm CO2, the partial pressure rises from 40.5 to 48.6 Pa. That effect already appears in the [canopy study](../../../biosphere/canopy/README.md), alongside the limitation from slower gas diffusion. Its older leaf-temperature and light assumptions are not a new 301 K crop result. Existing diffuse-light and ecological benefits must likewise be retained in their own accounts, rather than counted again here.

The practical priorities are aerial carriers and platforms, heat-limited air circuits, liquid-handling layouts, and selected vacuum-assisted fabrication. Earth and the array can reproduce higher pressure in enclosed equipment; the Open Moon makes it available across open infrastructure and large working areas. Those recurring local services can reduce particular operating or capital costs. Their value must be assessed alongside the atmosphere's already-accounted construction and sustaining costs, with no additional GWP or campaign revenue assigned by this study.
