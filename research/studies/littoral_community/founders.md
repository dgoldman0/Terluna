# Founders for the freshwater littoral: which, why and where

9 November 2026, on domain/ecology. A bounded literature review, sized to one question: which Earth species could found a self-sustaining community in the Open Moon's fresh shallows, what makes each easy to select, and where each belongs. The working direction for founders is that they are aggressive colonisers, since they enter waters with no life in them: microbes, algae and water plants go first, crayfish follow once those hold, and predators of the crayfish follow them, with finer shaping and designed organisms coming as the communities establish and diversify. The review tests that direction against Earth's record and against the shore's own conditions ([README](README.md#the-shores-water-through-the-lunar-month)).

Its evidence is 44 literature searches of 9 November and two papers whose own pages were fetched for their numbers, marked *read*. [sources.json](sources.json) lists every source with how far it was read: most are known through a search summary of the page, and a statement marked *recalled* comes from memory. The placements and the first community are proposals. The land founders raised in the same session, the nitrogen-fixing trees among them, are a companion review still to do.

## What the review finds

**Choose animals that already stand the shore's heat, and spend selection where it responds.** Upper heat tolerance barely moves under selection in a fish: zebrafish from wild stock, selected for six generations with a third kept each time, gained 0.04 °C a generation and 0.22 °C in all, with a realised heritability of 0.10, while the lines selected downward lost 0.74 °C; warm acclimation had given 1.3 °C at the start and gave a quarter less in the selected lines, and the authors read a hard ceiling in it (Morgan and others 2020, read). Other traits answer quickly. A green alga, *Chlorella vulgaris*, evolved tolerance of high temperature in about 100 generations, in an experiment running to 33 °C, by lowering its respiration relative to its photosynthesis, the same economy the long night asks for (Padfield and others 2016). *Daphnia magna* gained heat tolerance in a two-year selection experiment on 150 clones, and in its lake between the 1960s and the 2000s, read from hatched resting eggs (Geerts and others 2015). Growth in Nile tilapia has risen by 5–17% a generation in reports that span sixteen generations of the GIFT lines. In redclaw crayfish the heritability of harvest weight fell from 0.38 to 0.13 within four generations of a closed line, and its breeders advise crossing to outside stock (McPhee, Jones and others 2004). So the heat sorts the candidates first, and breeding then works on timing, dormancy, growth and the loss of toxins.

**The gentlest shores lie poleward, and the hardest are sheltered low-latitude shallows.** On the corrected design climate a 2 m sea shore trading three tenths of its water a day with the sea runs 27–36 °C at 8° and 30°, 28–33 °C at 47° and 28–31 °C at 64°, and its nights without photosynthetic light last 261, 244, 210 and 109 hours. A closed 1 m pan at 8° passes 40 °C, and a sheltered lake margin at 14° spends 36 hours of each lunar day above 36 °C. Light at the poleward shores, 9–18 mol m⁻² a day just below the surface, is two to fourteen times what stoneworts need at their depth limits ([underwater light](../underwater_light/README.md)), before the water above them takes its share. A first self-sustaining community is best tried on a sea shore between 45° and 65°, open to the sea, on a shelf 2 m and more below low water.

**The design water favours stonewort lakes' life over Earth's notorious invaders, after a first rich decade.** The held seas are hard, clear and poor in phosphorus: 34 mg/L of calcium, 1.68 meq/L of alkalinity, pH 8.3, and 0.3–1.1 mg/m³ of phosphorus from the rivers ([plan](../aquatic_biogeochemistry/plan.md#where-it-starts)). On Earth such water grows stonewort meadows and a food web fed from the floor: periphyton makes 80–98% of the primary production of shallow nutrient-poor lakes in Greenland, a share that falls as phosphorus rises (Vadeboncoeur and others 2003). Water hyacinth, salvinia, hydrilla, carp and the red swamp crayfish win in rich, disturbed water. New waters are rich for about a decade ([ecology, finding 17](../../../biosphere/ecology/README.md#waters)), and Lake Kariba shows the course: salvinia covered 22% of the new lake within four years of filling and fell back to 10–15% as the lake turned nutrient-poor (Marshall and Junor 1981). The founders therefore come in two sets, fast colonisers that use the rich first years and recede as the nutrients fall, and low-nutrient competitors that hold the mature water.

**Plants first, then crayfish, then their predators: Earth's lakes confirm the order and add two conditions.** The first is that the plants are established widely, and guarded, before crayfish arrive. In a restored Dutch lake, cages holding red swamp crayfish at 150 g/m² strongly reduced the biomass and survival of planted *Chara*, *Elodea* and *Myriophyllum* within six weeks, while fish and waterfowl added nothing to the loss (van der Wal and others 2013, read); in tank experiments one crayfish a square metre cost about 40% of the plants and three about 80%; Lake Chozas turned from clear to turbid after the crayfish came (Rodríguez and others 2003); and in Lake Naivasha crayfish and submerged plants have cycled against each other since 1970 (Harper and others 2002). Transplanted *Vallisneria* in the Brisbane River held only inside fences and vanished when they were removed. The second condition is that the predators come with the crayfish and are of the kind that takes them small, soft and in the dark. Eels take small and moulting crayfish from behind and cut the survivors' feeding (Aquiloni and others 2010), and eels near their carrying capacity collapsed crayfish populations in French ponds, short of removing them (Freshwater Biology 2015). A sight-hunting fish-eater does something else: forty-five years after peacock bass entered Lake Gatún, its small shore fishes stand 96% below their earlier numbers (Sharpe and others 2017).

**The night's tools already exist in the founders, and the cue is the breeding target.** Water plants and small animals carry dormant stores: duckweed turions, stonewort bulbils and oospores, hydrilla tubers, the resting eggs of water fleas. Bulbils of the stonewort *Nitellopsis obtusa* hold 42–55% starch against 3–10% in its shoots; water plants' turions respire a half to a quarter as fast as their shoots; and 27 clones of the greater duckweed kept their different turion yields through years of culture, a heritable difference (Kuehdorf and others 2014). On Earth these stores answer short days, cold and scarcity: hydrilla forms tubers under short days and low nutrients. On the Moon they have to answer the lunar dusk, the dusk-set timer of the [lunar-cycle register](../lunar_cycle_ecology/README.md#c-clocks-and-cues). Algae differ widely in the dark: a green alga and a diatom kept their biomass through 20 dark days while a bloom-forming cyanobacterium fell to 1% of its cells, and warmth shortens dark survival. No measurement was found of any stonewort through 110–260 warm dark hours, which makes that the first experiment.

**Animals meet a gentler night than plants do.** Near-side nights keep half a lux to about three lux of Earthlight on the water, and most far-side nights keep tenths of a lux of twilight ([lunar-cycle ecology](../lunar_cycle_ecology/README.md#the-setting)); fishes feed by sight down to 0.01–1 lux, and full Earth carries 0.1 lux tens of metres into clear water ([underwater light](../underwater_light/README.md)). Sight-feeders can work the shallows through most nights, the dark nights near the far side's equator excepted, and eels, catfish and crayfish hunt by smell and touch everywhere (*recalled*). The long day does not stop the best-studied founder fish from breeding: Nile tilapia under continuous light spawned at short intervals with the second-highest fecundity of four light regimes (Campos-Mendoza and others 2004). Breeding through ten dark days is untested in every animal listed here.

**Found each species from many sources and let the stocks mix; keep one clonal workhorse.** The brown anole reached Florida in at least eight introductions and holds more genetic variation there than at home (Kolbe and others 2004), and across invasions several introductions go with more variation (Dlugosch and Parker 2008). Mixing also feeds diversification: the roughly 700 cichlid species of Lake Victoria's region descend from a hybrid swarm of two lineages (Meier and others 2017), and in the crater lake Apoyo a new open-water cichlid arose within about 10,000 years (Barluenga and others 2006; the sympatric reading is disputed). With some 33,000 rain-fed lakes, many of them in craters, mixed cichlid stocks are the natural diversifiers. The opposite founder is the marbled crayfish, a single all-female clone that spread through Madagascar from 1,000 km² to over 100,000 km² in ten years and is sold there as food (Gutekunst and others 2018): it establishes from one animal and stays uniform, and selection has almost nothing to work on in it.

**Aggressive founders bring four costs to design around.** Redclaw crayfish in Lake Kariba spoil 212 t of netted fish a year (a study of its gillnet fishery; White Rose eprint 198654). The snail *Melanoides tuberculata* is first host to at least eleven flukes that infect people, two of them caught from raw fish, and carried such flukes at 19 of 41 fishing sites surveyed in southern California (a preprint); with raw fish on the table and organisms crossing between the worlds ([register, 6 November](../../decisions.md)), the snails are founded from parasite-free lines and the choice of snail is weighed. A top fish-eater strips the small fishes, as at Gatún. The golden mussel fouls water intakes. Aggression within a habitat sits inside the rule that a wild design holds no advantage everywhere ([register, 12 October](../../decisions.md#machines-in-the-living-world-12-october-2026)): the heat limits, the soda lakes' chemistry, depth and drying bound every species here to part of the Moon's waters.

## A first community to test

A proposal for the milestone of a community that holds without restocking, at a sea shore between 45° and 65°.

1. **Microbes and algae**, with a nitrogen-fixer free of toxin genes: until the floor carries a mat and the water its plankton.
2. **Stoneworts of two kinds, greater duckweed, and azolla for the rich years**: until meadows cover the shelf, with the plantings fenced.
3. **Water fleas, bladder snails, then jade perch and tilapia**: the grazers and omnivores, each through several generations.
4. **Redclaw together with its predators**, spangled perch breeding on site and eels stocked: crayfish enter only extensive meadows, and the fences stay until the meadows hold with crayfish present.
5. **Pond mussels and mixed cichlids**, once fish hosts and prey are plentiful.

It counts as self-sustaining when every member but the eels completes its life cycle in the water through many lunar days, the plan's test of lasting performance; the water at the end of the night keeps its oxygen; the meadows hold their cover with crayfish present; and the phosphorus taken out as harvest is returned.

## What is still unknown, by leverage

1. **Stoneworts at 30–35 °C and through 110–260 dark hours.** No measurement was found, and the plan's calcite rests on them.
2. **Sodium and chloride.** The held seas carry almost no chloride, and animals' body fluids are salt solutions. Fishes of the Rio Negro keep their salts in water holding 30 µmol/L or less of sodium and chloride, a trait of particular lineages (Wood and others 2003; Gonzalez, Wilson and Wood 2005); crayfish and molluscs are unchecked. The chloride left in the delivered water ([resources' question 17](../../../resources/questions.md)) sets the level the founders are screened at.
3. **Magnesium**, which the reference seas lack and all life needs: the plan's second step.
4. **Heat limits not retrieved:** the marbled crayfish, the pond mussel, adult catfish, the bladder snail above 27 °C, and a warm-water freshwater shrimp, since the cherry shrimp's broods turn 82% male at 26 °C.
5. **Breeding in the dark**, for every animal here.
6. **The shore's exchange with the sea and the monthly tide**, which set the heat the founders meet.
7. **Predators from the redclaw's home rivers**, and the midges and other insects: not covered by these searches.
