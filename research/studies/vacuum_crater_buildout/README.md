# Crater communities through vacuum and atmospheric transition

The initial community is a complete small garden town for approximately **500–1,000 residents beneath one contiguous dome**. Its terraces, homes, workplaces, lake, planted walks and shared spaces establish the life intended for the eventual open town. The enclosure makes that life possible during a potentially long vacuum period; it is maintained as the atmosphere develops and removed once a sustainable breathable environment is established. The study therefore considers the permanent town, its temporary enclosure and its connections to other communities together.

The [adopted programme](../../../habitation/crater_gardens/buildout.json) seeks the best 2.5% of all catalogued craters 200–1,000 m across for eventual communities, with an initial 0.1% of the same population. It permits existing technology and reasonably confident improvements already under development. The fractions, single-community dome and eventual removal are accepted directions. The ranking rules, structural properties, operational loads and transport cases below are explicit study assumptions. Subdivision remains a comparison for larger communities.

## Scale and the first site selections

The [preceding census](../crater_gardens/README.md) counted 8,886,558 craters in the programme's size range. Downward rounding gives **222,163 eventual sites and 8,886 initial sites**, with the initial set comprising approximately 4% of the eventual set. These counts describe a buildout programme, with no construction timetable attached.

Of the census's 902,291 candidates passing its depth, water and regional garden-climate cuts, 724,781 have high catalogue confidence and no future-water cell in the eight cells around their centres. The present screen ranks these by architectural bowl proportions and fits circular estates around them. It reaches the full eventual quota with estates 1.5, 2 or 3 times the crater diameter. This establishes numerical headroom under these proxies; local terrain, foundation engineering, conservation and access determine the usable sites.

The reference eventual set contains 68,385 craters at 200–300 m, 75,021 at 300–500 m and 78,757 at 500–1,000 m. Its spacious and fuller architectural cases contain 203.0 million and 870.1 million residents respectively. These populations come from summing each selected diameter's floor space. They do not allocate the Moon's population or demonstrate the services needed to occupy every site. Circular estates include outer gardens; freight corridors, farms, power receivers, radiators, heritage buffers and protected land require further allocation.

The existing representative floor-space assumptions translate 500–1,000 people into **237.9–336.4 m diameter**. The initial selection stays inside that interval and inside the eventual set. Two placement comparisons test the relationship between short spans and practical travel:

Both comparisons contain 8,886 single-dome communities, each within the 500–1,000-person representative range. The groups contain 8–64 communities in this run, with each member within 25 km of its group's selected seed. Several groups can lie near one another. Their grouping reduces local travel distances while accepting somewhat larger domes: the grouped case uses 238–336 m craters and the smallest-span case 238–245 m craters.

That trade also changes geographic concentration. The grouped case places most sites in the northern farside highlands in today's coordinate frame; the middle 80% of its latitudes runs from 11.7° to 40.3° north. Its ranking rewards existing concentrations of compatible small craters. It has no allocation across future regions, freight ports, resources or Earth-communication horizons. The comparison therefore supports examining regional development together with dome size; it does not adopt these particular groups as the settlement map.

## How the locations are compared

The eventual ranking prefers depth/diameter near 55/750, the midpoint of the representative town's 40–70 m relief divided by its 750 m diameter. Absolute deviations are grouped in increments of 0.005; a deterministic identifier hash orders ties. This is an architectural-fit preference. The catalogue's detection confidence measures recognition of a crater, while the rim profile, circularity, load-bearing rock and construction surfaces remain to be surveyed. For craters above 500 m, this proportional preference is applied within the previous census's absolute 40–70 m depth window. The original catalogue also contains rim-height and interior-slope columns; the preceding export does not carry them, and this ranking has not evaluated them. Admitting those measurements and reading the complete catalogue methods would refine the geometry screen before local survey.

The selector takes ranked candidates in order and rejects any whose proposed circular estate overlaps an already selected estate. At the reference estate diameter of twice the crater diameter, the separation between two centres must be at least the sum of their crater diameters. Distances use the shared lunar radius and spherical geometry, including the longitude seam. The greedy procedure is reproducible and provides one packing, with no claim to maximize the number of possible settlements.

For the grouped initial case, eligible craters are drawn from the eventual set using the representative population band. Seeds are ordered by the number of eligible neighbours within 25 km, then geometry and identifier. Each group retains its seed and up to 63 available neighbours, preferring smaller 25 m diameter bands, then geometry. A new group requires at least eight available candidates; the final group may be shortened to meet the quota. The alternative takes the globally smallest eligible craters. Any quota shortfall is retained in the result rather than filled from unsuitable sizes or excluded places.

Future water and climate enter both stages because the initial towns must later support domeless life. The common-water mask resolves about 7.6 km at the equator and the lake mask about 1.9 km, larger than a town. Avoiding water and adjacent cells is a conservative regional screen, with no measured freeboard. Local lake levels, rim elevations, groundwater, runoff and the delivery sequence of the waters remain decisive. Mean ground temperature and rain identify broad regions; they do not establish human comfort or the garden's water balance. The climatic fields are already expressed in today's coordinate frame, matching the catalogue; vacuum illumination must be assessed in the current orientation, while the later climate and illumination must follow the project's transformed orientation.

The [conservation study](../conservation/README.md) requires written evaluation and community judgement, alongside scientific preservation. Approved exclusion polygons are not supplied by its current coordinate register. The selection therefore precedes heritage clearance and the [distribution programme's](../distributions/README.md) spatial commitments to wild land and dark sky. Neither a dry centre nor a small total estate area establishes those conditions.

## One dome over a complete community

A 300 m example has about **795 residents** under the representative floor-space case. Its lake is approximately 150 m across under the existing area convention. Homes, work and services use about 55,700 m² of built floor; planted terraces, paths, water and public spaces occupy the shared landscape. The enclosure comparison gives its roof a rise of 60 m above the rim. That rise is a study geometry, and a measured profile will determine how it meets the buildings and terrain.

The proposed engineering arrangement keeps the whole civic landscape in one pressure volume. A reinforced cap, perimeter structure, sealed ground boundary and foundations must form a complete pressure-vessel load path. A frame or cable arrangement may support the continuous roof without dividing the community into separate inhabited enclosures. Permanent streets, utility access, landscape drainage and the lake shore are arranged first; replaceable pressure interfaces and lifting access allow the roof to be maintained and ultimately removed around them.

Everyday services must operate while that roof remains in place. Homes, communal rooms, education, basic clinical care, workshops and recreation belong within the community. Food production, larger repair works, bulk storage and power equipment can serve several nearby communities, with dependable pressurised access and emergency reserves. Pressure-capable refuge rooms inside ordinary buildings provide a place to shelter during an enclosure fault; their required capacity and independent endurance need sizing. These refuges are part of a single-dome town's services.

Light and shielding require a joined design. The heavy shielding comparisons below would cover a transparent roof, so their landscape would need collected sunlight, shielded optical paths and/or electrical illumination. A more transparent enclosure with different radiation protection requires its own lifetime dose calculation, including everyday exposure and refuges. The study preserves the shared garden community in both cases. It does not credit ordinary glazing with the protection of metres of soil or assign the artificial sky a completed lighting design.

## Pressure, materials and shielding

For roof radius at the rim a = D/2 and rise h, the spherical-cap curvature is R = (a² + h²)/(2h). Roof area is π(a² + h²), upward pressure force is Δpπa², and the ideal spherical membrane force per unit length is ΔpR/2. Dividing that force by an assumed effective allowable gives a material-equivalent thickness. The full rim reaction, ground pressure and the forces transmitted through the closed structure must be resolved by the foundation and reinforcement design.

The reference calculation uses **101.325 kPa internal pressure in vacuum**, **293.15 K**, roof rise **0.2D**, an assumed **400 MPa effective tensile allowable**, and material density **1,600 kg/m³**. These last two values describe a hypothetical effective reinforcement, with no selected product or qualified joint system. An additional 1.2-atmosphere case raises pressure loads and pressure-material quantities by 20%, allowing comparison with the established Open Moon atmosphere. Operational gas composition, oxygen partial pressure and humidity require their own specification.

| Single-dome example | Pressure uplift | Ideal equivalent reinforcement thickness | Gross air inventory | Shield mass at 3 m and 1,600 kg/m³ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 238 m, approximately 500 residents | 4.51 GN | 21.9 mm | 1,811 tonnes | 247,710 tonnes |
| 300 m, approximately 795 residents | 7.16 GN | 27.5 mm | 3,626 tonnes | 393,579 tonnes |
| 336 m, approximately 998 residents | 8.98 GN | 30.9 mm | 5,095 tonnes | 493,705 tonnes |

Air inventory includes the spherical cap and a paraboloid bowl below the rim, with depth/diameter 55/750 for this table. Buildings, lake water and fill displace some of this gross volume. The selected-site totals instead use each catalogue depth. Edge bending, seams, penetrations, damage, creep, thermal cycles, fatigue and buckling require explicit design beyond the ideal membrane equation. The assumed effective allowable represents strength after hypothetical design reductions; joint, ageing and safety reductions have yet to be qualified. The calculated thickness is an equivalent amount of tensile material; it is not a specification for a transparent pressure sheet.

Current high-performance fibres support investigating improvements in reinforcement, with a clear distinction between fibre performance and structural allowable. Toray's [T1100G data sheet](https://www.toraycma.com/wp-content/uploads/T1100G-Data-Sheet.pdf) gives 7,000 MPa fibre strength and 3,460 MPa directional composite tensile strength, but only 80 MPa transverse tensile strength, with the stated values supplied as reference averages. The study therefore compares effective biaxial allowables of **400, 600 and 800 MPa** at fixed assumed density. These yield ideal pressure-material mass factors of **1, 2/3 and 1/2**. The higher values are development sensitivities informed by component capability; their use in a full dome requires qualified laminate or tendon layouts, manufacturing, joints, ageing performance and tests.

NASA's [wireless strain-monitoring tests](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250007894/downloads/NASA-TM-20250007894_r5.pdf), [robotic caretaking programme](https://www.nasa.gov/integrated-system-for-autonomous-and-adaptive-caretaking-isaac/) and [local-resource construction development](https://www.nasa.gov/centers-and-facilities/marshall/nasa-icon-advance-lunar-construction-technology-for-moon-missions/) provide evidence for instrumented, serviceable and increasingly automated construction. These developments justify studying replaceable panels, accessible tendons, robotic inspection and locally made foundations and shielding. No century service life or numerical maintenance reduction follows from the inspected work.

For all 8,886 grouped initial communities, the reference whole-dome geometry contains **27.3 million tonnes of air** before displacement and **646 km² of curved roof**. Its equivalent pressure reinforcement is **27.3 million tonnes at 400 MPa**, falling to **18.2 million tonnes at 600 MPa** and **13.6 million tonnes at 800 MPa**. A uniform 3 m shield adds **3.10 billion tonnes**, or **7.24 billion tonnes at 7 m**. These are material quantities for the stated geometry, before frames, foundations, joints, transport and construction equipment. The zero-shield case is a mass reference and establishes no radiation protection.

The 3 m and 7 m comparisons follow the preliminary objectives examined in NASA's [Lunar Safe Haven study](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20210021007/downloads/LSH_Overview%20for%20Excavation%20%26%20Construction%20TIM_08-31-2021%20v5.pptx.pdf). Their effectiveness depends on composition, openings, geometry and the radiation environment. For the reference cap, a 3 m covering weighs only 8.93% of the one-atmosphere uplift under lunar gravity. Stronger reinforcement reduces pressure-material mass while shielding, foundations and the load path retain their separate requirements.

## Operation over a long vacuum period

A functioning community needs maintained air and water processing, food supply, heat rejection, power, repair stocks and emergency refuge. These services must work independently of a promised terraforming date. The calculation therefore compares **50, 200 and 500 years** as durations, without predicting when the atmosphere becomes usable.

At assumed annual air losses of 0.1%, 1% and 5%, the grouped initial set needs approximately **27,300, 273,000 or 1.37 million tonnes of replacement air per year**. These fractions are sensitivities; airlock operations, maintenance and accidents need additional accounts. At 1% annually over 200 years, replacement totals about 54.6 million tonnes. Replaceable components lasting 20, 50 or 100 years would undergo nine, three or one replacements before the end of that interval. An enclosure intended to last through the interval needs an accessible replacement system even when its primary foundations last much longer.

The basic service comparison assumes **2, 5 or 10 kW of electricity per resident**, giving **12.5, 31.3 or 62.7 GW** across the grouped set. These are load sensitivities before a complete equipment schedule and food-production account. Half a synodic month is 354.37 hours: the 5 kW case needs **11.1 TWh delivered** through that interval if generation is unavailable. With assumed 90% discharge efficiency, 80% usable storage and a 20% reserve, installed storage becomes **18.5 TWh**. Local horizon and season determine actual darkness.

The project's [beamed-power programme](../../../provisioning/energy_options.md) remains the bulk-supply design. Vacuum communities need commissioned receivers, local distribution and independently operable reserves before occupation. Solar, stored reactants and continuous generation can be compared for construction power and regional resilience. NASA's [fission programme](https://www.nasa.gov/exploration-systems-development-mission-directorate/fission-surface-power/) is a development precedent for sunlight-independent operation; it supplies no demonstrated town-scale lunar utility. The future atmosphere changes beam transmission, surface generation and reserve requirements, so each stage needs its own supply account.

Food is produced on the Moon under the existing programme. The [food-systems study](../food_systems/README.md) supplies two process-energy comparisons. Its power-made food development case adds **4.36–17.49 GW** for 6.27 million residents, bringing the 5 kW community-service case to **35.7–48.8 GW**. Its fully lamp-lit food case adds **32.6–332 GW**, bringing the same comparison to **63.9–364 GW**. The former still lacks measured complete routes for fats and the full range of plant compounds; neither comparison selects a diet. Vacuum agricultural structures, crop lighting schedules, process heat and thermal control require separate sizing. Collected sunlight and a mixed food system deserve direct assessment because all-electric crop lighting can exceed the communities' other loads.

At the reference 31.3 GW service load, an ideal shaded radiator at 330 K and emissivity 0.9 needs **51.8 km² of emitting area**, assuming that electrical load becomes heat rejected there. Solar absorption, warm terrain, pumps, food production and conversion losses can enlarge the required system. NASA's [radiator study](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240010276/downloads/Lunar_Latitude_and_Terrain_Radiator_Sensitivity_V2.pdf) shows why terrain view matters. Radiator access and clear views belong in the estate and regional utility design; later atmospheric convection supplies another heat-transfer path.

NASA's [ISS water-recovery result](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/station/nasa-achieves-water-recovery-milestone-on-international-space-station/) supports a 98% recovery comparison within its processing boundary. At an assumed 100 kg per resident per day of handled water, 2% makeup is about **12,537 tonnes per day** across the grouped initial population. That calculation excludes lake filling and losses, crop streams, construction water, exports and leakage outside the processed loop. The permanent landscape needs recoverable drainage, humidity control and water-quality management during enclosure, then a planned connection to the future catchment.

## Getting between communities without an atmosphere

Local travel can use pressurised passenger vehicles and autonomous freight carriers on surveyed, improved routes. Pressure docking allows residents to move between a vehicle and a community terminal without wearing a suit. NASA's [Space Exploration Vehicle concept](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/464826main_sev_factsheet_508.pdf) provides a precedent for docking, dust-limiting suitports and mutual rescue support, with a concept speed around 10 km/h. The study also compares 40 km/h improved roads and 150 km/h regional rail as advanced infrastructure assumptions. Track quality, traction, guidance, thermal movement, dust, braking, maintenance and rescue must support the chosen service speed.

The grouped case's median nearest neighbour is 2.32 km away and 90% have a neighbour within 5.02 km. These direct distances favour examining local roads and shared utility corridors before assigning fast rail everywhere. Busy links between groups can justify dedicated rail and larger freight equipment. Vehicles carry their pressure enclosure; a continuous pressure tube would be an additional construction choice. NASA's [FLOAT cargo-track project](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/stmd/niac/niac-studies/flexible-levitation-on-a-track-float/) is an early low-speed logistics precedent, with no demonstrated passenger-rail performance.

| Direct route length | 10 km/h rover comparison | 40 km/h road comparison | 150 km/h rail comparison |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 25 km | 2.50 h | 0.64 h | 0.22 h |
| 50 km | 5.00 h | 1.27 h | 0.39 h |
| 250 km | 25.00 h | 6.27 h | 1.72 h |
| 1,000 km | 100.00 h | 25.02 h | 6.72 h |

These kinematic times include ideal acceleration and braking at 0.2 m/s², with no stops, docking, gradients, detours or traffic. At 150 km/h, braking alone requires approximately 4.34 km under that assumed deceleration. The operational network needs rescue vehicles, communications, safe stopping and refuges whose endurance covers an interruption, not merely nominal journey time. A paired route or spare vehicle must remain available when the primary connection is unavailable.

The minimum connecting trees provide geographical comparisons: 39,468 km for the grouped initial set and 223,308 km for the scattered smallest-span set. They have no redundant links and do not include terrain detours. At a 25 km maximum link length, the grouped sites form 36 geographical components; the smallest-span sites form 3,507, with 2,175 isolated sites. The two placements also differ in population and geographical coverage, so their network-length difference compares whole siting policies rather than isolating a saving at equal population and coverage. Local grouping offers closer mutual support, while longer connections and regional supply still need engineering.

Dry endpoints do not ensure a dry future route. Sampling the connecting trees at intervals of at most 1 km flags **40 grouped-case links**, totalling **6,244 km**, that intersect the coarse future-water mask, compared with 2,125 links in the smallest-span comparison. Those lengths include entire flagged links, rather than only the portions over water. Removing flagged links leaves 55 components in the grouped 25 km tree, including five isolated sites; alternative dry links have not been searched. Future routes must follow terrain and lasting dry corridors or explicitly include bridges, tunnels or a later replacement. Communication relays and the ability to repair isolated links are especially important for the farside concentration.

Place stations, service ducts and utility corridors so the open town can keep using them. During the atmosphere's growth, upgrade dust management for moving air and precipitation, keep electrical and thermal systems weather-capable, and control runoff around roads and rail. After the communities open, pressure docking can be retired in stages while the transport vehicles and routes continue serving the same places. Atmospheric aircraft become an additional mode once the necessary air and operating conditions exist.

## Transition and removal

The enclosure must survive more than its full-vacuum load. As exterior pressure rises, outward membrane tension falls; wind, rain, wet surface loads and drainage demands appear. For a roof carrying the reference 3 m shielding, the net upward pressure force falls below shield weight when the pressure difference drops below about **9.04 kPa**. That point occurs before equal pressure. A separate structural load path must therefore support the roof and covering through low differential pressure and pressure loss. Yin's [preliminary habitat study](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19900015683/downloads/19900015683.pdf) examined support after deflation at approximately 16 m scale; the present spans require their own assessment.

| Operating condition | Required behaviour | What persists into the open town |
|---|---|---|
| Long vacuum operation | One complete pressure boundary, qualified protection, maintained air and water, night power, refuge and pressurised access | Buildings, streets, terraces, lake, service access and terminal |
| Atmosphere accumulating | Adjust internal pressure control and heat rejection; retain containment; provide drainage and assess wind, rain and zero or reverse pressure loads | Foundations and services designed for later weather |
| Sustainable breathable outdoor environment established | Verify local air quality and environmental protection, connect open drainage and utilities, transfer roof loads to dismantling supports | The occupied town continues functioning |
| Enclosure removal | Remove covering, pressure panels, temporary supports and perimeter interfaces in a planned sequence; recover materials | Domeless civic landscape, gardens, buildings and transport links |

An internal pressure held at 1 atmosphere against 1.2 atmospheres outside would impose 0.2 atmosphere inward. The transition must manage the internal pressure trajectory or qualify the structure for that load. Equal pressure alone does not establish breathable composition, acceptable contaminants or the required radiation, weather and water conditions. Removal follows those conditions, with no prescribed calendar date.

The permanent design also needs a dismantling route: lifting access outside the occupied walks, protected zones for moving roof material, redundant utility connections, drainage ready before the covering comes away, and a destination for recovered reinforcement and shielding. The study's intended end state removes the community enclosure. Long-term pressure capability survives only where a building or special service needs it.
