# Water from the air

**Mean rain meets the reference aerophyte's water need over half the Moon, equatorward of 35–38°; with cool leaves and a
CO₂-concentrating mechanism the need falls fivefold and rain meets it to 51°, and in humid layers to 65–69°.** The
reference transpires 1.25 kg/m² a day, as a cycle mean, to buy its carbon at 10 km in 60% air with leaves 5 K warmer
than the air. Leaves held at air temperature in the 64% air need 0.59 kg/m², a C4- or algal-style concentrating
mechanism that works at an internal CO₂ of 0.3 of the air's cuts that to 0.25, and the same plant in 90% air needs
0.07. Rain falls onto an aerophyte whatever the wind, and the design climate's zonal rain meets each need to the
latitudes in the table.

| Trait set at 10 km | Need, kg/m² a day | Rain meets it to | Share of the Moon |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Reference: 60% air, leaf 5 K warm, C3 (C_i/C_a 0.7) | 1.25 | 35° N, 38° S | 51% |
| Cool leaves in the 64% air | 0.59 | 42° N, 45° S | 63% |
| Cool leaves, humid 90% layer | 0.16 | 56° N, 55° S | 76% |
| CO₂-concentrating (C_i/C_a 0.3), cool, 64% air | 0.25 | 51° | 71% |
| Concentrating, humid 90% layer | 0.07 | 65° N, 69° S | 87% |
| Concentrating, near-saturated 98% layer | 0.014 | 86° | 100% |

Numbers are *screen* values (`water` in the product). The latitudes interpolate the zonal rain to the need; the
shares count the grid cells whose mean rain meets it. The 64% is the CM1 equatorial box's mean clear-air humidity at
10 km (60% at 20 km), which supports the reference's imposed 60%. Rain is counted at full catch and at the surface
rate; the ring's heaviest storm carried 1.9 times its surface rain flux at 10 km, so the surface rate is the
conservative choice.

## Rain: plentiful in the tropics, timed by the storms

**The design climate rains 2.24 mm a day over the Moon, 5.5 mm near the equator, 1.1 mm at 36° and under 0.15 mm
poleward of 55°.** These are the GCM's ten-year zonal means. Rain needs no airflow through the body, and an aerophyte's
upper surface is its catchment.

**Rain falls in the afternoon storm sector, from noon to 120° past noon: a third of the cycle.** An aerophyte crossing
that sector once per relative solar period meets rain every 15–30 days and goes 10–20 days without it: 10.2 days on
the 15-day cycle of the 20-km wind, 14 days on the 21-day cycle of the 10-km wind ([sailing](sailing.md)), and 20 days
at a fixed place. Its leaves transpire by day only, at twice the cycle mean, and the dry gap holds 3.8–7.4 days of
daylight, from sunrise to the next storms. Bridging the gap takes stored water: 9.6–18.5 kg/m² for the reference,
1.9–3.7 kg/m² with a concentrating mechanism in 64% air and 0.5–1.0 kg/m² with the mechanism in a humid layer. Night
cuticular loss adds a little to each.

## The trim rule: intake must track loss

**Spare superpressure of 300 Pa absorbs a water swing of 0.1 kg/m² in a 40-m body, 0.5 in a 200-m body and 4.8 in a
2-km body.** Losing water lightens a body; holding its height without venting gas needs the gas compressed or dense
air taken into a ballonet, both at a superpressure of Δp = ΔM p/(ρ V/A) per kg/m² of swing, where V/A is the gas
column per square metre. That is 3.4 kPa per kg/m² for a raft of 40-m modules (a 24.2 m column), 1.4 kPa for a 60-m
quilt, 624 Pa for a 200-m sphere and 62 Pa for a 2-km sphere. Venting instead costs 0.075 kg of hydrogen per kg of
water ([trim cycle](trim_cycle.md)), 104 W/m² of hydrogen output for a daily 1 kg/m² swing. The coupled round bodies'
hulls carry 300 Pa of spare superpressure for this and the colony modules' 100 Pa ([structure](structure.md)); the
spare pressure is a design guess, 100–1,000 Pa.

**So small and flat aerophytes must drink as they lose, and giants can drink between storms.** A raft or a 40-m body
must match intake to loss within the 0.03–0.3 kg/m² that 100–1,000 Pa of spare pressure absorbs (0.04 kg/m² for a
60-m colony module at its 100 Pa): within hours at the thrifty needs. A 2-km round body absorbs 4.8 kg/m² at its
300 Pa and 1.6–16 kg/m² across the range, days of a thrifty need, and can bank storm rain.

## Vapour

**A sorbent skin at water activity 0.3–0.5 takes 0.2–2.4 kg/m² a day from the 64% air at 10 km.** The uptake is
h_m(ρ_v,air − a_w ρ_sat(T_s)) with the mass-transfer coefficient h_c/(ρc_p Le^(2/3)) and a Lewis number of 0.85, and
the latent heat it releases warms the surface until convection and radiation carry it off: 0.8–4.4 K warmer at
convective coefficients of 2–10 W/m²/K. In 90% air the same skins take 0.6–4.2 kg/m² a day. One square metre of
sorbent per square metre of aerophyte meets the thrifty needs and, with good exchange, the reference need.

**Using vapour costs work or heat.** Moving water from 64% air into tissue at activity 0.99 takes at least 58 kJ/kg,
0.68 W/m² for each kg/m² a day; from 90% air, 0.15 W/m². Driving absorbed water out again with sunlight takes the
latent heat, about 28 W/m² per kg/m² a day, a small share of the 100–600 W/m² a sunlit surface absorbs. The cheap
design absorbs on shaded faces and regenerates in sunlit chambers.

**Animals already take vapour from unsaturated air, above about 80%.** The desert burrowing cockroach *Arenivaga*
gains water from air above 82.5% RH through bladder-like extensions of its hypopharynx (O'Donnell 1977), and tick
larvae hold their weight down to about 80–85% RH (Knülle 1966). The aerophyte's sorbent at 0.3–0.5 works well beyond
these thresholds, so it is an engineered trait with these as its biological precedents.

## Dew and radiative condensation

**Dew forms aloft only in humid layers.** A surface 0.5–2.8 K below the air, the depression that 5–20 W/m² of net
longwave loss holds against convection, gathers dew only above 83–97% RH, and at most 0.18–0.7 kg/m² a day (the
latent energy ceiling). The longwave loss is a stated range: the box's air above 10 km holds 113 kg/m² of vapour,
four and a half times Earth's whole column, so the sky seen from there is nearly opaque. Through the 354-hour night,
dew helps aerophytes resting in near-saturated layers and gives little in the 64% air.

## Cloud droplets

**Fine fibres catch cloud droplets when air moves through them: 0.1-mm fibres take 10–20 µm droplets at 0.3 m/s with
54–83% deposition efficiency.** Deposition on a long cylinder is St/(St + π/2) with St = 2ρ_w r²v/(9μR), the
Langmuir–Blodgett fit used for fog nets (Park et al. 2013); real collectors pass only part of the oncoming air, and
their overall efficiency stays near 20–30% (Rivera 2011). A whole body is too large to catch droplets, so collectors
are fringes and nets in the stream.

**A cloud of 0.1 g/m³ passing at 1 m/s through collectors of 30% overall efficiency gives 2.6 kg/m² a day while in
cloud.** The relative flow comes from sailing (0.4–0.8 m/s across the shear), from holding height in an updraft, or
from rising and sinking through a cloud layer. Cloud fills 2% of the air at 10 km on the cycle's average and 12% at
its afternoon peak (25° past noon), so an aerophyte that spends 5–20% of its time in cloud, seeking it out, gathers
0.1–0.5 kg/m² a day.

## Where water closes, and the tension with the Sun

**Rain is tropical while Sun-holding and twilight are polar, and two kinds of aerophyte answer it.**
- **Tropical rain-drinkers** live within 35–51° of the equator, where the zonal rain meets the reference or the
  concentrating need. They meet the storm sector every two to four weeks and need 0.5–19 kg/m² between storms. The
  concentrating trait's 1.9–3.7 kg/m² fits within a 2-km round body's 4.8 kg/m² of trim at 300 Pa, so the
  rain-drinking giants carry the concentrating trait and bank storm rain in their tall gas columns; the reference
  trait's 10–19 kg/m² is more than any hull's spare pressure holds. Colonies holding the storm sector's hours drink
  as it rains.
- **Polar vapour-gatherers** live poleward of 55°, where rain fails and the long twilight and the slow Sun are. They
  carry the concentrating trait, keep their leaves cool and their gas columns short, and drink continuously from
  sorbent skins and humid layers, which the trim rule requires of flat bodies anyway.
- **Migrants between them.** A wing on a tether sails an aerophyte 30–70° of latitude in a lunar day
  ([sailing](sailing.md)), enough to drink in the tropics by day and spend the dark half of its cycle in the polar
  twilight.

The other branch's wind product, resolved by latitude and solar hour, will place the rain sector and the humid
layers along real paths; the present map uses zonal means and one equatorial box and ring.

## Sources and checks

[water.py](water.py) holds the functions; [water_sources.json](water_sources.json) records Park et al. (2013),
Rivera (2011), O'Donnell (1977) and Knülle (1966) with what was read. Inputs are the
[design climatology](../../../climate/gcm/products/climatology_A28_dim5_moon.npz), the CM1
[box](../../../climate/results/crm/mixed_phase_box_0e.json) and
[equatorial ring](../../../climate/results/crm/ring_ring_equator.json) with its storm section, the sky-ship air and
the [sailing](sailing.md) component's noon-to-noon periods. [test_water.py](test_water.py) checks the vapour
density, the concentrating-mechanism factor, the GCM's Moon-mean rain, the closure interpolation, the daylight hours
of the dry gap, the trim rule and its inverse, the sorbent's heat balance, the work and dew limits and the deposition
fit. Independent read-only reviews on 9 October 2026 found the dry gap's period hard-coded, the stores charged for
night transpiration, the closures read at grid rows, the Lewis number taken as one and the rain sector's edges
counted two ways; this version corrects all five.
