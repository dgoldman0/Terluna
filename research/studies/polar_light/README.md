# Polar light on the turned Moon

**8 November 2026.** The water's load turns the Moon about 8°, so the ground about the turned poles is new polar ground whose light no product has yet computed. This study works out what light the polar regions receive from 60° to the poles in both hemispheres, for plants and for people. It also separates what the spin axis's tilt and its precession set from what the air, the clouds and the terrain set. It uses the turned Moon's frame (`geology/results/moon_orientation.json`), the solved 1.2-atm column through the light calendar's evaluator, the solved standard solution's spectral channels for photons, the turned run's cloud cover over years 10–49, and LOLA's terrain at 16 px/deg.

[run.py](run.py) writes [results/polar_light.json](results/polar_light.json) in about twelve minutes on four workers. The product's schema is `terluna.research.polar-light/1`, and it carries the producer's file hashes, every input's hash, the shared constants it read, its evidence statement and its reading rule. Every number below is computed by it unless marked *source*, a value taken from the literature or another product. Light is on level, open ground under a clear sky unless a finding names the run's clouds or the terrain. The tilt is 1.613°, the design state with the seas and lakes, unless 2.5° is named, and the precession's phase is the run's, with the south's midsummer at perihelion. A lunar day is 708.7 hours. Daily light integrals (DLI) are the lunar day's photosynthetic photons (400–700 nm) per 24 hours.

## What it finds

1. **Twilight in the tall air keeps the polar ground lit through every lunar day.** The solved column's twilight carries far past the terminator. With the Sun 10° below the horizon, open ground still receives 1,194 lux and 20.6 µmol/m²/s of photosynthetic light; at 20° below, 181 lux and 3.1 µmol/m²/s; at 30° below, 25 lux and 0.42 µmol/m²/s. Over the polar caps the Sun never sinks far: at local midnight it stands as far below the horizon as the colatitude, less the Sun's declination toward that pole. The year's darkest moment on open ground is 19 lux at 60° N, 50 lux at 65°, 136 at 70°, 368 at 75°, 937 at 80°, 2,100 at 85° and 4,035 at the north pole. The south pole's is 3,780 lux, since its winter falls at aphelion in the run's phase. Clear-sky ground never falls below the plant model's dark of 1 µmol/m²/s poleward of 66° N and 67° S, and never below the 8 lux of reading light anywhere poleward of 60°.

2. **The Sun circles low, and only within 1.6° of the poles does it stay up or down for a whole lunar day.** At 60° the Sun's centre is above the horizon for 343–365 of the lunar day's 708.7 hours and climbs to 28.4–31.6°. At 70° it is up 337–372 hours and climbs to 18.4–21.6°; at 80°, 318–390 hours and 8.4–11.6°; at 85°, 281–427 hours and 3.4–6.6°. Hours and heights follow the Sun's declination through the eclipse year of 346.6 days, and the hours' swing grows toward the poles. Polar day and polar night hold poleward of 88.4°; at the poles the Sun circles at its declination, between 1.6° up and 1.6° down. At a tilt of 2.5° the swing widens to 337–371 hours at 60° and 298–410 at 80°, and polar day and night reach to 87.5°.

3. **Refraction in the tall air is small, and near the poles it still adds days.** The design column bends a horizontal ray by 0.112° (6.7′), a fifth of the 0.569° (34′) the same integral gives for Earth's column. The bending comes from the vertical gradient of refractivity along the ray. The Moon's air is denser at the ground, but its density falls off over a 61-km scale height, eight times Earth's, and on a small body the ray soon climbs out of the dense air; for an exponential atmosphere the horizontal refraction scales as (n₀ − 1)√(πr/2H), which gives the Moon a fifth of Earth's. Counting the Sun's upper limb, refraction adds 0.5–1 hour a lunar day at 60°, 1–1.5 hours at 70°, 2.5 at 80°, 5–5.5 at 85°, 13–38 at 88°, and up to 94 hours at the pole in the lunar day of the equinox, when the Sun creeps along the horizon. The Sun's disc and refraction together move polar day 0.38° equatorward, from 88.39° to 88.01°; refraction makes 0.11° of that.

4. **Most polar light is sky light, and the daily light falls from 17 to 7 mol/m² between 60° and the poles.** With the Sun 5° up the clear sky gives open ground 7,620 lux, of which the direct beam brings 297 lux, 4%. At 10° the beam brings 1,339 of 11,560 lux (12%), and at 20° 6,567 of 22,240 (30%): the tall column scatters the low Sun's beam into the sky before it reaches the ground. Averaged over the year, the clear-sky DLI is 16.9 mol/m²/d at 60°, 14.3 at 65°, 11.9 at 70°, 9.8 at 75°, 8.1 at 80°, 7.1 at 85° and 6.8 at the poles. Through the eclipse year it varies by ±5% at 60° and ±13% at the north pole (5.9–7.7); at the south pole it varies by ±20% (5.5–8.3), where tilt and distance add in the run's phase. Within a lunar day the light swings widely at 60°, where 24-hour windows hold 0.03–54 mol/m², and barely at the poles. The north pole's midwinter lunar day, with the Sun never rising, holds 4,040–4,070 lux and 5.9 mol/m² in every 24 hours.

5. **The Earth adds little.** On the near side, at turned longitude 0°, the Earth's light on the ground peaks at 1.6–2.7 lux at 60°, 0.9–1.9 lux at 70° and 0.4–1.1 lux at 80°. It makes 5–12% of the darkest moment's light at 60° and under 1.3% poleward of 70°. The full Earth stands highest in each pole's winter: at full Earth the sub-Earth latitude is −(1 + i/ε) times the Sun's declination, so in the north's midwinter the full Earth stands 6.8° higher than its mean place. On the limb the Earth gives at most 0.6 lux. On the far side its light arrives only poleward of about 82°, where libration brings the Earth to the horizon or over the pole, and gives 0.04–0.5 lux.

6. **The run's clouds take a sixth to a third of the light and bring dark hours back to 72°.** The run's cover over the polar rows is 22–34% in the north and 21–36% in the south. In the two-state sky, with overcast passing 0.25 of the clear sky's light, the year's mean DLI falls to 11.5 mol/m²/d at 60°, 8.8 at 70°, 6.6 at 80° and 5.7 at the poles, 68–84% of clear. An overcast lunar day brings dark hours wherever its darkest light falls below 4 µmol/m²/s. The expected dark hours are 137 a lunar day at 60°, 49 at 65°, 35 at 66°, 15 at 70° and 3.4 at 72°, with none from 73°; the south, cloudier and with its winter at aphelion, has 52 at 66° and 7.4 at 72°. The overcast's transmission sets that boundary: with overcast passing 0.1 of the light, dark hours reach 77°; with 0.5, they end at 69°. Walking light of 1 lux holds at every hour poleward of 60° even under overcast; reading light of 8 lux holds from 63° N and 64° S.

7. **The new polar terrain keeps a small share of the ground from the Sun for whole lunar days, and its shadows still receive the sky's light.** The north cap spans today's north polar region and the far side's northern highlands, and is 12% sea. The south cap holds today's south polar craters about 8° from the new pole and is 30% sea. In the north, ground without direct sunlight through the equinox lunar day covers 0.8% of the cap: 4.5% of the ground at 80–85° and 8.9% at 85–90°. Ground without direct sunlight all year covers 0.34% of the cap, 8,550 km² (7,000 km² of land): 2.0% of 80–85° and 2.7% of 85–90°. In the south the equinox shadow covers 4.6% of 80–85° and 16% of 85–90°, and the year-round shadow covers 0.48% of the cap, 12,170 km² (11,200 km² of land), 7.5% of 85–90°. Through the equinox lunar day terrain takes 12–13% of open ground's sun hours over each cap, 28–34% at 80–85° and 42–54% at 85–90°. A horizontal surface draws little light from the sky near the horizon, so at the darkest moment the horizon blocks a median of 0.3–0.4% of the sky's light over each cap and 2.5–2.8% at the 90th percentile (4.3% at 85–90° in the south). Ground without direct sunlight through the equinox lunar day still receives 6.5–7.5 mol/m²/d then (10th to 90th percentile), 5.5–6.7 in midwinter and 7.5–8.5 in midsummer. Its darkest light stays between about 450 and 3,600 lux, and it has no dark hours under a clear sky.

8. **For plants, the polar light is steady, mostly diffuse and below saturation, and "no dark hours poleward of 65°" holds only under a clear sky.** The clear-sky DLI of 17 mol/m²/d at 60° lies within the 12–20 recommended for leafy greens, 9.8–11.9 at 70–75° within the 8–12 of shade crops such as impatiens, and 6.8–8.1 at 80–90° within the 5–10 of seedlings and cuttings (Stallknecht 2025, *source*). Under the run's clouds the DLI falls to 11.5 at 60°, 8.8 at 70° and 5.7–6.6 poleward of 80°, within the shade crops' band at 60–70° and the seedlings' poleward. In Faust et al. (2005, *source*) bedding plants' dry mass rose with DLI from 5 to 43 mol/m²/d, begonia's and impatiens' to 19. Every polar DLI here, at most 20 mol/m²/d even in the brightest summers at 60°, lies where Earth plants' growth still rises with the light. The canopy's saturation onset, the light-limitation study's Ek of 100–300 µmol/m²/s, is exceeded for 336 and 224 hours a lunar day at 60°, 327 and 146 at 70° and 300 and 0 at 80°. The light never exceeds 300 µmol/m²/s poleward of 76°, and from 89° to the poles it never exceeds 100. The plant model's 120 dark hours for its 60° band match the 116 a lunar day computed here at 60° (92–136 through the year). Its "none poleward of 65°", ecology's finding 15, holds under a clear sky from 66° N and 67° S at the design tilt, and from 67° at 2.5°. At 65° itself the clear sky gives 14 dark hours a lunar day on average and up to 52 (19 and 63 in the south). Under the run's clouds dark hours continue to 72°, on 90% of lunar days at 70° and 39% at 72°, or to 69–77° across overcast transmissions of 0.5–0.1. On the terrain, 63–64% of the land at 60–70° has dark hours in a clear midwinter lunar day and all of it under the run's clouds; at 70–80° a third of the land has them under clouds in midwinter.

9. **For people, the polar regions have no dark hours, and outdoor task light falls short only toward 60–75°.** The living-light study's levels for walking (1 lux), faces at 4 m (1.6 lux) and the 3 lux that ends civil twilight on Earth hold at every hour of every lunar day poleward of 60°, clear or overcast. Reading at 8 lux falls short only under clouds: 14 hours a lunar day at 60°, 3 at 62°, none from 63–64°. Close work outdoors at 100 lux (EN 12464-2, as living light cites it) falls short 152 hours a lunar day at 60°, 85 at 65°, 10 at 68° and none from 70° under a clear sky. Under the run's clouds it falls short 34 hours at 70° (50 in the south), 2.5 at 75° (6.4) and none from 78°. Indoor reading's 500 lux, met outdoors, falls short 233 hours at 60° and 41 at 75° under a clear sky, and holds at every hour from 78° (from 85° under clouds).

10. **The tilt moves the polar seasons; the precession moves only the amount of light, by ±3.4%; the air, clouds and terrain set the rest.**

    The tilt sets the declination's amplitude. It therefore moves the Sun's heights, the hours, the poles' seasonal light (±9% at the poles between the two tilts), where polar day begins and how much ground stays in shadow all year. It leaves the year's mean light within 1%. The precession keeps the tilt and the eclipse year and moves only the season at which the Sun is nearest: in the run's fixed phase the south's summers are brighter and its winters darker than the north's. Over the 18.6-year cycle that asymmetry reverses, the north's midsummer DLI rising 6.9% and its midwinter DLI falling 6.5% when perihelion comes in its summer. The run's polar sunlight swings 20 W/m² over the year in the south against 11 W/m² in the north; the same sense appears above the air (42.8 against 32.5 W/m²) and at the ground under a clear sky (the south pole's DLI swings 2.7 mol/m²/d, the north pole's 1.8). Set by the air are the twilight's floor, the end of clear-sky dark hours at 66–67°, the light's diffuse share and the 6.7′ of refraction. Set by the clouds are a sixth to a third of the light and the dark hours to 72°. Set by the terrain are the year-round shadow on 0.3–0.5% of each cap, an eighth of the sun hours, and little of the sky's light.

11. **The run's own surface sunlight cannot serve as a cloud transmission in the polar rows.** Read for years 10–49 by tenth of the lunar day, its all-sky over clear-sky surface sunlight on land runs 0.45–1.04 in the tenth centred on sunrise and 1.4–3.4 in the tenth centred on sunset, from 58° to 86°. Its ratio over the whole lunar day reaches 1.0 at 80° and 1.15 at 85.8°. In the night tenths, whose mean solar height is zero, both fluxes stay above zero: 13–24 W/m² at 85.8°. The all-sky flux at 85.8° stays nearly flat through the lunar day (24–30 W/m²) while the clear-sky flux follows the Sun from 13 to 37 W/m². The pattern points to the run's time sampling or its clear-sky diagnostic in the polar rows, which the climate lane can trace; this study uses the run's cloud cover alone. The extract is on the research drive (`run_sunlight_y10-49.npz`) and summarised in the product's `clouds.run_sunlight_diagnostic`.

## Method

**The Sun's path.** The ephemeris is the turned Moon's mean geometry in its own frame (`run.lunar_days`). In Cassini state 2 the spin axis keeps its tilt ε to the ecliptic and precesses with the orbit's node. The Sun's declination over the lunar equator is therefore asin(sin ε sin u), where u advances through the eclipse year of 346.63 days, derived from the run's year of 365.256 days and geology's nodal period of 6,798.38 days. The Sun's distance follows Earth's orbit at the run's eccentricity of 0.0167. The precession's phase ψ is the value of u at perihelion; the run holds ψ at 270°, the south's midsummer at perihelion every year, and the opposite phase comes 9.3 years later. The Sun's hour angle runs at the mean synodic rate. Each latitude from 60° to 90° in both hemispheres (1° steps, 0.5° poleward of 85°) gets 72 lunar days, one for every 5° of u at local noon. Each is sampled every quarter hour from midnight to midnight, with the declination advancing through the day. Hours above the horizon are counted three ways: the disc's centre above the geometric horizon, its upper limb, and its upper limb lifted by refraction. Annual values weight each season by the time the Sun spends there.

**Refraction.** The bending of a ray in a spherically stratified atmosphere follows from Bouguer's invariant n r cos h. The runner integrates it through the design column in the variable √(r − r₀), which removes the horizon's singularity (`run.refraction`). The column is the middle atmosphere's 1.2-atm profile, blended in log density between its 288 K and 298 K cases to the solved column's 294.9 K surface at 123,596 Pa. Its refractivity is that of standard air at 550 nm, 2.7784×10⁻⁴ at 15 °C and 101,325 Pa (Edlén 1966, *source*), scaled by number density; at the ground that gives 3.31×10⁻⁴. The same integral on the Earth control's column gives 0.569° at the horizon against the 34′ measured on Earth. On an isothermal exponential atmosphere it returns the analytic (n₀ − 1)√(πr/2H) within 3% (tests).

**The light at the ground.** Sunlight and Earthlight on open level ground come from the light calendar's evaluator (`illumination.calendar.validate.Response`). It integrates the finite solar disc and the phase-dependent Earth disc over the point-source response of the solved 1.2-atm column, the column whose twilight carries far beyond the terminator. Photosynthetic photons per lux come from the solved standard solution behind the solved-sky atlas, with 114 spectral channels, 95 of them in 400–700 nm. For the diffuse light the runner takes each channel's ground flux at every solar elevation from −90° to 90°; for the direct beam, each channel's slant optical depth through the solution's 100 spherical shells. The channel sums reproduce the solved sky's direct normal light of 57,790 lux overhead and its 3,405 lux at 5°. The sky's light carries 0.0163–0.018 µmol/m²/s per lux and the direct beam 0.0167 per lux overhead, rising to 0.039 at the horizon, where the beam is red. That puts the twilight's photons at −25° 7% above the plant model's, which scales one daytime diffuse spectrum. The Earth stands at sub-Earth latitude −asin(sin(i + ε) sin F), F being the Moon's argument of latitude, and its light is tabulated by its elevation and phase through the same evaluator.

**Clouds.** The run's cloud cover by latitude row and hour angle (climatology years 10–49, `clt_sun`) enters a two-state sky: clear with probability 1 − c and overcast with probability c. Overcast passes 0.25 of the clear sky's light, Kasten and Czeplak's clear-sky index at eight oktas, 1 − 0.75 (N/8)^3.4 (*source*). That relation was fitted to hourly data with the Sun above 5° and is applied here to the low Sun and to twilight as well. Mean photons take the factor 1 − 0.75c. Hours below a level are the expectation over the two states, with crossings found by log-linear interpolation. The product also gives an overcast passing 0.1 and 0.5 of the light (`clouds.overcast_sensitivity`).

**Terrain.** LOLA's heights above the GRAIL geoid at 16 px/deg (`geography.topography`) are joined with the seas at the atlas's level of −1,654.2 m and the forty-year routing's rain-fed lakes at their levels as flat water. They are carried into the turned frame (`climate.gcm.turned.to_today`) and resampled bilinearly on a polar stereographic grid of 1.895 km about each turned pole. Every grid pixel from 60° to the pole, 758,273 per cap, gets a horizon in 72 azimuths: the highest elevation of the ground or water surface along each great circle at 35 distances out to 265 km, with the Moon's curvature exact. Direct sunlight is the Sun's centre above that horizon through a lunar day of 360 Sun positions. It is computed at five declinations: midwinter and midsummer at each tilt, and the equinox. Ground in shadow through the midsummer lunar day stays in shadow all year. The light the horizon still admits uses the solved atlas's angular sky radiance, integrated from the horizon up in each azimuth relative to the Sun, so a ridge toward the twilight's bright sunward sky blocks more than one facing away. That calculation runs on every second pixel in each direction, 189,569 per cap, at 48 Sun positions per lunar day and at the mean distance.

## Limits

- **The sky is the solved molecular column's.** One horizontally uniform 1.2-atm column with a Lambertian ground of albedo 0.1 and no aerosol carries every number on open ground. Its long twilight is the reason the poles stay lit. Haze, a regional temperature profile or a brighter ground would shift the twilight's floor; the twilight's low-Sun radiance has the numerical checks of `illumination/sky/SOLVED_COLUMN.md` and no measurement.
- **Refraction enters only the hours.** The scattering solution uses straight rays. Refraction therefore lifts the Sun's disc for counting hours and for direct sunlight on terrain, and the twilight's brightness stays as solved.
- **Mean geometry.** The ephemeris has uniform rotation, sinusoidal libration in longitude, a mean Earth distance and the Sun's hour angle at the mean synodic rate. The Sun's apparent motion and the Earth's distance change through the month by a few percent, which the ephemeris leaves out. It reproduces the declination's amplitude and the full Earth's latitude, and it is no ephemeris for dates.
- **Clouds are a two-state screen.** The overcast transmission of 0.25 is an Earth relation applied beyond its fitted Sun heights. The cloud-twilight study found evening clouds on the Moon both brighter and darker than the clear sky, from 111% brighter to 80% dimmer. The cover is the run's T21 climatology, whose cloud scheme is Earth-tuned.
- **Terrain.** Horizons come from 1.9 km pixels, with nearest-neighbour samples at 35 distances. Walls narrower than a pixel and peaks between samples beyond about 50 km are missed, so the shadow shares lean low for small, steep craters. Light at terrain pixels falls on horizontal ground. It leaves out the light the terrain itself reflects, the Earth's light and the Sun's changing distance; the last moves the dark-hour shares at 60–70° by a few points. The seas and lakes are flat water at the forty-year routing's levels on today's dry-Moon geoid, as the atlas has them.
- **Plants and people.** The plant model's dark (1 µmol/m²/s), the light-limitation study's Ek and the levels for people are thresholds for Earth organisms and Earth eyes. How engineered plants and adapted people use a 708-hour day of mostly diffuse light lies outside this study.
- **Not done.** No new run of the sky solver was needed, since the standard solution covers −90° to 90°. Earthlight carries sunlight's photons per lux. Sloped surfaces, snow and the polar cold-trap question lie outside this study.
