# Seawater lakes for a migration native to the Moon

6 November 2026, on `domain/hydrosphere`. This study designs the salt water for a migration of the Moon's own: fish live in the large fresh seas and their rivers, run down a one-way channel to spawn in a seawater lake, and their young climb back up into the fresh sea, in the eel's pattern with places chosen on this world. The eel dossier on `domain/food-systems` (cb85374c, on the remote; [sources.json](sources.json)) sets its preferred endpoint as a complete freshwater life cycle that recruits on its own, its route R3. It names two routes this study serves: R1, selection for a local spawning site, potentially in an artificial saline lake, and a fallback, an autonomously recruiting local saline population. The migration through the one-way channel is that fallback made physical. The register's leaning of 15 October, saltwater life kept in aquariums with the soda lakes left as their own ecosystems, stays open.

Two sets of screens answer it, both closed-form and both seconds of computation; no climate, cloud or lake model ran. [run.py](run.py) works out how a seawater lake of Earth's make-up, 35.165 g/kg at a pH near 8.1, is held and kept apart from the fresh waters and how the channel passes fish, and writes [results/seawater_lakes.json](results/seawater_lakes.json) (schema `terluna.research.seawater-lakes/1`). [sites.py](sites.py) screens the atlas's hollows near the large fresh seas on the turned Moon's design climate and writes [results/sites.json](results/sites.json) (schema `terluna.research.seawater-lake-sites/1`). Each product carries its producer's and inputs' sha256, an evidence statement and a reading rule. [sources.json](sources.json) records each published value and how far it was read.

Evidence labels: *product* (a project result, with its source), *computed* (this study's arithmetic on stated inputs), *published* (with the source and read depth in sources.json), *recalled* (from memory, unverified here), *design guess*.

## The migration

**The life cycle in lunar terms**, with the eel dossier's stage evidence (*published* via the dossier, which cites each study):

1. **Growth in fresh water.** Young eels grow for years in the fresh seas, at 0.14 g/kg and pH 8.3, and in their rivers, in the habitats the dossier proposes: silty channels and backwaters, rock and gravel refuges, vegetated lake margins and the sea shores. They need daytime shelter through the 354-hour lunar day.
2. **The run down.** Maturing adults descend the channel into the seawater lake, swimming with its flow. On Earth the Japanese eel spawns at 150–200 m depth in the nights before the new moon. The Moon offers a dark cue in its own lunar night, and the depth's pressure would need 0.9–1.2 km of water at lunar gravity, a cue hatcheries already do without. Selection for local spawning, the dossier's R1, has to make maturation and spawning happen in the lake.
3. **Spawning and eggs in full-strength water.** Eggs and early embryos develop best near 34–35 psu (the dossier's E06, which tested 24–42 psu), so the lake's open water stays at seawater strength.
4. **Months of larval life, better in brackish water.** In captivity half-strength seawater gave 18.2% larval survival over two months against 8.2% in full strength, and unfed larvae did best at 13–16 psu, while fresh water and 10% seawater killed every larva within six days (E05, E07). The dossier's hatchery scenario runs a 270-day larval cycle (a scenario assumption), and wild larvae drift for months (*recalled*). The nursery therefore holds two waters: open seawater for the eggs, and a brackish reach of about 13–17 psu where the fishway's fresh water spreads into the lake, for the older larvae.
5. **Glass eels climb back.** Metamorphosed glass eels, about 0.2 g each in the dossier's accounts, find the inflow by its fresh water and climb the channel's eel ramp into the fresh sea, where step 1 begins again.

The larval stage spans four to nine lunar days. The dossier counts larval food among the questions that limit recruitment, and the waters' trophic design holds open water nutrient-poor, so the nursery lake has to be one of the productive habitats that design allows. The same channel serves the reverse, anadromous case the dossier's sturgeon study treats, fish living in salt water that spawn in fresh rivers, by passing them up the fishway.

**The one-way channel is built for downstream adults and climbing juveniles.** The lake stands below its fresh sea, and the sea's water falls into it down a pool fishway that carries the lake's whole inflow, so water and salt move only into the lake (below). Adults descend with the flow. Glass eels swim too weakly to pass a pool fishway's jets, so an eel ramp runs beside it the full height of the drop, a wetted climbing surface of bristle or studded substrate fed by a trickle (Earth eel-pass practice, *recalled*). Lunar gravity eases both: a pool's 1.0 m/s jet comes from a 0.31 m drop where Earth's comes from 0.051 m, so a few pools climb the whole drop, and a climbing eel carries a sixth of its Earth weight, while the ramp's trickle runs slower and deeper for the same discharge *(computed scalings; the steeper ramps they allow are a design guess to test)*. The inflow is the attraction flow the migrants follow: 7–84 m³/s at the best sites below, a real stream at every one of them.

**The four criteria.** The nursery is a lake of at least 100 km², 300 km² for a good one, with a mean depth of at least 20 m *(design guess)*. A larval cohort spreading by a lake's horizontal eddy diffusivity of 1–10 m²/s (*recalled*) over 120–270 days, wild larvae's months to the dossier's hatchery cycle, spreads 4.6–21.6 km (one standard deviation, *computed*). A lake about 20 km across or more therefore keeps a cohort's core in open water, with room for its brackish reach. The stream is an inflow of at least 1 m³/s at the low end of the lake's evaporation. The route is at most 20 km to one of the six named seas. The salt is at most 10 Gt, about a decade of the chloride the crust's top-up water would yield (24 km³ of seawater a year, above).

**How coarse it is.** The atlas grid has 4 px/deg, 7.6 km pixels, so the distances are good to about a pixel and the passes to the pixel's relief, and the hollows in the table span 13–67 pixels. The GCM's T21 cells are 5.5° across, about 170 km and 29,000 km² at the equator, so each of these hollows covers 3–11% of the cell whose rain and demand it takes. The nearest land cell's centre lies 0.7–3.3° from these hollows, and neighbouring hollows share a cell. The lake's evaporation is the storm-water pools' result, 0.80–0.93 m a year on the design years' semi-arid air, scaled by the cell's Priestley–Taylor demand over that air's 714 mm a year; the turned run's own semi-arid land demands 669 mm. Its net evaporation is that less the cell's rain, from one run's ten years, `A28R_301k_design_l15_snow_s924_turned_refit` years 10–19.

## Holding the lake

**The easiest seawater lake is a terminal lake in a dry belt, set in a closed hollow beside a held fresh sea or a large fresh lake, with its level held a metre or more below its fresh neighbour's lowest water and the water table around it, on a fine-grained floor, and fed with the held fresh sea's own water through one downhill channel.** Each part of that setting answers one way a seawater lake fails. A dry belt makes the lake lose more water to the air than it receives as rain, so salt can stay while fresh water enters to make up the loss. The hollow below its neighbours makes it the low point of the local groundwater, so seepage runs into the lake and carries no salt out. The fine floor keeps dense brine from sinking into the ground. The held fresh sea's calcium bicarbonate water brings no alkalinity the lake's calcifiers cannot lay down, so the lake stays off the soda path. A downhill channel carries water in one direction only, and a pool fishway on it lets fish pass both ways. The atlas's common-level lakes in the dry belts, small waters in closed hollows below the sea level beside the seas and half the desert shore ([coast water](../coast_water/README.md)), are the natural candidates: in a dry belt, evaporation would hold such a hollow's water below the sea's level, so that seepage from the sea runs into it. A depth of 10–30 m balances the salt, 0.36–1.08 Mt per km², against wind setup, which a 5 m lake takes four times as strongly as a 20 m one, and against the chemistry's drift, whose times grow with depth. *(Superseded for siting by [the turned-Moon screen](#where-it-can-go-on-the-turned-moon): the candidates are now named hollows on the turned Moon's climate, and with each lake held near the sea's level its depth is the hollow's own, 36–142 m on average at the best sites.)* A crater bowl serves as well as any other hollow once its floor is sealed, by the fine sediment lakes lay (0.15–2.4 mm a year under the rain-fed lakes in geography's groundwater model, *design guesses* there) or by placed clay, since a crater's shattered floor passes water freely (*recalled*). Sediment threatens a seawater lake less than it does the rain-fed lakes, whose small members fill in a median 4,600 years ([terrain through time](../terrain_through_time/README.md)): its feed is clear sea water, its own catchment can be kept small, and its calcifiers lay 0.017–0.027 mm of calcite a year *(computed from 47–73 t per km² at 2,700 kg/m³)*.

**In a dry belt the lake needs 18–28 L/s of fresh inflow per km² (0.56–0.87 million m³ a year) to hold its salinity, and in a wet belt it has 0.56–1.57 m of surplus water a year to shed without its salt** *(computed on products)*. A pool over the design years' semi-arid land evaporates 0.80–0.84 m a year at 3 m and 0.3 m deep, and 0.88–0.93 m with Lake Mead's measured transfer factor on its daylight loss ([storm water](../storm_water/README.md), phase 2b, *product*). Less the semi-arid land's 0.243 m of rain (design years) or the deserts' 0.062 m ([coast water](../coast_water/README.md), at the interpolated 301 K resort), that leaves 0.56–0.87 m a year of net evaporation. The design years class 57.8% of the land as dryland by UNEP's aridity classes (climate/gcm), so dry-belt sites are plentiful. A wet-belt lake under 1.55–2.55 m of rain (Tranquillitatis's land to the land box at 0° E, *product*) and evaporating as the GCM's open water does, 0.98–1.00 m, gains 0.56–1.57 m a year more than it loses. Its surplus can leave only by spilling, which at 35.9 kg/m³ of salt takes 20–56 kt of salt per km² a year and empties a 20 m lake of its salt in 13–36 years, or through reverse osmosis at 0.19–0.72 MW per km², continuously (3–4 kWh/m³, *recalled*). The open water's global mean sits between the two belts: 0.43 m a year net in the design years and 0.40 m in the turned run with the refit.

**The feed water sets the chemistry's upkeep.** Calcifiers lay a feed's calcium with twice its alkalinity as calcite. Any alkalinity left over, brought with magnesium, sodium or potassium, they can lay only with the lake's own calcium, which then falls; once that calcium is spent the lake starts down the soda path the [sea chemistry](../sea_chemistry/README.md#closed-lakes-soda-lakes-and-salt-seas) describes. Dosing calcium chloride replaces the calcium, and a brine bleed to evaporation pans exports the feed's magnesium, sodium and potassium with the dose's chloride. **Isolation rests on the lake being the local low point.** A seawater lake standing above the water table, as one set inland in the dry belts would over water tables a median 295 m down at 30–45° and 556–808 m poleward of 45° (geography, *product*), leaks through any floor. Through a metre of liner under 20 m of water, Earth's compacted-clay standard (10⁻⁹ m/s on Earth, *recalled*) passes 0.11 m a year at lunar gravity (0.66 m on Earth), 3,900 t of salt per km² a year, 0.55% of the lake's salt; ten times tighter passes 390 t and a hundred times tighter 39 t *(computed)*. That salt goes into groundwater, which flows toward lower fresh waters. A lake that takes groundwater in, as nearly all the mature Moon's lakes do at a median 5 cm a year (geography, *product*), holds its salt within about 0.6 m of its floor, where diffusion against the inflow stops it *(computed)*. Its floor must also stay stable under the dense water above it: a 10 m porous floor stays free of convective brine fingers below a permeability of 3.0×10⁻¹⁴ m² on the Moon and 5.0×10⁻¹⁵ m² on Earth (porous Rayleigh number 4π², *computed*), so clays and fine silts qualify while sands and fractured rock lie above it (*recalled* permeability ranges). Beside a lake held below its neighbours, fresh groundwater meets the lake's brine at a depth of 38 m for each metre the fresh water table stands above the lake's level (Ghyben and Herzberg's relation, the same at any gravity). That seepage carries little water: held 1–3 m below fresh water a kilometre from its whole shore, at the shores' central transmissivity of 5.2×10⁻⁴ m²/s ([coast water](../coast_water/README.md), *product*), a 10 km² lake takes in 2–10% of the inflow it needs, a 1 km² lake 7–31% and lakes of 100 km² and more under 3% *(computed)*. The margin sets the direction of flow, and the channel supplies the water.

**Lunar gravity makes wind setup six times as large and spray drift six times as far, and the margin and the buffer have to cover them.** A 10 m/s wind over 10 km of a 20 m lake piles its downwind end up 5.5 cm on the Moon against 0.9 cm on Earth, and a 20 m/s storm over 10 km of 5 m water 0.88 m against 0.15 m *(computed, Earth's drag coefficient)*. A 20 µm spray droplet settles at 2.0 mm/s against 12 mm/s, so from 10 m up in a 5 m/s wind it drifts 25 km against 4 km *(computed, Stokes settling)*. The level margin below the fresh neighbour therefore has to exceed the setup, a storm's rise and any tide in the fresh water. The semi-arid land's 243 mm of rain a year comes to about 20 mm a lunar day *(computed)*, so a lake with a small catchment rises by centimetres in a storm period. The tides are the larger term: the Near-side Sea's typical monthly range is 3.7 m, South Pole–Aitken's 2.1 m and Smythii–Marginis's 0.55 m (register, *product*), and the rain-fed lakes have almost none. Fresh intakes and reservoirs belong upwind and kilometres away, and the lake's overflow, its bleed and its floodwater go to pans in their own closed hollow.

**A downhill channel with a pool fishway keeps the flow one way and passes fish both ways; a level channel and a fish lock leak.** A salt wedge held below a sill needs fresh water crossing the sill at the densimetric Froude number 1 (*published*, two-layer hydraulics). At lunar gravity the density contrast of 26 kg/m³ gives a reduced gravity of 0.042 m/s² (0.253 on Earth), so a 0.5 m deep sill arrests the wedge with 0.072 m²/s of fresh water per metre of width, crossing at 0.145 m/s, where Earth needs 0.178 m²/s at 0.355 m/s *(computed)*. The lake's own inflow is the only water for it: a 10 km² lake's 0.18–0.28 m³/s holds a 0.5 m sill 2.5–3.8 m wide, a 100 km² lake's one 25–38 m wide, and a 1 km² lake's only 0.25–0.38 m. A level connection of any larger section runs as an exchange flow, fresh water in at the top and the lake's brine out along the bed toward the fresh side, and every wind setup of a few centimetres pushes the arrested wedge over the sill. Holding the lake below its fresh neighbour removes both risks: water falling over a drop carries nothing upstream. A pool fishway then climbs the drop with jets of v = √(2gΔz): at 1.0 m/s a pool drops 0.31 m on the Moon and 0.051 m on Earth, so 7 pools climb 2 m where Earth needs 40, and at 1.5 m/s 3 pools do *(computed)*. Each jet crosses a 1 m pool at a densimetric Froude number of 4.9–7.3, so the lake's salt stops at the lowest slot. A fish lock leaks salt to the fresh side on every cycle. A 20 × 4 × 2 m chamber opened to the lake fills with seawater in about 4.6 minutes on the Moon and 1.9 on Earth (lock-exchange front at half of √(g'H), *recalled*), and raised and opened unflushed it releases 5.75 t of salt upstream. Flushed through its floor before the upper gate opens, as the Krammer locks' salt-fresh separation does, it still releases 0.06–0.29 t a cycle *(design guess: 1–5% left)*, though the flushing water is part of the lake's inflow and the 10 km² lake's inflow covers 64–99 flushes a day. The Fish Migration River through the Afsluitdijk, a winding channel several kilometres long whose valves close when its salt rises and which is to pass about 70% of some 250 million fish a year, is Earth's working example of salt-fresh passage under a no-salt rule (*published*; its salt record not read).

## Recommendation

**For the migration**: hollow 547, 37.5° N, 43.6° E on the turned Moon (43.7° N, 54.1° E today), a lake of 789 km², 40 m deep on average, 1 m or more below the Near-side Sea's lowest water and 13 km from it over a 45 m pass. The channel runs below sea level all the way, so it is cut through that pass, about 46 m deep at its highest ground; the cut's length and earthwork are not sized here, and South Pole–Aitken's hollow 2347 needs only about 11 m. Its arid cell gives it 0.98–1.15 m a year of net evaporation, so the sea's water runs down its fishway at 25–29 m³/s. The lake takes 1.15 Gt of salt. The salt is held as below, and the seawater basin carries a brackish reach where the fishway's water spreads. Hollows 492 and 294 beside the same sea, 366 beside Humboldtianum and 2347 beside South Pole–Aitken are the alternatives in the table above, and the atlas round, where districts are mapped, chooses among them with a well survey of each one's water table.

**With fish passage**, as first proposed *(siting superseded by the turned-Moon screen)*: one of the atlas's common-level hollows in a dry belt, 10–100 km² and 10–30 m deep, beside a held fresh sea; its level held a metre or more below the sea's lowest water, 1–3 m below its mean beside a sea with a small monthly range such as Smythii–Marginis and more beside the Near-side Sea *(design guess, set by the setup, storm rise and tide it must cover)*; a floor of silt or clay with groundwater entering it; fed by the sea's own water with its magnesium at the low end of the range under study, through a pool fishway that carries the whole inflow downhill, gated by salinity sensors as the Afsluitdijk's valves are; a brackish marsh at the fishway's foot; brine pans in a separate closed hollow below the lake; barramundi as the first spawning species and mullet in the largest lake. A hollow beside a large rain-fed lake works the same way with a smaller margin, since such a lake barely moves, and a slightly larger bleed. **Without fish passage**: the same setting at any size from 1 km², fed by pipe or a gated drop.

**Not viable**: seawater lakes in wet belts without continuous desalination; perched lakes over deep water tables without a nearly perfect seal; level, open connections to fresh water; a fish lock where any salt in the fresh side matters; eels spawning on their own in a lake; Australian bass as Earth's line.

**Costs beside aquariums.** Per km² of a 20 m lake: 718 kt of salt to fill, chloride from 1.4 km³ of delivered water; 18–28 L/s of fresh inflow; upkeep of nothing to 3,000 t of salt a year depending on the feed (nothing to 0.4% of the fill), with 0–48 t of calcium chloride; pans of 0–12% of its area; and little energy, since the inflow runs by gravity and lifting the bleed takes tens of watts. The management is continuous: level and inflow control, chemistry monitoring and dosing, the bleed and its pans, salinity-gated passage, and wells around the lake watching for salt in the groundwater. Aquariums hold a million cubic metres with 36,000 t of salt (sea chemistry) and run life support without pause: pumping through filters alone takes 7–23 MW per million m³ *(design guess: a turnover every one to two hours through 30–50 kPa at 60% efficiency, the same at any gravity)*, before light and heat. A 10 km² lake 20 m deep holds 200 million m³ for 7.2 Mt of salt and gives ocean habitat at scale: spawning aggregations, reefs and migrations. Aquariums give full control for little salt and little land.

## How it is set up

- **Seawater** is Millero et al.'s reference composition at 35.165 g/kg as the sea chemistry carries it (`SEAWATER`, imported), practical salinity 35.0, at 29.5 °C, the design years' sea surface *(design guess for every lake and its inflow)*. Densities follow UNESCO's EOS-80 at one atmosphere, checked against its three published check values in the tests: 1,022.0 kg/m³ for the seawater and 995.9 kg/m³ for the held fresh sea at 0.142 g/kg. The held fresh sea's make-up is read from the sea chemistry's product, the central managed run kept open at a billion years.
- **Gravity** is GM/R² from `shared/constants.json`, 1.6242 m/s² (the brief's 1.62 is the rounded legacy value, 0.26% lower), against Earth's standard 9.80665 m/s². Every Moon–Earth pair differs only in gravity.
- **The climate's water.** The design years (`A28R_301k_design_l15_snow_s934`, years 10–19) come from climate/gcm's README. The turned run with the refit (`A28R_301k_design_l15_snow_s924_turned_refit`, years 10–19, 301.33 K and 1.98 mm/day of rain globally) is read from its drift check on the research drive (`session-tools/turned_design/drift_check_step2.out`), whose means the runner carries with the file's hash and the tests check against it where the drive is mounted: its open water evaporates 2.73 mm/day and receives 1.63, and its land receives 2.19 and evaporates 1.50. The products read here give no open-water evaporation by belt, so the dryland lake's comes from the storm-water study's pools, whose energy balance runs on the design years' semi-arid air.
- **The chemistry** is a steady budget per km²: inflow equal to net evaporation; calcite from the feed's calcium; the leftover alkalinity drawing the lake's calcium at half its rate; calcium chloride replacing that calcium; the bleed set by the most demanding of magnesium, sodium with potassium, and the dose's chloride, each held within a tenth of seawater's; the bleed's salt replaced as seawater's salts, or only its non-halite part where the pans' halite returns. Earth's terminal seawater-like lakes show why the bleed is needed: the Salton Sea, filled with fresh river water in 1905–07 at about 3.5 g/L, passed 40 g/kg by 1985 and stood near 57 g/kg in 2015 as its inflow's salts gathered (*published*, summaries).
- **Isolation** uses a liner's Darcy seepage with free drainage beneath, its conductivity scaled from Earth's by gravity (the same permeability and viscosity); the porous Rayleigh criterion 4π² (Horton, Rogers and Lapwood, *recalled*) with a porosity of 0.4, water's viscosity at 29.5 °C and a pore diffusivity of 10⁻⁹ m²/s *(recalled and design guesses)*; steady wind setup τL/(ρgh) with Earth's drag coefficient 1.3×10⁻³ (*Earth calibration*) and the design air's 1.41 kg/m³; Stokes settling of spray.
- **The channel** uses the two-layer criterion for an arrested wedge, the jets of a pool fishway and the lock-exchange front (*recalled*: Benjamin 1968; Shin, Dalziel and Linden 2004). Fishway jet speeds of 1.0–1.5 m/s are design values for small, weak swimmers; a fish's swimming speed does not depend on gravity, so the limit carries over from Earth practice unchanged.
- **The site screen** ([sites.py](sites.py)) reads geography's atlas grid (`products/atlas_28pct_4ppd.npz`, present and hashed) for every water body below the common level, its lake at 1 m below that level, and its pass to the nearest large water by the minimax height along 8-connected paths. Hollows poleward of 79.5° are left out, where the atlas's cylindrical grid misstates small craters. Each hollow's area-weighted centre is carried into the turned frame by the rotation in domain/climate's frame record (`climate/results/gcm/turned_surface_8d71fd350ca4c6cd.json`, read from that worktree), as `climate/gcm/turned.py` does. The turned run's rain and Priestley–Taylor demand are recomputed from the biome map's cached yearly sums (`session-tools/turned_design/peek/cache`) with `biosphere/ecology/biome_map.py`'s arithmetic, without importing either domain's code. A test checks the reader against the turned years' own land reading at the frame record's places.

## What it leaves out

- **The lake as a body of water.** No lake model ran: stratification, oxygen at depth, a fresh layer spreading from the fishway over the seawater, and the warm lake's thermal regime are outside the screens. A seawater lake fed from one end has a brackish gradient there and stratifies under the fresh inflow; its oxygen at depth needs the [lake oxygen](../lake_oxygen/README.md) model with salinity added.
- **Evaporation in a desert's dry air.** The pools' energy balance runs on the semi-arid air, and a desert lake evaporates more ([coast water](../coast_water/README.md)), so the desert end of the inflow is likely low. Day and night differ: a 3 m pool loses 47 mm in the lunar day's daylight and 18 mm through the night, so the inflow or the lake's level has to swing through each lunar day.
- **The orientation.** The dry belts, the desert shores round the common-level lakes and the water tables come from products made on today's orientation (coast water's desert masks, geography's groundwater); only the open water's evaporation and rain come from the turned design years. The turned Moon moves its latitudes by about 7° and its drylands with them, drier in the south (52% to 60% of the southern land) and wetter in the north (64% to 59%), so which hollows lie in dry belts is for the atlas round on the turned Moon; the settings, the isolation, the fish passage and the chemistry do not depend on the orientation. The site screen now reads the turned design years directly for the hollows near the seas; the dryland lake's evaporation and the water tables beneath the sites still come from today's orientation.
- **The site screen's resolution and climate.** Distances, passes and small hollows are as coarse as the 7.6 km atlas pixels; each hollow takes one T21 cell's rain and demand from one run's ten years; the lake's evaporation is the pools' result scaled by demand, without a lake energy balance at each site. A site's evaporation and its water table need finer work in the atlas round, and the margin beside the tidal seas lowers each lake by 1–2 m more than the screen's 1 m.
- **The nursery and the migration.** The nursery's size rests on a recalled range of lake eddy diffusivities and on larval durations from the wild and the dossier's scenario; no larval drift, food or population model ran. The eel ramp's design at lunar gravity is untested. Whether a selected line spawns in the lake without hormones, and whether its larvae find food there, are the dossier's R1 and recruitment questions, which this study leaves open.
- **The chemistry's kinetics and minor ions.** Calcifiers are assumed to keep the lake near calcite saturation; dolomite, high-magnesium calcite, sulphate, strontium, boron, the carbonate system's pH and the CO₂ the calcite returns are not followed. Feeding the lake's own magnesium from the feed in place of a bleed, or softening the feed at the intake by the sea chemistry's magnesite route, would cut the upkeep further and is not computed.
- **Spray flux.** The screens give how far spray travels; the salt it carries from a lake's small, fetch-limited waves at lunar gravity is not computed.
- **Groundwater in detail.** The interface depth and the inward seepage are screens; a lake's own solution at tens of metres belongs to resources' question 16. The brine that fills the pores beneath a gaining lake once, before the inward flow holds it, is not counted.
- **Sizes for fish.** The lake sizes in the fish table are design guesses from the spawning settings; no population or larval model ran.

## Open choices, with recommendations

- **The feed water.** Recommended: the held fresh sea's water. It ties the seawater lakes' bleed to the held seas' magnesium, the 0.05–0.5 mmol/kg the register's row of 19 October puts under study, and the low end suits both.
- **The fresh neighbour.** Recommended: a held fresh sea with a small monthly range, whose water needs no bleed at all while its magnesium stays near zero. A large rain-fed lake, whose level barely moves, is the alternative; beside the Near-side Sea, with its 3.7 m monthly range, the margin grows to about 2–3 m below the mean.
- **The site.** Recommended: hollow 547 beside the Near-side Sea, with 492, 294, 366 and 2347 as alternatives, chosen finally in the atlas round with a well survey of each candidate's water table. *(This replaces the earlier choice among the dry belts' common-level hollows, which the turned-Moon screen has made.)*
- **The nursery's salinities.** Recommended: open water at seawater strength for the eggs, with a brackish reach of about 13–17 psu for the older larvae, following the eel dossier's stage evidence.
- **The salinity.** Recommended: 35 g/kg for barramundi and reefs, 35–37 g/kg in a lake meant for mullet.
- **Halite return.** Recommended: return the pans' halite, which cuts the salt brought for upkeep to about a fifth.
