# Aerophytes in the living sky: food, tenants and phosphorus

**Partial research checkpoint, 10 October 2026.** The existing calculations and saved product are preserved before further work on guards and symbioses. Known accounting, provenance and interpretation limits are recorded in [status.md](status.md); the numerical tables are conditional screens, not demonstrated communities.

The author asked on 10 October 2026: "How do these things integrate with the broader aerial ecosystem and global
ecosystem? Smaller ones can probably be eaten as part of the food chain. Larger ones could probably become homes for
lots of organisms, right? I'm sure too much on them and they'll start to sink, but I bet you could get lots of things on
decent size ones before that happens." This study answers in one coupled screen on the
[aerophyte](../aerophytes/README.md) and [aerial-ecology](../aerial_ecology/README.md) studies, with the phosphorus
checked against the resources branch's return model. The findings are *screen* values from [run.py](run.py) unless
marked *derived*. Two states of the sky are kept apart throughout: a growing sky, whose bodies spend their surplus on new
bodies, and a mature one, whose bodies hold their size while recruits replace the dead.

## The food web

- **Aerophytes are the sky's foundation species, as kelp, coral and trees are in theirs.** They gather the light of
  open air into bodies an animal can land on. A filter feeder in the open sky meets its resident aeroplankton at
  10⁻⁵–10⁻⁴ mg/m³ and recovers 2 × 10⁻⁷ to 10⁻⁴ of the energy it spends. An albatross-scale flyer flying between
  aerophytes at 1% cover spends 0.2–9% of a day's energy on each flight. [Food web](food_web.md).
- **Over 1% of the Moon they build 162–413 Mt C of biomass a year while they grow and 134–397 Mt once they hold their
  size, at the reference light, and give their tenants 38 Mt C of sugar besides.** Per square metre that is 0.43–1.09 kg
  C a year growing and 0.35–1.05 kg C mature; in canopy-like light growing bodies build 0.66–2.45 kg C, up to 928 Mt over
  1%. By class at the reference light, growing and mature:
  - lesser aerophytes (G02), 0.43–0.72 and 0.35–0.42 kg C;
  - round giants (G03), 0.79–1.00 and 0.70–0.93;
  - sky reefs (G03), 0.54–1.09 and 0.46–1.05.

  The aerial study's central 1,000 g C/m² of biomass sits at the top of the giants' and the reefs' ranges. Growing bodies
  add 0.3–10% to their standing biomass a year at the reference light. The cover runs from 0.1% to 2%. One per cent is
  the aerial study's central case, and the provisioning branch's sky towns for five billion people shade 1.8%
  (*derived*), so the sky is shared.
- **Giants take grazing as trees do, and the tenants that eat them are short of phosphorus.** Grazers taking a tropical
  canopy's 12–19% of the green tissue (Metcalfe et al. 2014) cost a reef module 3.8–6.0% of its growth surplus and a
  1-km giant 7.6–12%. The host's sugar carries no phosphorus and its green tissue 1–3 g P per kg, so its grazers and
  guards reach 8–41% of their carbon limit at the bounding edges (4–89% over every corner of the ranges, median 18%).
  The sea's phosphorus, brought up by roosting flyers, feeds the whole web.
- **The web on 1% cover is lean.** Its grazers and guards produce 0.6–7.0 Mt C a year and hold 0.12–3.4 Mt C; its
  invertebrate predators hold 0.005–0.53 Mt C. Earth's terrestrial arthropods hold about 200 Mt C and its wild birds
  about 2 Mt C (Bar-On et al. 2018).
- **That sky feeds 1.8–4.8 million albatross-scale grazing flyers, or 200,000–530,000 of the Quetzalcoatlus scale: a
  quarter to nearly twice the biomass of Earth's wild birds.**
  - **Energy.** A flyer six times the wandering albatross in every length weighs 1.7 t and burns 185 MJ a day by bird
    allometry (Nagy 2005). A species can sit at 30–170% of the allometry, which widens the albatross-scale count to
    1.1–16 million. The rule of similarity brackets the allometry's 39 times its Earth original between 36 and 48 times
    (*derived*), a consistency check; the extrapolation from tens of kilograms to tonnes stays open.
  - **Flight.** A kilometre costs it 0.45–0.93 MJ, a sixth of the same animal's cost on Earth.
  - **As predators** on the grazers the same sky feeds 28,000–1.6 million albatross-scale flyers.
- **Small aerophytes are a fast-growing pasture of costly food.**
  - **A 10-m body** storing no water carries 0.36 of the reference organism's living tissue, close to the two-fifths
    estimate.
  - **Only 5–8% of what a small body costs is living tissue a grazer can eat:** 59–66% went into its gas, 19–28% into
    its cellulose envelope, which goes to the decomposers, and 6–8% into construction respiration.
  - **They turn over fast.** Bodies of 8–15 m can lose 40–121% of their numbers a year to grazers and hold steady.
    Their edible production, 0.077–0.094 kg C/m² a year at the reference light, is a third to two-fifths of a giant's
    renewed green tissue.
- **Litter falls as sky snow beneath its track.** Flakes fall at 0.34–0.67 m/s from 10 km and drift 6–12 km along the
  design run's mean winds, 41–81 km from 20 km. Across the wind they drift at most 20 km, inside one 171-km cell of the
  wind product.

## Tenants

- **The undersides are lit.** The top is the host's income of light; tenants live on the flanks, the undersides, the
  fringes and in the pools.
  - **The air below** sends up 14–27% of the daylight over ground of albedo 0.05–0.2, and 53% over cloud.
  - **A lone body's underside** gets 19–30% of the light on its top, 8–13 mol/m² a day.
  - **A sky reef** lights its undersides at 11–22%, its contact rings at 5–8%.

  The estimate takes the illumination domain's surface-light product carried up through the air below by two-stream
  adding: 980–1,018 µmol/m²/s of daytime light reaches a level top at 10 km, 6–10% more than the ground gets.
  [Tenants](tenants.md).
- **An aerophyte carries a bromeliad forest's water.**
  - **Surface.** A round body has four square metres of surface per projected square metre.
  - **Pools.** Its free water store, 1–5 L/m² as pools, is what a cloud forest's tank bromeliads hold, up to 5 L per m²
    of ground (Sugden & Robins 1979).
  - **A reef's pockets.** Between its modules a reef has pockets of 12–30 m³ per m² of raft, where tank membranes make
    its pools.
- **A 100-m body carries 196 t of tenants within its 30% reserve, as estimated, and at most 1.9 t may arrive at once.**
  - **Sudden loads.** By the trim rule its 300 Pa of spare pressure absorbs 0.24 kg/m². That pressure is the day's water
    swing's allowance, so all of it is free only at the swing's low point. Dropping its free water it takes up to 33 t,
    spends its damage reserve, and must drink the water back.
  - **Heavy flocks belong on the big ones.** A 200-m body takes up to 15 t at once, a 500-m body 236 t and a 1-km giant
    1,890 t: 1,100 albatross-scale flyers by trim.
  - **Gas carbon binds giants.** Tenants need gas, and 10–30% of a century's surplus buys it for 2,700–36,700 t on a
    1-km giant. A mature body has its whole surplus beyond replacing its dead for such uses.
- **Sky reefs can grow domatia, modules for their tenants, as siphonophores specialise their zooids.** A 60-m domatium
  without a green top carries 11 kg/m², three times a green module's 3.4, and two to six green modules feed it. Landing
  domatia of 100–150 m take at most one to four albatross-scale flyers by trim and 21–49 dropping water.
- **Every guild has its Earth precedent:**
  - guards in domatia, as ants guard ant-plants (Heil et al. 1997; Treseder et al. 1995);
  - nesting flyers, as on seabird islands (Otero et al. 2018; Graham et al. 2018);
  - pool food webs, as in phytotelmata (Romero et al. 2006);
  - canopy soils on giants' flanks (Nadkarni 1981);
  - epiphytes and nitrogen fixers, as on drifting Sargassum (Coston-Clements et al. 1991; Johnson et al. 2023).

  Juveniles leave their megaforest crowns with 2.3–16 kg/m² of lift to spare for the canopy's life they carry.
- **A height band is one contact group.** At 10 km, passive routes released 300 km apart in latitude close to a median
  41 km within half a year, then swing between 0.2 and 92 km as their tracks merge and part. A spore shed at 10 km takes
  about 210 days to fall. The 1983 Diadema die-off swept 3.5 million km² in 13 months along currents (Lessios 1988).
  Heights and sailing keep populations apart.

## Phosphorus

- **Aerophyte tissues hold 0.7–8% of the phosphorus one Redfield ratio would give them.** A body holds 1.9–22 g P per
  m², and a year's biomass carries 2.7–17% of Redfield's for the same carbon. The inert envelope and the giants' tendons
  are almost all carbon. [Phosphorus](phosphorus.md).
- **The tenants are the aerophyte's soil.**
  - **Alone,** a mature body recycles only 38–69% of the phosphorus it cycles each year, because its litter falls 10 km.
  - **With pools and canopy soils** catching litter as a forest floor does, a mature sky recycles 89–98%. Over 1% of the
    Moon it needs 3.8–60 kt of new phosphorus a year, against 67–382 kt with nothing to catch the litter.
  - **A growing sky needs more.** Its new bodies take up to 0.4 g P per m² a year of new stock, so with forest-floor
    pools it recycles 74–96% and needs 13–164 kt.
  - **No body that dies keeps 99.9%.** In a mature sky the falls of whole bodies and the tenants that leave take
    0.6–6.4% of a reef's throughput.
- **The resources branch's low case reproduces exactly, and aerophyte stoichiometry moves it.** The case returns
  10,726 t of P a year to the sky. With a reef's tissues and recycling it becomes:
  - 0.5–85 kt with designed pools and 8–104 kt with forest-floor pools in a mature sky;
  - 22–264 kt with either kind of pool in a growing sky;
  - 137–737 kt with nothing to catch the litter.

  Its Redfield ratio overstates the sky's throughput 7–23-fold, and its 99.9% recycling is out of reach. A handful of
  roosting flyers closes a giant's phosphorus: 0.2–15 albatross-scale flyers for a mature 1-km giant with forest-floor
  pools.
- **Flyers can return the land's dissolved phosphorus.** Earth's seabirds carry 99 Gg of P a year from sea to land
  (Otero et al. 2018), 2.4–6.7% of the Moon's land loss of 1.47–4.16 Tg and 53–141% of its dissolved part (*derived*).
  - **The dissolved loss** comes back through roosts over land with 2.0–11 million albatross-scale flyers.
  - **A tenth of the whole loss** is the most they could carry.
  - **The eroded rest** goes to the sea floor, for dredging.

  Aerophytes carry the seabird island into the sky: a drifting roost scatters the sea's phosphorus over the land
  beneath its track.
- **Litter lands beneath shared tracks.** Passive bodies at 10 km linger near the equator (1.3–1.8 times their share)
  and over the north pole (3.4). At 20 km they gather over both poles (8.6–12). Their routes spend 75% of their time over
  land at 10 km and 71% at 20 km. Reefs that sail, 30–70° of latitude in a lunar day, spread it.
- **A giant's fall is the land's whale fall.** A 2-km sky reef falls with 6–18 t of P and 21,000 t of dry matter. It lays
  0.5–5.6 g P/m² over the ground it drapes, 3–90 years of the land's natural loss there. At 1% cover 60–300 reefs fall a
  year; a cover held fixed while its bodies grew would need 1,600–5,100.

## What a reef's community looks like

A sky reef is a kilometre or two of green-topped modules drifting at 10–20 km.
- **Its tops** are the host's.
- **Between its modules** the crevices hold tank pools of the reef's own water. Decomposers there mineralise litter and
  the droppings of roosting flyers, and the modules take the phosphate back as a bromeliad does.
- **Its undersides** glow with the light of the dense air below and carry shade phototrophs and nitrogen-fixing films,
  as Sargassum carries its epiphytes.
- **Its contact rings** are dark, for nests and the guards' domatia.
- **Its edge** holds landing domatia with spare pressure and full water stores, where great flyers roost after a day's
  flight from the sea.
- **Its fringes** carry spiders and collectors gathering the air's pollen and insects.
- **Small aerophytes** drift past on the same wind, eaten by the reef's flyers.

When a reef dies it falls as a draped tent of tens of thousands of tonnes over a few square kilometres, new soil fed
in part by the sea.

## How the study is built

Run `python -m research.studies.sky_ecology.run`; check with `python -m pytest research/studies/sky_ecology`. The
[product](results/sky_ecology.json) (schema `terluna.research.sky-ecology/1`) records the bindings, the evidence
statement and the reading rule.

**Bindings.**
- **This study's files and the aerophyte code** it calls are hashed.
- **The committed inputs** are hashed: the aerophyte, aerial-ecology, people, first-screen, taxonomy, surface-light,
  sky-ship and sky-fleet products and the water scenario. The sky-ship product gives the air's density through the
  fall of litter and the sky fleet's gliders bound the flyers' glide ratios.
- **Files from other branches** are bound by commit, blob and SHA-256 until integration, as
  research/studies/daily_life binds main's places:
  - the resources branch's phosphorus product, model and note at b47c452;
  - the provisioning branch's zonal-wind product, its arrays and note at d9bc800;
  - its ways-of-living product at e364c08.

  They are read in-tree once their hashes match there, and from Git until then.
- **The 28% atlas grid**, an ignored input, places the routes over land and sea when present.

**Records.** The shared constants the code reads are recorded by name and value. Literature sources and what was read
are in [sources.json](sources.json); the independent review's findings and their fixes are in [checks.json](checks.json).

| Component | Holds |
|---|---|
| [food web](food_web.md) | Production by class, state and cover, trophic levels, great flyers, the eaten base, sky snow |
| [tenants](tenants.md) | Light on each surface, real estate, carrying capacity and sudden loads, domatia, guilds, the nursery, contact |
| [phosphorus](phosphorus.md) | P by tissue, the sky's loop in both states, the resources check, the sea's return, deposition, falls |
| [stoichiometry](stoichiometry.py) | The P contents both other components use, the bounding edges and the consumers' P limit |

The taxonomy's identifiers used here (G01–G03, G08, G11, G14, C01, C10, M18–M21, M27, M28) are checked against
[the register](../../../biosphere/ecology/results/taxa.json).

## What would decide it next

1. **Measured tissues.** Phosphorus, resorption and turnover of a designed aerophyte's green tissue and envelope, which
   set every stock and flow here.
2. **A population model on the routes.** Aerophytes, their tenants and roosting flyers followed along the latitude and
   solar-hour winds, with phosphorus, litter and falls; it would also say how the sky divides between growing and mature
   bodies, which the two states here bound.
3. **The seas' food web.** How much fish the 28% seas grow on the land's phosphorus, which sets how many sea-feeding
   flyers can carry it back.
4. **Great flyers' energetics at the lunar scale.** The FMR extrapolation from tens of kilograms to tonnes.
5. **The resources box model with aerophyte stoichiometry.** Its sky box taking the tissue contents, the retention
   cases, the two states and the falls here.
