# Phosphorus in the sky

**An aerophyte holds 1.9–22 g of phosphorus per square metre, 0.7–8% of what one Redfield ratio would put in the same
biomass, and its tenants are its soil: without pools and decomposers to catch its litter a mature reef recycles only
45–68% of the phosphorus it cycles each year.** With pools and canopy soils that catch litter as a forest floor does, a
mature reef recycles 92–97.5%, and a growing one 76–92%, because its new bodies need new phosphorus. Falls of whole
bodies, tenants that leave and growth keep every case below the 99.9% the resources model asks. Numbers are *screen*
values from [phosphorus.py](phosphorus.py) (`phosphorus` in the [product](results/sky_ecology.json)) unless marked
*derived*. The P contents, resorption, retention cases, roosting, hazards and drape are design guesses, labelled once
here and given as ranges in [stoichiometry.py](stoichiometry.py) and `phosphorus.design_guesses`; the first ones are the
aerophyte growth note's.

**A year's biomass carries 0.52–2.05 g P per m², 2.7–17% of Redfield's for the same carbon.** Half the green tissue
renewed each year takes 0.5–1.5 g of it and renewed structure 0.02–0.38 g; a growing body's new bodies take 0.01–0.42 g
more, and a mature body's replacement of its dead 0.002–0.11 g. The inert envelope and the giants' tendons are almost all
carbon, the reason the aerophyte biology note warns against one ratio for the whole organism.

## The sky's own loop

**The losses.** Each year a body sheds green tissue after resorbing 50–80% of its phosphorus (the mean of terrestrial
leaves is 65%; Vergutz et al. 2012) and sheds renewed structure with all of its little phosphorus. People harvest 5–20%
of the green tissue and return 99% of its phosphorus, the resources model's food return. Grazers eat 12–19% of the green
tissue before resorption, as Metcalfe et al. (2014) found in tropical canopies, and build part of its phosphorus into
themselves; great flyers eat 10–50% of the grazers and leave 40–70% of their droppings elsewhere. Whole bodies fall at a
hazard of 0.001–0.005 a year (the aerophyte growth note) with all their stock.

**What the pools catch.** Alone, a body keeps only what it resorbs from its shed green tissue; its structure, the
grazers' frass and their remains all fall. With pools, the share of the rest (the year's renewal less the harvest and the
departing tenants) that the pools and canopy soils keep is the case: the land's own recycling, about 98% of plant P
demand (Cleveland et al. 2013), for pools that catch litter as a forest floor does, and 99.9% for designed pools.

**The two states.** A mature sky replaces its dead with recruits, so its stock holds and what must come in equals what
leaves. A growing sky also builds new bodies at the food web's growth rates, and their phosphorus is new stock that must
come in: its need is what leaves plus the change of its stock, the new bodies less the falls.

**The supplies.** Rain brings 0.004–0.1 g P per m² a year and caught dust 0.0003–0.8 g (the growth note's ranges); both
are land and sea P already on the Moon. The canopy nursery gives each juvenile its host's P once: 2.4–8.8 kg per 50-m
juvenile, at 0.002–0.05 recruits per adult a year (the growth note's design guesses). The author was unsure of this
route ("we'll have to guess here"), and here it stays an explicit design guess: 0.0006–0.06 g per m² a year for 100-m
bodies, under 0.0006 g for 1-km giants and under 0.00014 g for 2-km reefs. Sea-feeding flyers' droppings are the
balancing supply.

**Over 1% of the Moon, with the classes at their design shares (half in reefs, 30% in giants, 20% in lesser bodies),
the sky needs 3.8–60 kt of new phosphorus a year with forest-floor pools once mature, and 13–164 kt while it grows.**
Designed pools bring that to 0.26–50 kt and 9.4–154 kt; with nothing to catch the litter it is 67–382 kt and 76–486 kt.
Round giants recycle 38–63% alone and 89–97% with forest-floor pools in a mature sky (87–96% growing); lesser bodies
47–69% and 92–97.5% (74–92% growing). Falls, departing flyers and growth set the floor: in a mature sky the falls and the
departing flyers alone take 0.6–6.4% of a reef's throughput, so no body that dies and falls recycles the resources
model's 99.9%.

**The grazers' shortfall is the web's demand for droppings.** On host food alone the tenants that eat the host reach
7.9–41% of their carbon limit at the bounding edges ([food web](food_web.md)); reaching it takes 0.41–0.70 g P per m² a
year, more than a mature reef's whole need in the forest-floor case. The droppings of sea-feeding flyers, caught by the
pools, feed the reef's animals first.

**A handful of roosting flyers closes a giant's phosphorus.** An albatross-scale flyer of 1.7 t eats 36 kg of fish a day
and excretes 78 kg of P a year (Otero et al. 2018's seabird diet, 0.6% P wet, at its FMR); 30–60% of that at its roost
and 50–90% of the roost's droppings kept by the host (design guesses) bring 12–42 kg a year. With forest-floor pools a
mature 1-km round giant needs 10–170 kg of P a year, the droppings of 0.23–15 such flyers roosting on it year-round
(16–210 kg and 0.4–18 flyers while it grows); a 2-km reef 28–420 kg and 0.7–36 flyers (130–1,560 kg and 3–133 growing); a
100-m body 0.07–1.05 kg, a flyer roosting on it up to five weeks a year (up to 18 weeks while it grows).

**Flyers can close the sky's need.** With forest-floor pools a mature sky's 3.8–60 kt a year takes 91,000–5.1 million
roosting albatross-scale flyers (10,000–560,000 of the Quetzalcoatlus scale), eating 1.2–67 Mt of fish a year, 2–95% of
what Earth's seabirds eat (70 Mt, Brooke 2004). A growing sky's 13–164 kt takes 310,000–14 million (34,000–1.5 million),
eating 4–182 Mt, 6–260% of the seabirds' (*derived* on the screen's needs).

## The check against the resources model

**The resources branch's low case, 10,726 t of P a year to return to the sky, reproduces exactly from its own product.
With a reef's tissues and recycling it becomes 0.5–85 kt with designed pools and 8–104 kt with forest-floor pools in a
mature sky, 22–264 kt with either in a growing sky, and 137–737 kt with nothing to catch the litter.** The resources model
([resources/phosphorus.md](https://github.com/dgoldman0/Terluna/blob/domain/resources/resources/phosphorus.md), bound at
commit b47c452 by SHA-256) takes 1% of the Moon at 1,000 g C/m² a year of biomass at Redfield C:P: 9.23 Mt of P a year
through production, 5% harvested with 99% returned, 99.9% of the rest recycled, against a low upward supply of 2,655 t a
year from dust. Its export, [(1 − h)(1 − r) + h(1 − q)]F, is 13,381 t and its shortfall 10,726 t; this study's own
implementation of the formula gives the same to the kilogram. Redone with the P a reef's biomass carries per unit of
carbon (the biomass basis, as the resources and aerial models' 1,000 g C/m² is all dry biomass), 4.7–14.5% of Redfield's
in a mature sky and 4.3–12% in a growing one, the throughput is 431–1,340 kt or 395–1,100 kt a year. The formula's r
takes each case's recycling, every loss counted, onto the unharvested 95% of the throughput it applies to:

| Case | Mature: r | Mature: return needed | Growing: r | Growing: return needed | Against the published 10.7 kt |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Designed pools | 93.2–99.3% | 0.5–85 kt | 75.7–93.5% | 22–251 kt | 0.05–23 times |
| Forest-floor pools | 91.7–97.4% | 8.3–104 kt | 74.4–91.7% | 29–264 kt | 0.8–25 times |
| Nothing catches the litter | 42–66% | 137–737 kt | 34–62% | 140–689 kt | 13–69 times |

**The resources model's Redfield ratio overstates the sky's throughput 7–23-fold, and its 99.9% recycling is out of
reach once bodies fall, tenants leave and new bodies grow; the two partly offset.** At the low edge of a mature sky with
designed pools the low upward supply from dust covers most of the export; at the high edge the departing flyers and the
falls dominate, and in a growing sky the new bodies. The product's `flyer_need` gives the roosting flyers for these
needs on the resources model's production, beside `sky_need_flyers` on the aerophytes' own.

## The sea's return to the land

**Earth's seabirds carry 99 Gg of P a year from sea to land (Otero et al. 2018): 2.4–6.7% of the Moon's land loss of
1.47–4.16 Tg a year, and 53–141% of its dissolved part.** The land's loss is the people product's natural loss of
0.063–0.178 g per m² a year over 23.4 million km² of dry land, mostly particulate; its dissolved part, 0.003–0.008 g/m² a
year at Earth's rivers' rates (the lunar-cycle ecology register, H4), is 70–187 kt a year (*derived*). Sea-feeding
flyers that roost on aerophytes return P to the land through the droppings they leave at roosts over land, directly
or through their hosts' litter: the routes at 10 km spend 75% of their time over land in the 28% atlas.

| Return to the land through roosts | Albatross-scale flyers, 1.7 t | Their food against Earth's seabirds' | P in that food against the seas' yearly input |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Earth's seabird scale, 99 Gg a year | 2.8–5.6 million (4.9–9.7 Mt alive) | 0.52–1.05 times | 5–30% |
| The dissolved loss, 70–187 kt a year | 2.0–10.7 million | 0.37–2.0 times | 4–57% |
| A tenth of the whole loss, 147–416 kt a year | 4.2–24 million | 0.78–4.4 times | 8–126% |

**Flyers can return the land's dissolved loss and a few per cent of the whole; the eroded rest goes to the sea floor,
where the dredging of the lunar-cycle register (H10) recovers it.** A tenth of the whole loss would have the flyers eat,
at its high end, more phosphorus than the seas receive each year. Aerophytes carry the seabird island into the sky: a
drifting roost scatters the sea's phosphorus over the land beneath its track, where a colony on a cliff drops it within
50–320 m (Zwolicki et al. 2013).

## Where litter and falls land

**Passive aerophytes at 10 km spend their years near the equator and over the northern pole; at 20 km over both poles.**
The provisioning branch's zonal-wind product (bound at commit d9bc800) follows routes released 10° of latitude and 30° of
hour apart for ten years in the design run's 3-day mean winds, which superrotate above about 8 km and blow west only in
the lowest few kilometres; passive routes gather onto shared tracks within months to years. In years 5–10 the time at
each latitude, over the band's exact share of the Moon's area:

| Latitude band | 10 km | 20 km |
|---|---:|---:|
| 80–90° N | 3.4 times | 12.0 times |
| 60–80° N | 1.9–2.9 | 4.6–8.9 |
| 0–20° N | 1.3–1.8 | 0–0.01 |
| 0–20° S | 0.9–1.3 | 0 |
| 40–60° S | 0.4 | 0.4–1.3 |
| 70–90° S | 0.6 | 6.6–8.6 |

Litter falls within 6–81 km of its route along the wind and within 20 km across it ([food web](food_web.md)), inside
the product's 171-km cells. A route sweeps every longitude of its band as its hours turn, so over years its litter falls
on land and sea as the band divides them: spreading each band's time over its longitudes in the 28% atlas, 75% of the
10-km routes' time and 71% of the 20-km routes' lies over land. The twenty snapshots of the routes themselves, placed
with the Sun's track from the wind code's half-step frame, put 77% and 72% over land, single snapshots 59–92% and 49–85%; the
atlas and the climate model are taken to share their zero meridian.

**Sky snow returns a small share of the land's loss, and its phosphorus moves within the Moon.** At 1% cover with
forest-floor pools, the classes at their design shares drop 3.9–10 kt of P a year as litter: 0.18–0.48 mg per m² a year
beneath the 10-km routes just north of the equator and 0.35–0.91 mg over the north pole, and 0.9–3.2 mg over the poles
beneath the 20-km routes, against the land's loss of 63–178 mg. Without pools the sky drops 67–332 kt: 3.2–16 mg/m² just
north of the equator and 6–30 mg over the north pole at 10 km, and 15–105 mg over the poles at 20 km. Only the part that
flyers brought from the sea is a return; dust and rain P is land P moved.

**Sky reefs that sail spread the deposition.** A tethered wing sails a reef 30–70° of latitude in a lunar day (the
aerophyte sailing note), so a reef chooses where its litter falls; reefs spread by area would drop on every band its
share.

## Falls

**A 2-km sky reef falls with 6–18 t of P and 21,000 t of dry matter (9,500 t C, *derived*), laying 0.5–5.6 g P per m²
on the ground it drapes: 3–90 years of the land's natural loss there.** A deflated body drapes one to four times its
footprint (a design guess). A 1-km round giant falls with 3.6–17 t of P and 48,000 t of dry matter, 1.2–22 g P/m²,
6–350 years of the land's loss. A 40-t whale fall gives the 50 m² beneath it about 2,000 years of the deep sea floor's
background carbon flux (Smith & Baco 2003); a giant's fall is the land's whale fall, weaker per square metre and
spread over square kilometres; its tendons, like a whale's bones, decay for decades.

| At 1% cover (design shares: half in 2-km reefs, 30% in 1-km giants, 20% in 150-m bodies) | Falls a year | P to the ground | Ground draped | Falls a year if the cover held while every body grew |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2-km sky reefs, 60,000 of them | 60–300 | 360–5,400 t | 190–3,800 km² | 1,600–5,100 |
| 1-km round giants, 145,000 | 145–720 | 530–12,500 t | 110–2,300 km² | 450–1,100 |
| 150-m bodies, 4.3 million | 4,300–21,500 | 160–2,700 t | 76–1,500 km² | 91,000–270,000 |

The falls follow the growth note's adult hazard of 0.001–0.005 a year, as in a mature sky. A cover held at 1% while its
bodies spent their whole surplus on new bodies would need falls at the growth rate instead, 1.5–26 times as many, and the
new bodies' phosphorus would reach the ground with them. A given square kilometre of land lies under a reef fall every
8,000–160,000 years (*derived*).

## Checks

[test_phosphorus.py](test_phosphorus.py) checks:
- that stocks sum by tissue and stay below Redfield's, and that the throughput adds up in both states;
- that every loop closes: what is renewed is recycled, falls as litter, is harvested or leaves; the need is what leaves
  plus the change of stock; the grazers' frass and bodies hold what they ate;
- that the retention cases order the recycling in both states, and that growth adds exactly the new bodies less the
  falls;
- that the sky's need weights the classes, and that grazers cannot build more P than they eat;
- the grazers' P limit against the carbon limit;
- the resources formula, its reproduction of the published 10,725,774 kg from the bound product, and the mapping of each
  loop's recycling onto the formula's r;
- that the occupancy uses exact band areas and the deposition returns the flux it spreads;
- land shares on uniform, hemispheric and longitude-split atlases, and that the land share used is the band-weighted one;
- that the subsolar longitude follows the wind code's half-step frame and that route positions add the hour angle to it;
- the falls' P, fertilisation and frequency, and the sea-return arithmetic.
