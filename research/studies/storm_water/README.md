# Storm water on the drylands

1 November 2026, on `domain/hydrosphere`. PlaSim holds each land cell's water in one 0.5 m bucket that sheds water only when full, and on the design years the drylands keep that bucket 0.4–8% full, so the climate returns all of their rain to the air. The study works out what the drylands' storms would do instead. Phase 1 found how much of the rain runs off dry ground where it falls; phase 2a follows the wetted soil between storms and the runoff down the slopes and channels to the hollows, the sea and the soil; phase 2b runs the hollows' pools through the lunar day and night and finishes the leverage on PlaSim's land scheme, the biome maps' wet-patch layer and each surface's wear and salts. All three phases run offline on the design years `A28R_301k_design_l15_snow_s934`, years 10–19, following the plan of 1 November (`session-tools/agents/runoff/plan.md` on the research drive) and the register's direction of the same day ([the gap test's rules and the storm water](../../decisions.md#the-gap-tests-rules-and-the-storm-water-1-november-2026)): four designed surfaces side by side, young and mature crater hollows both worked out, no dam-building organisms, and the recharge reported beside the water table's 1% floor for dry land. [run.py](run.py) runs the three phases in about an hour and writes [results/storm_water.json](results/storm_water.json) (schema `terluna.research.storm-water-runoff/2`, its fields keeping their meanings, with phase 2b's added) and the wet-patch layer [results/wet_patches.json](results/wet_patches.json); no climate or cloud model runs.

## Phase 2b: the pools through the lunar night, the leverage, the wet-patch layer and the soils' wear

Phase 2b runs the water phase 2a delivers to the hollows through the design years in every fed depression of the 1.9 km grid and in the crater bowls below it, with each pool's evaporation from an energy balance through the lunar day and night, its seepage through a designed floor and its spill along the depression tree. From the pools it finishes the leverage on PlaSim's land scheme, writes the biome maps' wet-patch layer, and sets each surface's loss of soil to the storms and the salts its hollows keep.

The median depression's pool stands 0.12–0.61 m deep while wet under Walnut Gulch's storms on young land and reaches 0.4–1.7 m at its deepest; the deepest tenth reach 2.7–11 m.

- **The night costs a pool a few millimetres; its floor costs more.** A 0.3 m pool over the semi-arid land evaporates 49 mm in the lunar day's daylight and 6.6 mm through the 354-hour night, 56 mm in all against the register's hand estimate of about 60 mm *(model result)*. The design years' 10 m winds average 0.5 m/s at night and 1.0–1.4 m/s by day, so at noon the pool runs 8 K above the air and gives part of its sunlight back as longwave and sensible heat; at night it cools 1.7 K below the air and evaporates on the warm sky's longwave and the air's heat. Deeper pools move their loss into the night, a 3 m pool losing 36 mm by day and 13 mm by night. The clay seal passes 2.6 mm a day, 38 mm over the night alone, so seepage takes more of the stored water than evaporation does on every clay floor.

- **Most of the hollows' water goes into the ground.** On clay floors the depressions return 18–35% of their water to the air and pass 58–66% through their floors, the wall-fed bowls 33–41% and 52–62%, the channel-fed bowls 9–32% and 40–61%, with the rest stored or spilled *(model result)*. With sandy banks seepage takes 54–94%. The pools' seepage and phase 2a's beds together recharge 2.0–3.0% of the drylands' rain on the sandy loam and the crust under Walnut Gulch's storms, 7.5–11% on the loam, 18–29% on the sealed loam and 9–42% under CM1's storms, all above the water table's 1% floor for dry land and most above the 0.1–5% of Earth's semi-arid and arid study areas (Scanlon et al. 2006, via the plan). The Moon's crater bowls and closed depressions receive 51–96% of the runoff (phase 2a), and their floors pass most of it on.

- **Standing water covers 0.15–3.7% of the drylands.** Under Walnut Gulch's storms on young land and clay floors pools cover 0.48% of the drylands on the sandy loam, 0.54% on the crust, 1.4% on the loam and 2.8% on the sealed loam, 54,000–310,000 km², and 0.32–1.4% with sandy banks; CM1's storms give 1.6–3.7% *(model result)*. Most of that area lies in pools that last from one storm period to the next or stand, and it varies by 2–25% through the lunar day, peaking in the first tenths after sunset and least in the late morning. Mature land keeps 0.15–2.4%. The plan estimated 0.02–0.9%, about 18,000 km² at its centre; the chain's pools cover 3–17 times that central area under Walnut Gulch's storms, because the bowls below the grid hold water from one storm period to the next and the lunar floors drain slowly.

- **The bare soils wear faster than soil forms; the biological crust holds.** The storms detach 0.13–0.28 mm a year from the bare sandy loam, loam and sealed loam over the drylands, 0.14–0.31 mm in the semi-arid land and 0.31–0.63 mm in the dry sub-humid, against soil formation of 0.017–0.036 mm a year, so 85–93% of the drylands lose bare soil faster than it forms *(model result on Earth's bare-cropland erodibilities, an upper bound)*. The lunar storms detach 0.6–3% of what the same runoff would detach on Earth: the drops strike with 0.143 of Earth's energy, and the deeper, slower sheet's shear, 0.28 of Earth's at the same discharge, stays below the loam's critical shear in every event class of Walnut Gulch's storms, so only the sandy loam's largest events cut rills. The crust loses 0.013 mm a year, below formation over 68–70% of the drylands and above the faster rate only on 5–7%, chiefly the wettest dry sub-humid land.

- **Few hollows turn saline within centuries; soda pans need millennia.** With rain and dust as the only solutes, 250–1,150 of the depressions turn their floors saline within a thousand years, 0.3–1.2% of them, and 8–33% within ten thousand on clay floors, 4–7% with sandy banks *(model result on design inputs)*. Seepage carries most of the solutes down, the more so through sandy banks. Among the bowls, 0.2% of the wall-fed ones turn saline within a thousand years, the same 5 million young bowls under every surface and library; the dust's solutes make the strongest waters where they dissolve in the least rain. Up to 54% of the mature land's channel-fed bowls turn saline within ten thousand years on clay floors. The calcite, magnesium clays and opal cement the floors at a median of 35–250 mm a millennium in the depressions and wall-fed bowls on clay floors, and up to 1.2 m in the mature land's channel-fed bowls, which gather the most water.

- **Both Earth checks fail their pre-set rules, and both failures point to the pools' air.** At Lake Mead the neutral bulk law with the measured water-surface temperature, vapour pressure and mean wind gives 114 W/m² of latent heat against the Bowen-ratio energy budget's 164 W/m², a ratio of 0.69 against the rule's 0.75–1.25 *(model result against a measurement; Moreo and Swancar 2013)*. The shortfall is the same in the months the lake is cooler than its air (0.68) as in those it is warmer (0.73), so the stratification does not cause it; the lake's latent heat implies a transfer coefficient of 1.74 × 10⁻³ at its sensors' heights against the chain's 1.19 × 10⁻³. The two remaining candidates are the wind's variation within each month, which the flux follows as the product of wind and vapour deficit, and the scalar roughness, an Earth value with a wide spread; neither was tested, and no parameter was changed. The night check passes its night, 6.6 mm against the rule's 2.5–30 mm, and fails its day: a 0.3 m pool evaporates 0.70 of Priestley and Taylor's demand at water's albedo against the rule's 0.8–2.0, because the design years' weak winds let the pool run hot. With the transfer raised by Lake Mead's factor, a calibration kept out of the check, the day comes to 0.81 of the demand and the drylands' leverage moves by under a point.

### How it works

#### The pools' shapes

[phase2b.py](phase2b.py) gives every hollow an area–volume curve. Geography's depression tree, which phase 2a's grid now keeps for this phase, gives each closed depression its own pass, the higher of the two cells across the lowest point of its rim, and the depression its overflow enters. Geography's heights are point values on the 1.9 km cells, so below the pass the area rises linearly from the floor cell's height through each next cell's height, a cell's whole area flooding when the water reaches the cell above it in height order *(design choice)*. A pool smaller than its floor cell's part of that rise stands as a wedge whose area grows as the square root of its volume. The curve keeps each depression's lowest 16 cells; 15,101 of the 96,794 depressions have more below their pass, and their walls stand vertical above the sixteenth cell up to the volume at the pass. The crater bowls below the grid are paraboloids, Pike's fresh form at half its depth, as phase 2a and the terrain study have them *(design choice)*, so their area too grows as the square root of their volume.

In each T21 cell and diameter bin a wall-fed bowl takes its own walls' runoff and the sheet its rim catches from the slope above, in phase 2a's shares by area and by width. A channel-fed bowl adds the capture of the channels crossing it: phase 2a's routing now splits that capture over the bins by each bin's crossings and the share of a flow each takes, and the channel-fed bowls number the crossings along the cell's reaches, one bowl to a crossing. Every pool also receives the rain falling on its own surface beyond the runoff already counted there.

#### Evaporation by day and by night

The design years give each dryland cell's air by tenth: sunlight down from the net shortwave and PlaSim's surface albedo, longwave down from the net longwave and the ground's emission at an emissivity of 1, the air at 2 m, the specific humidity of the lowest model level (σ = 0.994, about 309 m up), the surface pressure, and the mean speed of each 3-day mean's 10 m wind; the extract now keeps the albedo and that humidity. A pool and 0.3 m of its wet floor (2.0 MJ/m³ per K) share one temperature *(design choice)*. They absorb sunlight at water's albedo of 0.07, exchange longwave at water's emissivity of 0.97, and lose latent and sensible heat by the neutral bulk law between the 10 m wind and the 2 m air, with Charnock's roughness at lunar gravity, z0 = 0.011 u*²/g + 0.11 ν/u*, and a viscous scalar roughness of 0.1 mm *(Earth values; the roughness's gravity a calculation)*. Waves of a given stress stand taller under a sixth of the gravity, so the water's roughness rises about threefold, and the transfer coefficient by about a tenth. The balance runs hourly through three lunar cycles at mean depths from 1 cm to 10 m, each tenth's forcing held through the tenth, and each pool reads its evaporation at its own mean depth. With no heat stored the balance is Penman's evaporation in exact form, which the tests confirm. With storage a deep pool carries the day's heat into the night and evaporates on it, and once a pool has cooled below the night air, the air's sensible heat and the warm sky's longwave feed what it evaporates.

#### Seepage through the floors

The clay-floored case lines every pool with a 5 cm clay seal at 2.8 × 10⁻⁹ m/s on Earth over a sandy loam *(design choice)*. The seal's Darcy flux, driven by the water's depth and the suction beneath the seal, equals the subsoil's drainage under gravity at that suction on Brooks and Corey's curve. On Earth the seal passes 5.6 mm a day under 0.5 m of water, the order of the HAPEX-Sahel plateau pools, which lose under half of their water to seepage *(Desconnets et al. 1997, abstract, via the plan)*. Conductivities scale with g and suction heads with 1/g, so on the Moon the water's weight drives a sixth as much flow while the capillary pull below the seal, the same in pressure, drives the same: the lunar seal passes 2.3–4.3 mm a day under 0.1–3 m of water, 0.25–0.58 of Earth's 4.0–17 mm *(calculation, tested)*. The sandy-banked case keeps the seal below a stage of 1 m, the lower end of the HAPEX-Sahel pools' 1–2 m threshold above which most stored water infiltrates, and gives the banks above it the channels' sand, Lane's class 2 at 3 in/h on Earth, which passes 303 mm a day at lunar gravity.

#### Fill, spill and persistence

Each pool runs through the 1,200 means in steps of half a mean, 35 hours. A mean's inflow follows its cell's runoff in that mean, scaled so that each tenth's inflow over the years is phase 2a's, and the volume advances implicitly in √V, which is exact for a paraboloid drying under constant losses. A depression filled to its pass spills into the depression below it in the tree at the next step; water leaving the drylands' depressions, and the rare spill over a bowl's rim, counts as passed on. The first design year fills the pools and years 2–10 give the statistics. A pool holds water while its deepest point stands at 1 cm or more, and three checks in each lunar day place it in a class: at sunset, where the storm period ends; at sunrise; and at the end of the morning, before the next storms. A pool dry at sunrise in most lunar days dries within its storm period or the night after it (the product's `dry_within_the_storm_period`); one wet at sunrise in at least half of them lasts the night; one wet before the next storms in at least half lasts from one storm period to the next; one that never dries after the first year is standing.

#### The leverage

With the pools, the drylands' evaporation differs from the bucket's by what the pools evaporate less what leaves the slopes' soil: phase 2a's water to the hollows, the sea and the humid land with its recharge, and the rain that falls on the pools. The bucket would have returned that water within the lunar day, at a timing only PlaSim can give. The chain therefore takes it off either in the tenth it runs off, an upper bound on any tenth's change, or spread over the tenths as the bucket's own evaporation is, while the pools give back what they evaporate in the tenth they evaporate it. The plan's thresholds stand: a change of 5% of the drylands' yearly evaporation, or of 10% in any tenth.

#### What sustains or wears the slopes

WEPP's baseline erodibilities for soils of 30% sand or more (Alberts et al. 1995, read in a search summary of WEPP's documentation) give each surface's interrill erodibility Ki = 2,728,000 + 192,100 VFS kg s/m⁴, rill erodibility Kr = 0.00197 + 0.00030 VFS + 0.03863 e^(−1.84 OM) s/m and critical shear τc = 2.67 + 0.065 clay − 0.058 VFS Pa, in percentages. The sandy loam has 65% sand, 10% clay and 15% very fine sand, the loam 40%, 20% and 10%, each with 1% organic matter *(design choices)*; the sealed loam keeps the loam's values, and the biological crust binds the sandy loam to a tenth of its erodibilities and three times its critical shear *(design choice, after recalled measurements)*. Each of phase 2a's event classes on the 100 m slope detaches Ki κ I σ by raindrops, with the drops' energy factor κ = 0.143, the rain rate I and the runoff rate σ, and Kr (τ − τc) in rills 1 m apart. The rills' width follows WEPP's 1.13 Q^0.303 *(recalled)* and their depth Manning's law with gravity explicit, so that at the same discharge the lunar flow stands 1.71 times as deep under a sixth of the gravity and its shear is 0.28 of Earth's *(calculation, tested)*. The critical shear of a cohesive soil is a strength and stays Earth's. The loss is detachment-limited on bare soil, an upper bound, and it is set against the project's natural soil formation of 0.017–0.036 mm a year (Montgomery 2007, as `biosphere/ecology/people.py` has it).

#### The salts

Rain from the fresh seas carries no sea salt; it brings 2 mg/L of continental solutes, and the dust's soluble part, 0.5 g/m² a year, dissolves in the year's rain, so the runoff carries 4 mg/L where 250 mm falls. Sodium and potassium make 2% of the solutes, the weathering share the [sea chemistry](../sea_chemistry/README.md) study gives *(design inputs)*. Each pool takes in its water's solutes, loses them with its seepage and its spill at its own concentration, and leaves the rest on its floor when it dries. Calcite, magnesium clays and opal precipitate first and cement the floor; the sodium and potassium stay as soda. A floor turns saline when the soda it keeps reaches 0.31 kg/m², the salt of a saturation extract at 4 dS/m in the top 0.3 m of a soil holding 0.4 of water.

#### The wet-patch layer

[results/wet_patches.json](results/wet_patches.json) (schema `terluna.research.storm-water-wet-patches/1`) gives for each T21 dryland cell, under every library, surface, hollow age and floor, the share of its dryland under standing water by tenth and by persistence class, in millionths, with the product's producer, evidence and reading rule. The ignored `research/runs/storm_water/wet_patches_2b.npz`, which run.py rebuilds and whose sha256 the layer records, gives for each 1.9 km cell a grid depression's pool covers the covered share by tenth and the pool's class; the bowls below the grid cover every 1.9 km cell of their T21 cell alike. Its numbers become final when the GCM and CM1 agree and the drylands' own storms replace the stand-ins.

### Limits

- The pools' air is the design years' by tenth. Their winds are the speeds of 3-day means, 0.5–1.3 m/s over the semi-arid land, which smooth away the gusts and turns PlaSim's own fluxes feel, so the pools' aerodynamic evaporation is a lower bound; the humidity is the lowest model level's, about 309 m up, taken as the surface layer's; and the transfer is neutral, which lunar gravity favours, since the same fluxes leave a sixth of Earth's buoyancy. Lake Mead's check finds the law's coefficient 0.69 of what that lake's latent heat implies, and the sensitivity case above shows what the factor would change.
- The bucket's timing is unknown offline. The verdict holds under both timings the chain brackets, and PlaSim's own response to a smaller bucket is stage (b)'s to find.
- The floors are designs. The seal sets most of the recharge and, through it, most of the yearly leverage; the tight-seal case above bounds that dependence. No measured lunar floor exists, and Earth's pool floors span orders of magnitude.
- A depression's curve below the grid's resolution is the linear rise between its cells, and each pool's inflow follows its floor cell's T21 runoff in each mean. The channel-fed bowls take one bowl to a crossing, and on mature land the channels' capture remains phase 2a's upper bound.
- The salts count the rain's and the dust's solutes only. Runoff that dissolves the soil's own minerals carries more, wind lifts salt from dry floors, and plants and microbes take some up; the soda share and the saline threshold are design inputs.
- The slopes' loss is WEPP's detachment on bare soil with Earth's cropland erodibilities, limited by neither transport nor cover. Plant cover, stones and consolidated developed soils lower it, often severalfold; a sandy soil's resistance that rests on its grains' weight would fall with gravity, which the cohesive critical shear here omits.
- The 1,936 depressions whose floors lie outside the drylands take their inflow out of the drylands' account.
- Two terms bias the clay floors' leverage toward a larger drop, and both are left for the next run of the chain. The leverage spreads the pools' evaporation over each T21 cell's whole area while phase 2a's fates count over the grid's dryland part of it, 0.89 of the area, which understates the pools' return by about a tenth (half a point a year on the loam, one on the sealed loam). And the channel-fed bowls, which phase 2a's routing lets each event fill afresh, stay full on clay floors and spill 4–40% of their inflow over their rims, water the chain counts as passed on instead of routing it to the hollows below; returned to the air in full it would lift the clay floors' yearly change by about 2 points on the sandy soils, 2–5 on the loam and 7–9 on the sealed loam *(estimate)*. The sandy banks spill little, and both corrections leave every verdict above unchanged.
- On clay floors the standing pools are still filling at the end of the record, by 2.5–136 km³ a year, 3–11% of their inflow, so their areas and evaporation have not reached their balance within the ten design years; sandy banks hold the pools to about a metre and balance within the record.

### Questions for the author

- **The floors.** Clay floors and sandy banks bracket the pools' fates; the seal's conductivity, a design choice scaled from the HAPEX-Sahel plateau pools, sets most of the recharge. Which floors the drylands' hollows should have, or whether they vary with the soils chosen for the biomes, decides the recharge and the water table's springs.
- **The water table's floor.** The hollows' recharge, 2–42% of the drylands' rain across the cases, exceeds the 1% floor everywhere. The register keeps the floor as it is; whether the recharge should replace it once the dry box's storms arrive is open.
- **Stage (b).** The leverage passes the plan's thresholds on the loams under both libraries and on every surface under CM1's storms, through the water the hollows send into the ground. Whether to build the land scheme's change now or after the dryland box's storms, which decide the sandy soils, is the author's call; the change the evidence points to is a surface store with seepage to a slow store (the plan's option b), since the pools' own evaporation matters little at T21.
- **The slopes' soils.** On Earth's bare-cropland erodibilities the bare sandy loam, loam and sealed loam lose soil several times faster than it forms over most of the drylands, while the biological crust holds over two thirds of them. Sustained dryland soils on slopes would then carry crusts, cover or both; the erodibilities of developed lunar soils are the measurement this rests on.
- **Soda pans.** A few hundred depressions and some millions of bowls turn saline within a thousand years, and up to half of the mature land's channel-fed bowls within ten thousand: the soda pans the register foresees as a biome of the closed basins.

### How it works

#### The wetting between storms

[wetting.py](wetting.py) carries the water each storm leaves in the topsoil as Smith, Corradini and Melone's (1993) rectangular profile, the form Ogden and Saghafian (1997) use between Green and Ampt storms: I mm of water above the soil's own content at a uniform saturation down to a sharp front. The front advances under gravity, at the layer's conductivity, and under the capillary pull of the drier soil below, Smith et al.'s profile factor p times the Brooks and Corey drive between the two contents over the front's depth. The surface dries at the smaller of the mean's Priestley and Taylor demand and the rate its desorptivity allows, S_d / (2√t), with S_d from the same curve (a sharp-front estimate). The next storm drives a new Green and Ampt front into the layer, with the deficit the layer has left and phase 1's front suction scaled by the drive from the layer's content to saturation; when it reaches the old front the two merge into one front carrying all the water into the dry soil. Each mean's storms fall at uniformly random times within its 2.95 days *(design choice)*, and the tables hold the expected runoff of exactly N storms for N up to 24 of Walnut Gulch's and 12 of CM1's, the Poisson mixture extrapolating beyond.

Gravity enters only through the conductivity (times g) and the bubbling pressure (over g). The capillary products, the diffusivity K dh/dθ, the drive, the desorptivity and with them the capillary spreading and the surface's drying are the same at any gravity; only the drainage under gravity falls to a sixth on the Moon *(calculation, tested)*. A wetted layer on the Moon therefore spreads almost as fast as on Earth while it is shallow and wet, and stays shallower once the capillary pull fades.

The curves are Rawls, Brakensiek and Saxton's (1982) class means as the GSSHA and SWMM5 tables reproduce them (read at that secondary): residual contents 0.041 and 0.027, bubbling pressures 146.6 and 111.5 mm and pore-size indices 0.378 and 0.252 (arithmetic means) for the sandy loam and the loam, and for the channels' sand 0.020, 72.6 mm and 0.694. Each curve's saturated conductivity is twice the Green and Ampt conductivity phase 1 uses, Rawls, Brakensiek and Miller's (1983) eq. 6 (read at the source), so that one soil underlies both models; the table's own 1.32 cm/h for a loam is twice that again. The range carries the bubbling pressure over one standard deviation of the class's suction either way (a factor of 4.1 for the sandy loam and 6.7 for the loam, Rawls et al. 1983, Table 2, read at the source), which sets both ends, and the profile factor at 2.0. Smith et al.'s factor of 1.7 is recalled, their paper not reached; with it the rectangular profile follows a numerical solution of Richards' equation for the same curves, at both gravities, to within 0.01–0.03 in surface saturation and a few millimetres in wetted depth over a day, with Richards' solution between the factors 1.7 and 2.0, while a factor of 1 misses by 0.04–0.05 *(calculation, tested)*. The pore-size index's spread was not found.

#### The routing

[routing.py](routing.py) and [phase2a.py](phase2a.py) route each surface's central runoff over geography's 1.9 km grid. `geography/drainage.py` now routes a supplied runoff field in place of the climatology's rain less evaporation (`compute(runoff=...)`, `--runoff`), keeping its default, with two new tests; the study supplies the loam's yearly runoff and takes the depression tree, the receivers and the drained areas back. Of the drylands' 6.16 million 1.9 km cells, 1.59 million lie below their depression's spill level, inside the grid's closed hollows; water arriving at any of them is delivered to that hollow. The other 4.57 million form a forest of slopes whose flow paths end in a hollow, the sea, or the humid land beyond the drylands.

Each storm's point runoff meets four things in turn.

- **The slope.** The excess runs 100 m down a slope of 0.05 *(design choices)* as a kinematic sheet under Manning's law with its gravity explicit, n = 0.05 on Earth *(an Earth value)* and 2.45 times that on the Moon: at the same discharge 1.71 times as deep and 0.58 times as fast *(calculation, tested)*. When the excess stops, the water still on the slope soaks into it at the soil's capacity while it drains over about half the equilibrium time; a slower, deeper sheet gives the slope longer to take it back.
- **The bowls below the grid.** The terrain study's saturation equilibrium, 1.54 D⁻² per km² with D in km at 0.69–3.9% of geometric saturation (its geometric middle, 1.6%), gives the bowls from 10 m to 1.9 km on young land and from 100 m on mature land, where the smaller ones have filled. They stand at half Pike's fresh depth of 0.196 D *(design choice: equilibrium craters are shallower)*. Water falling inside a bowl stays there; a sheet crossing one on its way down the slope fills it; and a channel crossing one loses up to the bowl's volume, the crossings Poisson along the path with a bowl's centre within half its diameter. Channels crossing bowls at random is the young land's picture; integrated drainage on mature land routes many channels around bowls, so the channels' capture is an upper bound there.
- **The channels.** Each reach runs to the steepest neighbour, 1.2 times the cells' distance *(design choice)*, in a bed w = 2.0 m (A / 1 km²)^0.5 wide *(design choice)*. Lane's (1983) method, NEH-4 chapter 19 (read at the source, eqs. 19-1 and 19-13 to 19-16), gives the reach's outflow a(x, w) + b(x, w) P above its threshold volume, with the unit channel's a = −0.00465 K D and k = −1.09 ln(1 − 0.00545 K D / P). The bed is Lane's class 2, clean sand and gravel at 3 in/h (2–5 in/h in his Table 19-1). The recast replaces K D, the depth a dry bed takes in during a flow, by K D times the ratio of the Green and Ampt depths the same dry bed takes in at the gravity in question and at Earth's (sand's front suction, 49.5 mm, Rawls et al. 1983): the capillary soaking stays Earth's and the drainage falls to a sixth, so a lunar bed takes in 0.72 of Earth's depth in a quarter-hour flow, 0.56 in an hour and 0.32 in ten hours *(calculation, tested)*. A flow's duration at a reach is its duration where it formed plus its travel from the farthest source at 1.5 m/s on Earth *(design choice)* times Manning's factor of 0.58 on the Moon.
- **The spread of a storm.** A storm makes its runoff over a core of 5 km²; where a reach drains more, one event brings it a core's worth and the reach sees proportionally more events. Syed et al. (2003, abstract) found that Walnut Gulch's storms mostly cover its 148 km² and that the core's coverage predicts the runoff; no core area was found, and the Walnut Gulch basin check below passes for cores of 3–9 km², so the check sets this value rather than testing it.

The storms enter as four classes of about equal runoff, drawn from each library's storms on the soil the design years give at storm time, and each cell's classes carry its central runoff in those shares. Every class falls on every dryland cell at once; the fates are summed per T21 cell where they happen and per depression where the water arrives, then weighted by each cell's events by tenth.

#### The recharge

The water the beds take in drains below the root zone, set at 1 m *(design choice)*, if the bed's yearly loss, spread over its area a lunar day's share at a time, reaches below that depth after a lunar day of redistribution and drying in sand at lunar gravity. Run-on soaks a few millimetres into the slopes, which the same calculation keeps within the root zone. The water standing in the hollows and seeping through their floors is phase 2b's.

### Limits

- Every routing parameter is a design choice or an Earth value: the slopes' length, gradient and roughness, the channels' width, sinuosity and bed, the flood wave's speed and the root zone. The core area alone is constrained, by the Walnut Gulch basin, and Lane's coefficients are regressions on Earth's channels, recast for gravity by a calculation that no lunar measurement can test.
- The bowls catch channel flow wherever a channel crosses one at random. That is the young land's picture; on mature land integrated drainage routes many channels around bowls or through breached rims, so the channels' capture there (26–46% of the runoff under Walnut Gulch's storms) is an upper bound, and the grid's hollows would receive more of it. The bowls stand at half the fresh depth, and their capacity, 1.5–8.9 m of water spread over the whole land (3.7 m at the central count), never limits what one storm period brings. Their count is the terrain study's equilibrium share at its geometric middle; across its range of 0.69–3.9% of geometric saturation the land inside bowls spans 8.5–40% on young land and 4.8–24% on mature land, against 19% and 11% here, and the bowls' share of the runoff moves with it. The routing was not rerun at the range's ends.
- Each event class falls on every dryland cell at once, and each loss or delivery counts in the T21 cell where it happens. Flows crossing a T21 border put a little of one cell's runoff into its neighbour's account; over the drylands the fates sum to the runoff within 0.01%.
- The depression tree comes from `geography.drainage.compute` with its default climatology (run A) for the lakes' balance, where geography's committed drainage product uses the dimmer-shield climate. The heights, depressions, spill levels and receivers do not depend on it; the drained areas that set the channels' widths do, behind depressions that spill under that climate, and so does the lossless reference.
- Every hollow is terminal: water reaching one stays there in this phase. That holds while a hollow's inflow over a storm period stays far below its volume below the spill level, which phase 2b checks with its fill and spill.
- The wetting model keeps the soil of each mean from PlaSim's bucket and carries nothing from one mean to the next; the storms fall at random times within the mean's 2.95 days, against the demand averaged over the rainy means (3.1 mm a day). The large-scale rain falls on the mean's soil, as in phase 1's treatment of each storm on fresh soil.
- The recharge follows a rectangular profile in sand and in the slopes' soils, with roots drawing nothing below 1 m and the beds' water redistributing for one lunar day. Shallower roots or deeper, coarser alluvium would pass more; the floors of the hollows, where most of the water ends, are phase 2b's.

- **The leverage, finished.** The evaporation the pools return by tenth, set against the slopes' evaporation the bucket gave, decides whether the drylands' evaporation moves beyond the plan's thresholds; the recharge through the hollows' floors joins the beds' recharge beside the 1% floor.
- **The biome maps' wet-patch layer**: the pools' wet-area fraction and persistence class per cell.

### How it works

#### The design years

[extract.py](extract.py) reads the design run `A28R_301k_design_l15_snow_s934`, years 10–19, the 1,200 3-day means of its year files on the research drive, and classes each land cell as `session-tools/design_run/design_years.py` does: its mean over the ten tenths of the lunar day, each tenth the mean of the 3-day means whose hour angle falls in it (from `climate.crm.gcm_site`'s subsolar longitude), and its rain over Priestley and Taylor's demand on that mean's net radiation, air and pressure, in UNEP's aridity classes. It finds the same shares: 19.1% of the land arid, 25.1% semi-arid and 9.4% dry sub-humid, 841 T21 cells together, with hyper-arid land at 4.2%. For each dryland cell it keeps every mean's rain and convective rain, evaporation, bucket water, net sunlight and longwave, air at 2 m, ground, surface pressure, 10 m wind and surface heat fluxes, with the tenth each mean falls in, in an ignored `research/runs/storm_water/design_years.npz` whose sidecar records the sha256 of each year file. `composite()` gives every field by tenth and over the day as `design_years.py` composites them; the pools of phase 2 read the radiation, air and wind from it.

#### The storms

[storms.py](storms.py) holds the storm libraries, each a set of point hyetographs at one-minute steps with weights, and the library is the input a later product replaces. A cell's 3-day mean of convective rain, D, brings a Poisson number of the library's storms to each point, with mean D over the library's mean storm depth; the expected runoff of the mean is the Poisson mixture of the runoff of that many storms.

- **Walnut Gulch** is a two-parameter family fitted to measured point storms. Each storm rises linearly to its peak over 5 minutes *(design choice)* and decays exponentially with a time constant τ, and storm depths are exponential with mean μ. Fitting the eight numbers of Osborn's (1983) Table 1, the seasonal counts and mean durations of point rain above 50, 100, 150 and 200 mm/h for a season of 203 mm of thunderstorm rain, gives μ = 6.4 mm and τ = 7.9 minutes. The fit gives 6.4, 1.3, 0.26 and 0.05 such events a season against the measured 4.6, 0.94, 0.29 and 0.06, lasting 6.7, 4.8, 4.1 and 3.6 minutes against 7.0, 4.3, 3.0 and 2.4. #### Infiltration on the four surfaces

[infiltration.py](infiltration.py) steps each storm in one-minute steps with Green and Ampt's capacity taken at each step's midpoint; rain first fills a 1 mm surface store *(design choice)*, the excess runs off, and the store soaks in after the rain. A crust is a thin layer of its own resistance over the soil, Green and Ampt's two-layer form, and the steady-rain test against Green and Ampt's exact solution holds within 0.5% at both gravities. Gravity enters through two terms. Every conductivity is k ρ g / μ and scales with g, every resistance with 1/g, and every suction head, a capillary pressure over ρ g, with 1/g; the product of conductivity and suction, and with it the sorptivity, stays the same at any gravity, while the time for gravity to take over from capillarity grows 36-fold *(calculation, each tested)*. The drops' energy per millimetre of rain scales with v², with g / ρ_air at a fixed drop size as the plan derives it, evaluated at the drylands' rain-weighted surface pressure; atmosphere/rain's solved fall speeds in the resort's air give 0.11–0.17 of Earth's energy for 2–4 mm drops, 0.137 at 3 mm against the scaling's 0.138 at the same pressure.

### Limits

- The runoff is the infiltration excess at a point on uniform ground. Run-on soaking into the slope below, the spread of storms over part of a cell and the channels' losses all lie in phase 2.
- The CM1 storms stand on wet ground at the equator and follow each core's wettest column; the Walnut Gulch storms are Earth's. Neither library is the drylands' own storms.
- The design years' numbers become final when the GCM and CM1 agree; `run.py --extract` rereads them.
- Water's viscosity and surface tension stay at the texture classes' reference temperature. Water at 30 °C, near the drylands' afternoon air, flows about a quarter more freely, which would raise every conductivity by that much on both worlds.
- The drops' energy factor holds at a fixed drop size. Lunar rain keeps larger drops, which survive breakup at lunar gravity, and at 4 mm the rain solver's speeds give 0.165 of Earth's energy per millimetre; the forming seal's sensitivity over 0.09–0.17 covers that range.
