# Storms, lightning and hydrogen fire

**A soft floater rides a storm's updraft and cannot fight it, so storms are escaped by route, hour and latitude:
they rain in the afternoon of the tropics, cover 1.4% of the equatorial ring at any moment, and flash only by day.**
A body whose gas fills 64% of its envelope at 10 km keeps rising with the air until it is full at 37–41 km, in the
cold above the 25.7 km freezing height and inside the electrified layer at 30–42 km. Holding height against the
storms' 90th-percentile updraft of 4.5 m/s takes 3.5–9 kg/m² of extra weight, about the whole free-water store, and
against the strongest at 10 km, 9.8 m/s, 17–43 kg/m². All numbers are *screen* values (`storms` in the product) from
the CM1 equatorial ring, the electrified box's lightning and the GCM air.

## Updrafts, downdrafts and venting

**A partly filled body has no restoring force until it is full.** Its gas expands as it rises, and it rides the air
to the height where its volume has grown by 1/f. From 10 km a body at 95% fill is full at 13–14 km, at 80% at
24–26 km, at 64% at 37–41 km and at 50% at 51–57 km; the lower height holds for gas at the air's temperature,
(T/T₀)(p₀/p) = 1/f, and the higher for gas cooling on its own adiabat, (p₀/p)^(1−κ) = 1/f with κ = 2/7. The ballonet
margin is the altitude a storm can take the body before it must vent.

**Venting to stay full at a fixed volume costs 1.6% of the gas per kilometre of forced rise: 7.8% over 5 km and 15%
over 10 km.** That is 1 − ρ(z)/ρ(10 km) for gas at the air's temperature, from the GCM profile; gas cooling on its own
adiabat vents 1.4%, 6.7% and 13%. At 10 km the air's pressure scale height is 52 km and its density scale height
62 km. A full body that keeps its gas instead holds a superpressure that grows by 1.6–1.9 kPa per kilometre at 10 km,
the lower figure with the gas cooling with the air and the higher with it holding its temperature (ρgΔz); only
giants' structures carry that.

**The storms' updrafts are gentle by Earth's measure and broad.** At 10 km the ring's vertical wind stays under
1.1 m/s 99% of the time, with updrafts to 9.8 m/s and downdrafts to 7.8 m/s; the strongest updraft of a storm is
1.2 m/s at the median, 4.5 m/s for the strongest tenth and 15.6 m/s at most. A downdraft at 7.8 m/s would carry a
body from 10 km to the ground in 21 minutes, but downdrafts spread out above the surface.

**Kept rain is a brake.** The median storm's 3.7 mm/h, held in the body's stores, adds enough weight to hold it
against a 1 m/s updraft in 3–7 minutes and against 4.5 m/s in one to two and a half hours; the strongest tenth's
18 mm/h does it five times faster. Lunar raindrops of 2 mm fall at about 2.5 m/s, so a 4.5 m/s updraft carries the
rain up with the body, and the surface rate overstates what it catches there. The same water must be dumped again
when the downdraft comes.

## Rain surges

**Storm rain must be shed as fast as it falls.** The heaviest sampled storm rained 68.5 mm/h: held for six minutes
it adds 6.9 kg/m² and sinks a body at 6.2 m/s, and held for an hour 68.5 kg/m² and 19.7 m/s. Shedding 70 kg/m² an
hour, about 19 g/m² a second, keeps the body level; the median storm's 3.7 mm/h needs only 4 kg/m² an hour. Water-
shedding upper skins and overflowing catchments do this; the catchment keeps what the trim rule allows
([water](water.md)).

## Hydrogen, air and flame

**Hydrogen burns in air between 4% and about 75%, and in the lunar air between 4% and about 71%.** Upward
propagation in dry air spans 4.0–75.0% (Coward & Jones 1952), and no hydrogen–nitrogen–air mixture propagates flame
below 4.9–5.0% oxygen (Coward & Jones 1952; Zabetakis 1965). The upper limit is therefore 1 − 0.05/x_O₂: 76% in
Earth's 21% oxygen and 71% in the Open Moon's 17.5%. The lifting gas at 98% hydrogen holds 0.35% oxygen and cannot
burn. A spark of 0.017 mJ ignites the most sensitive mixtures, at 22–26% hydrogen (Ono & Oda 2008).

**A hole's flammable zone reaches at most 240–380 hole diameters: 0.2–0.4 m from a 1-mm pinhole, 2.4–3.8 m from a 1-cm
puncture and up to 240–380 m from a 1-m tear.** A round jet's centreline mass fraction falls as Y_exit/Y = C_Y x/D_ef
with D_ef = d sqrt(ρ_gas/ρ_air); C_Y is 0.208 for a hydrogen round jet and 0.31–0.33 for jets from an orifice in the
side of a tube (Soleimani nia et al. 2020, tabulating Schefer et al. 2008). The 4% limit is a hydrogen mass fraction
of 0.003, and the lifting gas leaves at 0.77. The jet law holds while the jet's momentum drives it, over the momentum
length M^(3/4)/B^(1/2): 0.2–0.3 m for a 1-mm hole, 0.55–0.95 m for a 1-cm hole and 5–9 m for a 1-m tear at 100–300 Pa
of superpressure in lunar gravity. Beyond it the light gas rises as a buoyant plume and dilutes faster, so the reaches
of punctures and tears are upper bounds, likely several times too long. Pinholes make a mixing zone the size of a
hand, and tears make zones metres to tens of metres long.

**Oxygen creeps in through the barrier, and microbes can keep it out.** Permeating oxygen, driven by its 17.5 kPa
partial pressure, would bring an unscrubbed raft of 40-m modules (a 24-m gas column behind 4.5 m² of skin and
partition per square metre) to 5% oxygen in 70–350 years, a 200-m sphere (a 133-m column behind 5 m²) in 350–1,700
years and a 2-km sphere in 3,500–17,000 years, for oxygen permeabilities of 0.1–0.5 of hydrogen's. A lining of
hydrogen-oxidising microbes burns two hydrogen for each oxygen that enters, 3.6–18% more hydrogen than permeation
already costs, and keeps the gas far above its upper limit for the body's life. The permeability ratio is a design
guess anchored by chitin's 0.25 ([envelope](envelope.md)).

## Lightning

**A 200-m floater is struck 0.02–0.13 times in 300 years over the Moon's mean storms and up to 1.4 times over the
summit's convective cells; a 2-km floater 0.9–2.1 and 2.3–23 times.** The Moon averages 0.0043–0.0079 flashes per km²
a year, the summit port's cells 0.011–0.083 and the polar plain 0–0.0001 (the joint synthesis); 20% of the
electrified box's flashes struck the ground, and none flashed in the lunar night. A floater at 10 km lies below the
in-cloud flashes, which start at a median 35.8 km, so the channels that reach it are ground strikes. The rates count
those passing within the body's radius plus a striking distance of 50–200 m (a stated range; the low ends take
50 m); a body that triggers its own flash in a storm's field adds to them, and the products cannot rate that.

**Lightning's hazard falls to nearly nothing by night, at the poles and outside the afternoon storms.** Flashing
storms are a tenth of storm cores and flash for a median of 0.4–0.6 hours at their peak (the electricity study's
storm lives). A floater that leaves the afternoon sector's storm cores, or lives at the poles, meets lightning rarely.

## Fire and gas cells

**One burning gas cell releases 120 MJ per kilogram of hydrogen, and wet skins and small cells keep the fire there.**
A hydrogen flame radiates 3–16% of its heat (Bernardy et al. 2025, summarising the literature). A 40-m module's 1.75 t
burning beside a neighbour 40 m away gives it 0.3–1.7 MJ/m² of radiant heat, while a skin holding 0.5 kg/m² of water
needs 1.2 MJ/m² to dry before it can burn and one holding 2 kg/m² needs 4.9 MJ/m² (latent heat alone; warming the
water to boiling adds about 15%). With cells sized for water-dropping, the skin water that absorbs a burning cell's
radiant heat at twice the cell's radius (radiant share 0.1; the dose does not depend on the burn time) is 0.25–0.65
kg/m² in cellulose and 300 MPa round bodies up to 500 m, 0.62–0.75 kg/m² in collagen bodies of 150–200 m, 0.7–2.8
kg/m² in 1–2 km bodies and 0.62–1.0 kg/m² in colony modules of 60–150 m. Every coupled body carries it, at least 0.5
kg/m², as mass ([structure](structure.md)). The model radiates the fire from the cell's centre; skin that adjoins the
burning cell, one radius away, would take four times the dose, and carrying that water moves the smallest round body
to 60 m and the smallest colony module to 100 m. Hydrogen fireballs rise, so neighbours beside a burning module in a
one-layer raft meet radiation, while neighbours above it in a stacked cluster meet the flame itself.

**The safe giant is a colony of wet modules, or a round body of many small wet cells.** A strike burns the modules or
cells it reaches; the colony drops water for them and regrows them, and a round body drops water for its cell and
lives because the fire stops at the wet skin. Walls between cells hold hydrogen on both sides and cannot burn from
within. A shared, dry skin lets fire run from cell to cell, which is how a body is lost.

## The storm design, in short

- **Route:** live poleward of the storm belt, on the night side or outside the afternoon storm sector (5–115° past
  noon); the polar plain sees 0–0.0001 flashes per km² a year.
- **Margin:** keep a ballonet margin of 5–20% (3–16 km of free rise from 10 km) and accept venting only in the
  rare storm that carries the body further.
- **Water:** shed 70 kg/m² an hour at the skin, keep storm rain only within the trim, and use kept rain as a brake.
- **Cells:** at least supported mass ÷ dumpable water of them ([structure](structure.md)), with skins holding
  0.5–2.8 kg/m² of water, and a microbial lining that keeps the gas free of oxygen.
