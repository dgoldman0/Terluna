# The south sea as an ocean: its salt, its water and its rivers

15 November 2026, on `domain/hydrosphere`. The author asked to test the viability and the cost and benefit of making the south sea, the South Pole–Aitken Sea, an Earth-like ocean, the more so if it connects to brackish and fresh waterways, which would also give eels and other fish that hatch in salt water their sea. The request followed a conversation on why the Moon's waters turn to hard water and soda lakes, kept on the research drive (`conversation-exports/chatgpt-salt-seas-2026-11-15/`). The fresh seas and the leaning toward aquariums for saltwater life are the register's rows of [15 October](../../decisions.md#salt-water-and-the-fresh-seas-15-october-2026); the [seawater lakes](../seawater_lakes/README.md) of 6 November are the smaller alternative this study is weighed against.

This first stage answers what the project's products already settle: how much salt the sea holds, whether its water balance lets it keep that salt, which rivers reach it, what its floor takes, and how salt changes its mixing. [inflows.py](inflows.py) repeats geography's river routing under three rules (3.5 minutes, 5 GB) and writes [results/inflows.json](results/inflows.json) (schema `terluna.research.south-sea-inflows/1`). [run.py](run.py) reads it with the design years' climatology, the atlas and geography's crust uptake, in seconds, and writes [results/south_sea_ocean.json](results/south_sea_ocean.json) (schema `terluna.research.south-sea-ocean/1`). Where the salt comes from is worked out on `domain/resources` (`resources/sea_salt.md` there) and summarised below. A second stage the same day reads two costs off existing products, [what a salt sea does to its shores and its air](#what-a-salt-sea-does-to-its-shores-and-its-air), and a third sets [the ocean model on the sea](#the-circulation-run) and starts its run. The first two stages ran no ocean or climate model. What remains is named under [what is open](#what-is-open) with what each needs.

## What it finds

**The sea holds 174,000 Gt of salt at seawater's strength, 55% of it chloride** *(computed)*. The sea covers 2.32 million km², 6.1% of the Moon, from 22° S to 87° S today and from 14° S to 82° S on the turned Moon. It is 2.1 km deep on average and 6.7 km at its deepest, and its 4.84 million km³ are 53% of the six large seas' water. At seawater's make-up of 35.165 g/kg and 29.5 °C:

| | Chloride | Sodium | Sulphate | Magnesium | Calcium | Potassium | Bicarbonate | Minor ions | All salt |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| g per kg of sea water | 19.35 | 10.78 | 2.71 | 1.28 | 0.41 | 0.40 | 0.14 | 0.08 | 35.165 |
| In the sea, Gt | 95,650 | 53,300 | 13,410 | 6,350 | 2,040 | 1,970 | 690 | 400 | 173,800 |

A sea at half that strength, 17.6 g/kg like the Black Sea's surface *(recalled)*, holds 85,800 Gt, and one at a fifth, 7.0 g/kg like the central Baltic *(recalled)*, 34,100 Gt. Magnesium, calcium and sulphur are plentiful in lunar rock; chloride is the ion that has to be brought, and sodium comes with it (below).

**The sea loses 0.71 m of water a year to the air beyond its rain** *(computed on products)*. Over the model's water cells under the sea, which hold 96% of its area, the design years give 0.51 mm of rain a day against 2.45 mm of evaporation, at a mean surface of 30.2 °C. The loss is 1,640 km³ a year, 51,900 m³/s, and the whole sea's volume in 2,950 years. The climate lane carries the model's rain as uncertain by a factor of 0.75 to 2 against its cloud-resolving boxes; applied to the rain on the sea, the loss runs from 1,200 to 1,750 km³ a year. The sea is driest where it is coldest:

| Latitude on the turned Moon | 19° S | 30° S | 42° S | 53° S | 64° S | 75° S | 80° S |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rain, mm a day | 1.23 | 1.26 | 0.51 | 0.17 | 0.07 | 0.06 | 0.08 |
| Evaporation, mm a day | 2.86 | 2.94 | 2.81 | 2.42 | 1.66 | 1.21 | 0.97 |
| Surface, °C | 30.7 | 31.4 | 30.8 | 30.0 | 29.1 | 27.7 | 27.1 |

A sea that loses more water than its rain returns keeps its level only on what flows in, and keeps every gram of salt that flows in with it. The seawater lakes study found the easiest salt water to hold is a terminal lake in a dry belt, where every flow of water and salt runs inward and only evaporation leaves. The south sea is that setting at more than a thousand times the area of the largest lake that study sited.

**As the atlas draws the waters, almost no river reaches the sea; once the craters on the way overflow, rivers bring 61% of its loss, and by way of Moscoviense 176%** *(computed on products)*. geography's drainage counts every hollow below the common sea level as sea, so a river that ends in a crater below that level never arrives. [inflows.py](inflows.py) repeats the routing on the same design years under three rules:

| Rule for the waters below the common level | Rivers into the south sea | Share of its loss (over the rain range) | Its level, left to itself |
|---|---|---|---|
| Every hollow takes its rivers in at the common level, as geography's drainage has it | 26 km³ a year | 2% (1–2%) | falls 0.69 m a year |
| The six large seas take rivers in; other hollows fill by their own balance and spill | 998 km³ a year | 61% (57–83%) | falls 0.28 m a year |
| Only the Near-side and South Pole–Aitken seas take rivers in; the four smaller seas fill and spill too | 2,884 km³ a year | 176% (165–240%) | rises 0.54 m a year |

The first rule cannot hold for the hollows that matter. The four that take 5,500–32,900 m³/s are craters of 3–280 km² at the common level, and the water between that level and their rims, 800–2,000 km³, is two to six years of their rivers. Full, they stand 2.5–4.5 km above the common level and send their rivers on. Under the second rule three rivers then enter the sea on its north and east shores: 16,000 m³/s at 15° S, 174° W on the turned Moon, about the Mississippi's flow *(recalled)*; 6,300 m³/s at 41° S, 140° W; and 4,000 m³/s at 16° S, 171° E.

The third rule adds the far side's largest river system. It drains 2.1 million km² of the far side's wet tropics into the Moscoviense Sea at 59,000 m³/s, 83 times what that sea evaporates. Moscoviense spills 3,545 m above the common level. Below that rim, from about 3,270 m, its water joins the lowland to its south around the Freundlich–Sharonov basin *(the basin's name recalled)*, whose own hollows lie below the common level, and the two fill together: 1.31 million km³ over about 790 years of the system's surplus, 880 with the routing's budget closed, by the [drainage network study](../drainage_network/README.md). This stage first counted Moscoviense's own 272,000 km³ and 145 years. Its overflow runs south and enters the south sea at the third river's mouth, 16° S, 171° E, with 63,800 m³/s in all, a third of the Amazon's flow *(recalled)*.

**So the sea's level can be held by rivers alone, by choosing how much of the Moscoviense system's water goes south** *(reading of the computed values)*. Between 1,000 and 2,900 km³ a year can reach the sea against a loss of 1,640 (1,200–1,750). The balance point is about 640 km³ a year beyond the second rule's rivers, a third of what the Moscoviense route carries. Sending it means either letting Moscoviense fill to its rim, which makes it a lake 3.5 km higher and a quarter larger, or cutting its outflow at a chosen level and place; neither is designed here. Whatever the sea receives beyond its loss raises it, and at 1.35 km above the common level it would join the nearside ocean ([geography](../../../geography/README.md)), so the inflow has to be governed and its surplus sent elsewhere.

**The fresh water enters at the north, which is where brackish water would lie** *(reading; not modelled)*. All the large mouths fall on the sea's northern half, its warm and rainier end, between 15° S and 41° S on the turned Moon, and the far south receives no river. A terminal sea fed at one end grades from fresh at the mouths to its saltiest water at the far end: the Caspian runs from about 1 g/kg off the Volga to 13 g/kg in the south, and the Mediterranean from 36 to 39 g/kg eastward *(recalled)*. The rivers therefore give the sea its fresh and brackish waterways without a fishway: estuaries at three or four mouths, a river system of 2.5 million km² above the largest, and Moscoviense and the far-side lakes at its head. [The circulation run](#the-circulation-run) gives the reach at 30 km cells: about 4,500 km² of brackish water and 19,000 km² at 18–30 g/kg; the far south does not turn saltier than sea water.

**Evaporation would drive the sea's deep water, where cooling drives the fresh seas'** *(computed; the consequence is a reading)*. At 29.5 °C a gram of salt per kilogram weighs as much as 2.2 K of cooling. The sea's net evaporation salts a surface layer 100 m thick by 0.25 g/kg a year if nothing mixes it, the weight of 0.55 K of cooling each year, where the [sea oxygen study](../sea_oxygen/README.md) found the fresh sea's deep water renewed by cold spells of 0.5–2.9 K. The cold end at 80° S, 27.1 °C against 31.4 °C at 30° S, is 1.45 kg/m³ denser at equal salt, which 1.9 g/kg of extra salt at the warm end would offset. Surface water therefore grows dense over most of the sea, as in the Mediterranean and the Red Sea *(recalled)*. This stage read from that comparison that the deeps would stay ventilated, and [the circulation run](#the-circulation-run) did not bear it out: those seas renew their deep water through their straits, and a closed sea has none. Under the river plumes the opposite holds: river water is 26 kg/m³ lighter than the sea and caps it. Sea water also holds about a sixth less oxygen than fresh water at the same warmth (6.3 against 7.7 mg/L, the sea oxygen study's table). The run's dye gives the first estimate of the deep oxygen (below).

**The crust takes salt with the water it draws from the floor, a fifth of the sea's salt within 10,000 years at the central case** *(computed on products)*. geography's stage 1 follows the water the dry crust takes from under each sea floor. Under this sea, with what it carries at seawater's strength:

| Crust | By year 1,000 | By year 10,000 | By year 100,000 | Salt a year, years 1,000–10,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Slow | 24,000 km³, 0.5% of the sea | 195,000 km³, 4% | 1.12 million km³, 23% | 0.7 Gt |
| Central | 64,000 km³, 1.3% | 1.03 million km³, 21% | 2.53 million km³, 52% | 3.8 Gt |
| Fast | 503,000 km³, 10% | 2.48 million km³, 51% | 3.47 million km³, 72% | 7.9 Gt |

The salt goes down with the water and does not come back. Holding the sea at seawater's strength therefore takes the fill and then the crust's share on top, 37,000 Gt more by year 10,000 at the central case; if the water alone is replaced, the sea freshens by the same shares. Stage 1's columns are fresh water, and what salt does in the hot rock at depth is outside them.

**The rivers' own load is small.** At the held fresh sea's 0.14 g/kg, 1,640 km³ of river water a year bring 0.23 Gt of dissolved load, a millionth of the sea's salt, most of it calcium and magnesium carbonate that calcifiers lay down. The slow gathering of alkalinity in a salted sea is the [sea chemistry](../sea_chemistry/README.md)'s: pH 9 after 0.30–0.53 million years if left alone.

**For eels the sea offers what the lakes could not, with its warmth still to check** *(reading of published and recalled values)*. European eel eggs are activated and reared at 30–40 g/kg in water with sea salt's full mix of ions, and fertilisation failed in plain sodium chloride solutions; larvae from the fourth day tolerate half-strength water (Sørensen et al. 2016; Benini et al. 2022; Okamura et al. 2009; summaries, in the lookup on the drive). A sea at half strength would therefore not serve the spawning, while its brackish north serves the later larvae. The pressure of the eels' spawning depth, 150–200 m on Earth, lies 0.9–1.2 km down at lunar gravity (the seawater lakes study), which a sea 2.1 km deep on average has and no sited lake had. The open check is temperature: Japanese eel eggs hatch at 19–28 °C and none at 31 °C (the register's row of 6 November), and the sea's surface stands at 27–31 °C, so the spawning layer has to be the cooler water at depth or in the south.

**The salt has one source, the feed the project already plans to process, and whether it suffices rests on a grade nobody has measured** *(resources' calculation on `domain/resources`, summarised)*. Lunar ground cannot give the chloride: at the 7–70 ppm inferred for soils it would take 2–19% of the Moon's mass. Near-Earth asteroids cannot either: the whole population holds the chloride of at most 3,500 km³ of sea water, 0.07% of the sea. The chloride rides in the outer-system feed for the air and the water. If the air's nitrogen came from rock like CI chondrites, at 717 ppm of chlorine, that feed would carry 7.6 times the sea's chloride (1.2 times at the grade of one disputed 2017 study); if from ice like comet 67P's, whose gas alone has been measured, 13–93% of it, with the water's own chloride adding 4%. The rock that holds the sea's chloride, 1.3×10²⁰ kg, also holds 13 times its sodium. The salt is 1.3% of the standing water's mass and one to five years of the delivery stream.

**The other seas' balances do not close as drawn either** *(computed on products; outside the question asked)*. On the same reading:

| Sea | Net evaporation | Rivers in, six-seas rule | Share of its loss | If left to itself |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Near-side | 0.69 m, 3,660 km³ a year | 1,890 km³ a year | 52% (95% with the smaller seas spilling) | falls 0.33 m a year |
| South Pole–Aitken | 0.71 m, 1,640 km³ | 998 km³ | 61% | falls 0.28 m a year |
| Humboldtianum | 0.73 m, 209 km³ | 8 km³ | 4% | falls 0.71 m a year |
| Smythii–Marginis | 0.38 m, 108 km³ | 1,071 km³ | 10 times | fills to its rim, 767 m up, in 230 years |
| Orientale | 0.35 m, 31 km³ | 520 km³ | 17 times | fills to its rim, 2,342 m up, in 470 years |
| Moscoviense | 0.35 m, 23 km³ | 1,900 km³ | 83 times | fills to its rim, 3,545 m up, in 145 years |

The atlas's one common level is a storage geometry, as geography states. Under the design years' rain three of the six seas would rise to their rims within centuries and the other three would fall unless water is moved between them. That belongs to the atlas round; it is recorded here because the south sea's supply depends on it.

## What a salt sea does to its shores and its air

The second stage, 15 November 2026, reads two costs off products the project already holds. [shores.py](shores.py) takes the sea's own shore from the [coast-water study](../coast_water/README.md)'s 16 pixel/degree shores and applies that study's rules to it ([results/shores.json](results/shores.json), schema `terluna.research.south-sea-shores/1`). [spray.py](spray.py) drives a sea-spray source with the design year's 10 m wind as written every 6.5 hours and follows the salt to the ground on the same record's winds and rain ([results/spray.json](results/spray.json), schema `terluna.research.south-sea-spray/1`).

**The sea has 39,600 km of shore, and half of it lies in desert** *(computed on products)*. At 16 pixels per degree the mainland shore is 24,200 km and the sea's islands add 15,400 km, together 22% of the six large seas' shore. By the design years' rain at the model's land cell nearest each stretch:

| Rain behind the shore | Shore | Of it on islands |
|---|---|---|
| Under 0.5 mm a day, desert | 20,700 km | 7,600 km |
| 0.5–1 mm a day | 7,600 km | 4,800 km |
| 1–2 mm a day | 3,100 km | 600 km |
| 2 mm a day or more | 8,200 km | 2,400 km |

Every kilometre of shore poleward of 60° S on the turned Moon is desert, 14,200 km, with 3,900 of the 7,100 km between 45° and 60° S. The wet shores are the northern ones, where the rivers arrive.

**Along the desert shore a fresh sea feeds 4,000–6,800 km² of green rim, and a salt sea turns that ground to salt** *(computed with the coast-water study's rules)*. Plants there draw the sea's water through the ground, 1.9 mm a day in that study's deserts at 301 K. Under its six cases of ground and reach the belts along this sea's desert shore average 190–330 m in width, with a median strip of 160–290 m; they cover 4,400–6,100 km² at the central crust, hold 1,500–2,600 km² on the islands, and draw 2.8–4.9 km³ of the sea a year. That is about 28% of the belts the coast-water study counted on the Moon's desert sea and island coasts, on its own desert mask and the unturned Moon. From a sea at seawater's strength the same draw carries 100–175 Mt of salt a year under the roots, 25 kg onto each square metre of belt. By that study's rule for a saline root zone the soil water passes the threshold in 11 days; holding it fresh would take 9.9 m of water a year passing below the roots, where the desert's rain is 0.06 m; and left to dry, the salt builds 12 mm of rock salt a year. The desert rims of a salt sea are therefore salt ground. The coast-water study's tide run found that the monthly tide carries back a quarter to a third of a soluble load from the strip where high water lifts the water table near the surface, so salt-tolerant plants could hold in that strip and nowhere inland of it.

**The monthly high water covers 1,900 km² of shore** *(computed; the slopes are 2 km pixels')*. The typical monthly range along this shore is 2.0–3.6 m, 2.7 m at the median (geography's tide), and at the pixels' slopes its high water reaches a median 15 m inland and 88 m on the gentlest tenth of the shore. This is the ground a salt sea wets with sea water each month on every shore, wet or dry; 990 km² of it lies in desert and 780 km² on the islands. At 100 m the coast-water study found its belts 1.0–2.7 times narrower than this resolution gives, and the same caution holds for this strip.

**On the wet shores the land's own water holds the sea back** *(reading)*. Where the rain exceeds what the plants use, fresh groundwater flows to the sea and floats on the sea water beneath it, reaching 38 times as far below the sea's level as it stands above (the densities' ratio, *computed*). The 8,200 km of shore with 2 mm of rain a day or more keep fresh ground down to the high-water line. The 10,700 km between the two classes are undecided here: the plants' demand over the land is 2.9 mm a day in the coast-water study, more than that rain, so part of their water would come from the sea's side.

**170 small waters below the common level stand within 12 km of the sea** *(computed; the consequence is a reading)*. The atlas holds them at the sea's level; they cover 46,400 km², 5,400 km² of it within that distance and 3,200 km² in desert. Fed by rivers they fill and overflow, as the first stage found. In desert a hollow that loses more water than it receives stands below the sea and draws sea water through the ground, laying its salt down as a pan. Which hollows do which is not worked out.

**The sea would send about 2.4 Mt of salt a year into the air, a twenty-fifth of what the same area of Earth's ocean sends** *(computed; the lunar yield is an extrapolation)*. The record's wind at 10 m over the sea averages 2.3 m/s, stays under 3.5 m/s for 90% of the hours and under 4.6 m/s for 99%, and its strongest 6.5-hour mean is 7.2 m/s. The source is the function the aerosol study's marine note assembled (Gong 2003 with Jaeglé et al.'s weight for the water's warmth), carried to lunar gravity as that note proposes:

| Source | Salt into the air | From each m² of sea |
|---|---|---|
| Earth's function at the lunar wind, unchanged | 1.3 Mt a year | 0.6 g a year |
| Lunar, low yield | 0.8 Mt | 0.35 g |
| Lunar, central | 2.4 Mt | 1.0 g |
| Lunar, high yield | 5.8 Mt | 2.5 g |
| Lunar, with the gentler wind law | 7.7 Mt | 3.3 g |
| Earth's ocean, by the function fitted to the most measurements, with the range of published functions (Grythe et al. 2014, in the lookup) | | 25 g (8–190) |

Winds of 3–5 m/s, 22% of the hours, raise 64% of the salt. The water's warmth doubles the source: Jaeglé et al.'s weight averages 2.06 over this sea, held at its value for 30 °C, where their data end. A fresh sea at 0.14 g/kg puts 250 times less salt into the same spray.

**Three fifths of it falls back on the sea, and the land beside the sea receives about a kilogram of chloride per hectare a year** *(computed; three cases of removal on the central source)*:

| | Slow removal | Central | Fast removal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean time in the air | 10 days | 5.8 days | 2.7 days |
| Mean distance from where it rose | 770 km | 630 km | 440 km |
| Falls back on the sea | 57% | 59% | 66% |
| Falls on land and lakes | 43% | 40% | 34% |
| Reaches the five other seas | 0.4% | 0.1% | 0.01% |
| Chloride on the land beside the sea, kg per hectare a year | 0.9 | 1.1 | 1.2 |
| On the most exposed land cell | 6.9 | 8.1 | 6.8 |
| Mean over all the Moon's land | 0.17 | 0.17 | 0.14 |
| Chloride in the rain beside the sea, mg/L | 0.07 | 0.10 | 0.11 |
| Salt in the air over the sea, µg/m³ | 7.4 | 5.0 | 2.9 |
| Salt in the air over the land beside it, µg/m³ | 0.9 | 0.6 | 0.3 |

Every row scales with the source, 0.34 to 3.2 times these values across the lunar cases. The land beside the sea is the 1.7 million km² of the model's land cells that hold part of the sea or touch a cell that does.

Set beside Earth, these are a continent's interior. Australia's measured transect gives 101 kg of chloride per hectare a year at the shore, 13 at 100 km, 2.8 at 500 km and 0.5 at 1,000 km (Wilkins et al. 2022, in the lookup), so the land beside this sea receives what Australia's does about 800 km inland, and its most exposed cell what Australia's does 230 km inland. Rain in the interior of the United States carries 0.1–0.2 mg/L of chloride and several mg/L at the coasts *(recalled)*. A river draining the land beside the sea with half a metre of runoff a year would carry 0.2 mg/L from this source *(computed here)*, against 5.75 mg/L in the world's average natural river *(recalled, in the lookup)*.

**What that salt does** *(reading of the computed values)*:

- **To the ground.** Where rain passes through the soil, the salt goes down the rivers to the sea of its catchment. In desert nothing flushes it: at the rate of the land beside the sea a root zone would reach the coast-water study's saline threshold in about 4,000 years (1,200–11,000 across the source cases), and the desert land as a whole receives a thirteenth of that rate.
- **To the other waters.** The five other seas receive under half a percent of the salt between them. A closed lake gathers what falls on its catchment; at a kilogram per hectare a year over a catchment ten times its area, a lake 50 m deep gains 0.02 mg/L of chloride a year, 200 mg/L in 10,000 years *(computed here)*.
- **To the sea.** The salt that leaves for good is 1.0 Mt a year (0.3–3.1), a thousandth of the sea's salt in 180,000 years (56,000–530,000). The crust takes 3.8 Gt a year.
- **To aerial craft.** The air's lowest 1.7 km over the sea would hold 3–7 µg/m³ of salt (1–24 across the source cases) and the air over the land beside it under 1 µg/m³. Air over Earth's open ocean holds roughly 10–30 µg/m³ near the surface *(recalled; a short lookup on 15 November did not confirm a figure)*. Craft over this sea would fly in salt air at a tenth to a half of an Earth sea's, and over the rest of the Moon in almost none.

**The reading stops short of the shore itself.** The model's cells are 170 km across, the source holds no surf and no drops coarser than 20 µm at 80% humidity, and on Earth those lay most of the shore's 84 kg per hectare a year within 25 km. At a sixth of the gravity such drops settle a sixth as fast, so they would carry several times as far, while the light winds raise fewer of them. The first kilometres behind a salt shore are therefore open, and with them the spray on the green rims of the wet northern shores.

## The circulation run

The third stage, 15 November 2026, computes what the first two reasoned: where the sea's brackish water lies and how fast its deeps are renewed. [circulation.py](circulation.py) sets MITgcm on the sea with temperature and salt through [climate/ocean/stratified.py](../../../climate/ocean/stratified.py), which adds to the tide runs' tooling what a sea's own circulation needs. [results/circulation_trial_1deg.json](results/circulation_trial_1deg.json) (schema `terluna.research.south-sea-circulation-trial/1`) records the set-up and a measured trial. A run of 50 model years started on 15 November at 15:32 EST and ended at 23:26 EST, about 8 hours of machine time with other jobs on the machine; its first model year conserved the sea's salt to eleven digits. `circulation read` gathers it into [results/circulation_1deg.json](results/circulation_1deg.json) (schema `terluna.research.south-sea-circulation/1`): each model year's means at every level, each month's top layer, the deep oxygen estimated from the dye and the deep water's speeds. What follows is the run's reading at 30 km cells. Its limits are listed after the set-up.

|---|---|---|
| Under 5 | 720 km² | 0–780 km² |
| Brackish, 5–18 | 4,460 km² | 3,920–4,710 km² |
| Mixed, 18–30 | 18,800 km² | 15,500–23,700 km² |
| Diluted sea water, 30–34 | 240,000 km² | 206,000–285,000 km² |
| Sea water, 34–36 | 2,076,000 km² | 2,031,000–2,112,000 km² |

The classes have held these sizes since about model year 8, the diluted water growing by about 200 km² a year. The freshest cell, at the largest mouth, holds 2.6 g/kg, and the saltiest surface, in the far south, 35.6 g/kg against the start's 35.2, so no brine end forms. The brackish water fills six of the model's 784 km² cells and the water under 5 g/kg less than one, so the plumes are under-resolved at these cells. The half-degree run that would refine the whole sea is deferred until more computing is available, and the fine patch at the largest mouth, read next, shows how much the 30 km cells understate them.

**At 3 km cells the largest mouth makes a layered estuary 250 km long** *(computed: the fine patch, one model year)*. It took 2.1 hours of machine time on four processes overnight and kept the sea's salt (a mean of 35.0001 throughout). The river, about 24,000 m³/s with the smaller mouths beside it, is shared among the cells within 6 km of each mouth. The surface settles within three to four months; over the last six, within the patch:

|---|---|---|---|
| Fresh, under 0.5 | 280 km² | 220–320 km² | none |
| Nearly fresh, 0.5–5 | 2,190 km² | 1,900–2,690 km² | 720 km² |
| Brackish, 5–18 | 7,500 km² | 7,160–7,980 km² | 3,730 km² |
| Mixed, 18–30 | 4,710 km² | 3,950–5,210 km² | 7,080 km² |

**The set-up** *(a configuration; none of it is a result)*:

- **The grid** is spherical in a frame of its own, centred on the sea at 47.8° S, 176.9° W on the turned Moon, so that its cells stay 27.5–30.3 km across from the sea's north at 14° S to its end at 80° S: 66 by 72 cells of one degree, 2,651 of them water, on 32 levels from 5 m thick at the surface to 500 m at depth. The Coriolis parameter is the true latitude's, read from files. The model's sea covers 2.34 million km² and holds 4.88 million km³, 1.4% and 1.1% above LOLA's pixels; its deepest cell is 5,580 m, where single pixels reach 6,800 m.
- **The water** follows seawater's equation of state (Jackett and McDougall 1995) with its pressure from the Moon's gravity. It starts at rest at seawater's strength and 27 °C throughout, the warmth of the sea's coldest surface.
- **The surface** takes the design run's year 10 as written every 6.5 hours, repeated: its wind stress (0.010 Pa on average), its net heat flux with the sunlight inside it (0.65 W/m² out of the sea over the year), and its evaporation less rain (0.70 m a year, 51,700 m³/s). The top layer is also drawn towards the record's surface temperature, 26.6–32.9 °C, with a 30-day time scale, since the climate model's sea is a slab that carries no heat sideways.
- **The rivers** enter at their mouths: the six-seas routing's 372 mouths, 31,600 m³/s, in 31 cells, and 20,000 m³/s more at the mouth of the Moscoviense route, so that the sea's level holds. The cell at that mouth takes 24,000 m³/s, 2.6 m of water a day. The free surface carries the water itself, so a river dilutes the cell it enters and the sea's salt is conserved.
- **The mixing** is the turbulence closure of Gaspar et al. (1990) (MITgcm's `ggl90`), which also carries convection, over a background of 10⁻⁵ m²/s; the lateral viscosity is Smagorinsky's with a floor of 300 m²/s, and the bed drag is quadratic with the tide runs' 0.0025.
- **The old water.** A dye starts at 1 everywhere and is drawn to 0 in the top layer within a day. What is left of it at a depth is the share of that water that has not been at the surface since the start, so its fall measures the deeps' renewal.

**What was checked before the run:**

- **The Moon in the model.** The pinned source takes the namelist's gravity into the equation of state's pressure and into the closure's buoyancy; the forcing package's bulk formulas, which hold Earth's constants, are compiled out; the model's own printout gives gravity 1.6242 m/s² and a Coriolis parameter of −1.3 to −5.2×10⁻⁶ s⁻¹ over the sea.

- **Stopping and resuming.** Twenty days run in two halves from a pickup match twenty days run straight, bit for bit, in salt, temperature, flow and dye.
- **The cost.** 0.033 s a step on four processes with other jobs on the machine: 10 minutes for a model year of 357.5 days at 1,800 s steps. Fifty model years are about 8.5 hours. Cells of half a degree would cost eight times as much a model year at the same Courant number.

The 30-day time scale on the surface temperature is a numerical choice. None bounds a lunar quantity; each is a place where the Moon's value is unknown and Earth's is carried.

**What the run can and cannot settle.** It gives the top layer's salinity through 50 years, class by class, and so the size of the plumes and of any basin-wide gradient; the mixed layer's depth; the temperature at each depth, with the depth from which the water stands at or under the eels' 28 °C; and the dye's fall at depth. ## The Moon's own mixing

16 November 2026. The 50-year run mixed the sea's interior with a background diffusivity of 10⁻⁵ m²/s, the open ocean's value at thermocline depths on Earth, and the deep-oxygen rerun was first proposed at 10⁻⁴ m²/s, Earth's deep-ocean mean. The register's row of 16 November replaces both with the Moon's own value: the mixing that the power going into the sea's internal waves can pay for, under the Moon's gravity and rotation. [lunar_mixing.py](lunar_mixing.py) computes that power and sets MITgcm's IDEMIX closure on it in place of the fixed background (Olbers and Eden 2013; in the pinned source's `pkg/ggl90`, compiled in on request by [climate/ocean/stratified.py](../../../climate/ocean/stratified.py)). IDEMIX follows the waves' energy as it spreads and dissipates by the model's own buoyancy frequency and Coriolis parameter, and the energy it dissipates feeds the closure's turbulence. The background falls to heat's molecular diffusivity, 1.4×10⁻⁷ m²/s. The oxygen then runs with that mixing.

**The wind puts 0.54 GW into the motions that turn at the inertial period, and the internal waves take a fifth of it** *(computed: one model year with outputs every 0.99 days)*. Continued from its year-50 state for a model year with its own settings, the sea takes 2.34 GW of work from the wind, counted at every step. Only 0.55 GW of it is done on the monthly-mean flow, the part the 50-year reading counted. The Moon turns once a month, so the inertial period over the sea, 2π/|f|, runs from 14 days in the far south to 55 days in the north. Currents the wind sets turning at that period carry energy down as internal waves, a main route on Earth from the wind to the deep. Within a quarter of the local inertial frequency the wind does 0.54 GW of work (0.36 GW within 15%, 0.88 GW within 40%). The northern band, 14–30° S on the turned Moon, takes 0.24 GW of it at 0.52 mW/m², three times the other bands' rate. There the winds' 29.5-day cycle of day and night matches the inertial period, which equals it near 28° S. IDEMIX passes a fifth of the power into the internal waves, its share fitted on Earth, so 0.11 GW. [results/lunar_mixing_inputs_1deg.json](results/lunar_mixing_inputs_1deg.json) (schema `terluna.research.south-sea-lunar-mixing-inputs/1`) holds the figures, and the maps lie beside the runs. The inputs were computed at 06:23 on 16 November. Three edits followed before the commit: the GGL90 namelist's file names and the tracers' size header in `climate/ocean/stratified.py`, both for the one-year trial, and the command line of [lunar_mixing.py](lunar_mixing.py). None of them is in the functions that compute the inputs, but the product's recorded code hash is given by no committed version, and it names its maps by an absolute path into the `domain/hydrosphere` worktree; both are rebound when the inputs are next rebuilt, after the oxygen run, whose setup they feed.

**The tide puts 2 MW into internal waves** *(computed from the tide trial through a parameterisation fitted on Earth)*. Fitted to the month's and the fortnight's lines, the tide trial's currents at the floor have a root mean square of 0.6 mm/s at the median cell. The floor is rough, 280 m about each cell's plane at the median by LOLA's 16 px/deg heights, but the deep water is barely layered: its buoyancy frequency at the median floor is 1.8×10⁻⁵ s⁻¹. The monthly line also lies below the inertial frequency poleward of about 30°, where no internal wave can carry it away, so it radiates over a fifth of the sea. Jayne and St. Laurent's (2001) form for the power a tidal current puts into waves over a rough floor, half ρκh²NU² with κ = 2π/10 km, gives 1.5 MW from the monthly line and 0.4 MW from the fortnightly one.

**The floor adds 11 mW/m² of heat**, 26 GW over the sea, the middle of the 8–14 mW/m² predicted for the Schrödinger basin inside South Pole–Aitken (Santangelo and colleagues 2025, a search summary). The Apollo 15 and 17 sites measured 21 and 14 mW/m². The 50-year run had no heat from the floor.

**Between 400 m and 1 km the Moon's own mixing is ten to twenty times weaker than the 50-year run's** *(computed: a model year from the year-50 state with IDEMIX)*. With the waves' energy starting from nothing, the mixing settles within months. As sea-wide means of the closure's diffusivity it falls from 4×10⁻⁴ m²/s at 145 m and 7×10⁻⁵ at 220 m to between 5×10⁻⁶ and 3×10⁻⁵ at 330 m, as the mixed layer's reach changes with the season, and holds at about 5×10⁻⁷ m²/s from 400 to 850 m, rising to 1×10⁻⁶ m²/s at 1 km. Below about 1.2 km it rises to 10⁻³–10⁻² m²/s, in the water the run began with at a uniform 27 °C: that water holds almost no layering for the waves to work against, so they stir it without joining it to the surface. The waves carry about a third of the power that 10⁻⁵ m²/s took in the 50-year run, about 0.32 GW at IDEMIX's efficiency of a sixth, and they spend it mostly in the upper 400 m, where the wind's energy enters. The eels' layer, 270–600 m, spans the depths where the Moon's own mixing falls from about the run's value to a twentieth of it, so the water below 400 m is renewed even more slowly than the 50-year reading found, wherever the currents do not carry it *(reading)*. The trial's model year, with four tracers, took 0.064 s a step on four processes with the machine shared, 18 minutes in all.

**The oxygen run** carries two tracers from the year-50 state with this mixing and the floor's heat: a dye started at 1, which falls as the water reaches the surface, and the oxygen spent since the water last left the surface. The oxygen spent is held at 0 in the top layer and takes the sea oxygen study's nutrient-poor demand on each level, placed as an ocean's sinking matter places it, as in the 50-year estimate. The demand does not depend on the oxygen left, so the tracer scales with the export and gives every nutrient state at once. The water holds saturation less the oxygen spent, down to none. Water that has stayed below the surface since year 50 starts the run counted as saturated; counting it as holding none instead lowers it by saturation times the dye, so the two bound the oxygen until the dye has fallen. The one-year trial carried two more tracers, the 50-year run's dye continued and the oxygen spent in the lake model's placement; the run leaves them out to save machine time. It began on 16 November at 06:47 EST and ran its first three model years in 17, 17 and 23 minutes with the machine shared. It was then paused at model year 53, at that year's checkpoint, to leave the machine to lighter work. Once it resumes, its remaining 47 model years take about 13–18 hours at those rates. `lunar_mixing read` reduces whatever has run to `results/lunar_mixing_1deg.json` (schema `terluna.research.south-sea-lunar-mixing/1`), written when the run is first read.

**Terms fitted on Earth and taken as they stand:** IDEMIX's constants, among them its dissipation constant of a third, the spectral bandwidth of 5 taken from Earth's internal-wave spectrum, the waves' time scales of 2 and 10 days and a mixing efficiency of a sixth; the fifth of the inertial power it passes into the waves; the 1,024 kg/m³ by which it divides its inputs; and Jayne and St. Laurent's wavenumber. The band of a quarter of the inertial frequency is a choice. The closure's constants and the bed's drag remain the 50-year run's.

## The rivers' temperature and the storms' winds

22 November 2026. Two things the circulation run leaves out were sized from records the project already holds, with no new run: the rivers' own temperature, and the winds that storms raise inside one cell of the climate model. The scripts and their outputs lie on the research drive under `south-sea-ocean/wind_peaks_2026-11-22/` and `south-sea-ocean/storm_winds_2026-11-22/`; they are readings of existing runs, not products, and carry no tests.

**The rivers enter at the sea surface's temperature, and their own would not sink them** *(the set-up as read; arithmetic)*. The set-up gives MITgcm the rivers' volume alone, so the model adds river water at the temperature of the cell it enters. At the sea's mean surface, 30.1 °C and 35 g/kg, sea water weighs 1,021.7 kg/m³ and fresh water 995.6 kg/m³ (UNESCO's one-atmosphere equation of state). Rivers 1–4 K cooler than the surface, the range the [river deltas study](../river_deltas/README.md) takes for the fresh seas, weigh 0.3–1.1 kg/m³ more than that and stay 25–26 kg/m³ lighter than the sea, so they float whatever their temperature. Their heat is small over the sea: 24,000 m³/s entering 2.5 K cooler takes up 250 GW, 0.11 W/m² of the sea's surface. It is spent first in the estuary's top layer, where neither the sea-wide run nor the patch carries it.

In a fresh sea a river no longer floats by its freshness, and its temperature decides. The deltas study, giving its rivers the land air's temperature, finds them 0.4–2.1 kg/m³ denser than the water they meet and plunging as underflows, and the [sea oxygen study](../sea_oxygen/README.md)'s columns carry no inflows. The [rivers' oxygen study](../river_oxygen/README.md) of the same day counts what the rivers take down in the six fresh seas of the design surface: their water comes out of lakes as warm as the seas' surface or warmer, 4% of it goes below the nights' mixing, and the oxygen it carries is about half a percent of the deep water's demand, 6% if every river sank. For this sea the inflow that holds its level, 1,640 km³ a year, is 0.034% of its 4.84 million km³ a year, the order of the run's renewal of the water below each depth (0.02–0.18% a year). As a salt sea it keeps that water at its top, in a layer 18 m deep off the largest mouth *(reading)*.

**The record that drives the run holds no storm** *(computed from the 6.5-hour record)*. Over this sea's 160 model cells, each about 170 km across, the 10 m wind averages 2.26 m/s, stays under 4.6 m/s in 99% of the cell-records and reaches 7.2 m/s at most. The power the wind gives the waves and the water rises with the cube of its speed, and the record's mean cube is 1.56 times the cube of its mean: the strongest tenth of the cell-records holds 37% of it and the strongest hundredth 7%. So little power arrives in all that a wind of 15 m/s over the whole sea for 46 hours a year would equal the record's whole year, and 20 m/s for 19 hours.

**Storms over the sea are real in the cloud model, and their gusts are weak** *(read from CM1's sea box; one site)*. The box over the Near-side Sea at 5° S, 192 km square on 6 km cells, ran 18 lunar days: four under the design years' mean forcing by local time and fourteen following the design run's own weather at the site. Its hourly maxima were read over all of them and its surface fields every three hours from the third lunar day on (472 days). It makes storms: updraughts to 45 m/s, rain to 63 mm/h, downdraughts to 24 m/s and pools of air up to 3.8 K colder than their surroundings at the surface. Its 10 m wind averages 2.09 m/s and stays under 4.9 m/s in 99% of the cell-snapshots. The strongest 10 m wind anywhere in the box is 2.9 m/s in the median hour, passes 7.7 m/s in 1% of the hours and 10 m/s in 0.12%, and reaches 13.0 m/s at most in 12,744 hours. A cold pool spreads at about the square root of gravity times its relative coldness times its depth, which at a sixth of Earth's gravity is 0.41 of Earth's speed for the same pool: 4 m/s for a pool 3 K colder and 1 km deep, against 10 m/s on Earth.

**The gusts inside a cell add 7–27% to the wind's power** *(computed from the same fields)*. The cells' mean cubed wind is 1.26 times the cube of the box's mean wind at the same moments, and 1.27 times that of its 6-hour means. The ocean model takes its power from the cell's mean stress, as 3.75 times the friction velocity cubed, and in that measure the cells' mean is 1.07 times what the box's mean stress gives. Cells raining at 1 mm/h or more are 0.9% of the cell-snapshots and hold 3.2% of the cubed wind. If the interior's mixing follows the power, the 5×10⁻⁷ m²/s between 400 and 850 m becomes 5–6×10⁻⁷ m²/s, and a tenfold rise would be needed to reach even the 50-year run's 10⁻⁵ m²/s, under which the estimate already left the water below about 850 m without oxygen. The gusts spend their power in the surface mixed layer, at and above the top of the eels' layer, and the water below gains only through the internal waves. The winds' peaks therefore leave the deeps' reading as it stands *(reading)*.

**What these readings rest on.** The box stands near the equator of another sea, and its mean wind is drawn toward the climate model's on a two-hour time scale, so it cannot grow weather wider than itself; a storm system wider than the box and narrower than a few of the climate model's cells is in neither model, and whether the slowly turning Moon makes one is untested. Its cells are 6 km wide, wider than a gust front. The one storm run at 2 km cells, two days over land, reached 6.3 m/s at 10 m under a pool 4.4 K colder, no more than the 6 km boxes' storms, but it is not the same storm at two spacings. At this sea's latitudes only the two-dimensional rings of the earlier, cooler climate exist: over water their 10 m wind averages 1.8 m/s and reaches 9.2 m/s at 45° S, and averages 0.6 m/s and reaches 1.6 m/s at 80° S, where no storm formed. The fields are instantaneous, every three hours.

**The estimate placed the demand as Earth's oceans do, and on the Moon the matter decays higher** *(a correction to the estimate's form: Stokes's law with recalled values)*. The ocean form lets the sinking matter decay along Martin's curve, the flux falling as depth to the power −0.86 from 100 m, as measured in Earth's Pacific. That curve holds a sinking speed and a decay rate, and the estimate carried both to the Moon unchanged. A particle of one size and excess density sinks at a speed proportional to gravity over the water's viscosity. The Moon's gravity is 6.04 times weaker, and this sea's water at 28 °C is 0.60 times as viscous as the 5–10 °C water under Earth's curve, so the same particle sinks 3.6 times slower and, decaying at Earth's rate, spends itself over a fall 3.6 times shorter. If the decay also runs faster in water 20 K warmer, by a factor of 2 for each 10 K, the fall is 15 times shorter. Under the new forms the estimate's upper 250 m are not to be read, and its "under 2 mg/L from 104 m" does not stand; the tracer run decides there.

**What the two readings change** *(reading)*. The sea drawn by the 50-year estimate lost its oxygen from 104 m down. Under the Moon's own mixing and sinking the upper 250 m keep their oxygen, and the question narrows to the still layer from about 400 m to 1 km, the lower half of the eels' layer included, and the pool below it: a stock of oxygen that 5–12% of the export draws on and that mixing restores ten to twenty times more slowly than the 50-year run assumed. A fall that short also means little reaches the floor, 2–5% of the demand, so recovery at the floor reaches little of it. How that layer fares over centuries, and a living system that takes up the demand where the matter decays and carries oxygen below it, are worked out in the canopy colony study, `research/studies/canopy_colony` on `domain/ecology` (not on this branch until the branches next join).

**What these readings rest on.** Stokes's law holds for small particles that sink slowly; larger aggregates meet a drag that grows with their speed, and their speed then rises less than in proportion to gravity, so 3.6 is the upper end of gravity's effect on them. Whether particles on the Moon grow to Earth's sizes and densities, with weaker turbulence to break them and a sixth of the weight to compact them, is untested. The decay's factor of 2 for each 10 K is a usual value for microbes' respiration and is not measured for sinking matter at 28 °C; Earth's own warm waters are reported to spend their sinking matter at shallower depths than its cold ones *(recalled, not checked)*. The reference depth of 100 m and the export are the estimate's and unchanged. The paused run's three years carry the demand in Earth's form, and no tracer run carries the new forms.

## What is open

- **The sea is salt.** On 16 November the author decided that the South Pole–Aitken Sea is made a salt sea at seawater's strength, with satoumi, managed coastal and marine ecosystems, on its shores; how its waters are kept rich enough is the author's to work out (register on main, "The atlas round's frame"). Its level is held by a governed share of Moscoviense's water ([drainage network](../drainage_network/README.md#what-the-atlas-round-receives)).
- **The deeps' oxygen under the Moon's own mixing.** The oxygen run [above](#the-moons-own-mixing) is paused at model year 53, three of its 50 model years run. It waits for the basin the atlas round redraws with the water's own gravity and the crust's sag, which alone deepens the sea by 384 m over its deepest floor and its sill into the Tenebrarum sub-basin from 75 m to 314 m, and starts again on it ([register](../../decisions.md#the-atlas-rounds-frame-16-november-2026), 16 November); its year-53 checkpoint and set-up are kept meanwhile. Its three finished years are read [above](#the-paused-runs-three-years-and-how-fast-the-matter-sinks): they give the sea's structure under this mixing and leave the oxygen between wide bounds. The whole run's reading gives the oxygen by depth and export, with its bounds, and the renewal and temperature of the eels' layer. Engineered stirring, which a run with the background raised to 3×10⁻⁵ or 10⁻⁴ m²/s would size at about 0.8 or 2.7 GW, is a design question taken up only if that layer comes out short of oxygen. In the long run a sea without an outlet fills its deeps with the coldest water its surface makes, which this run's surface makes at about 28 °C, the eels' limit, and stirring that brings oxygen down brings heat with it *(reading)*. The half-degree run stays deferred; the plumes it would refine are under-resolved at 30 km, and the fine patch takes the largest mouth.
- **The deeps' oxygen demand at its source.** The deep water's oxygen is used by microbes that decompose the dead matter sinking from the sunlit layer, far more than by the animals that live at depth in most of Earth's oceans: the water between 150 and 1,000 m holds about 70% of the dark ocean's respiration, mostly microbial, while the animals' share runs from under 1% to over half by region (Arístegui and colleagues' review, a search summary). The demand is therefore set by what the surface grows and by what becomes of its debris, and reducing that supply is noted as a key method of limiting the sea's oxygen depletion. Three ways were named, none sized or designed here: a nutrient-poor surface; recovering the settled material before it decays, which the nutrient return cycle needs in any case and which spares the oxygen its decay would take, 2.7 t for each tonne of organic carbon as carbohydrate and about 3.5 t with the matter's nitrogen oxidised too *(stoichiometry; the second by Redfield's 138 O₂ to 106 C, recalled)*; and oxidising it on electrodes in place of dissolved oxygen. The 50-year reading sizes the first: with the demand placed as an ocean's sinking matter, the layer at 270–500 m keeps about 2 mg/L when 25 g of carbon per m² a year sink below 100 m and 4.3 mg/L at 12 g, near what Earth's poorest gyres send down. Placed as the lake model places it, 78% of the demand falls on the floor; placed as an ocean's, 56% falls in the water between 100 and 500 m, so recovery at the floor reaches the bottom water and the river arms where material collects, and the layer the eels would need is reached through the surface's production. How the method is implemented is the author's to work out. On 22 November the estimate's ocean form was corrected for the Moon's slower sinking ([above](#the-paused-runs-three-years-and-how-fast-the-matter-sinks)): 77–90% of the demand below 100 m then falls between 100 and 250 m and 2–5% on the floor, and the author's design for a living system that takes up the demand, a floating colony with feeders, a trunk and oxygen capsules, is refined in the canopy colony study on `domain/ecology`.
- **More of the shore at fine cells.** The patch at the largest mouth ran its model year (the reading [above](#the-circulation-run)). The other mouths, the 16,000 m³/s at 15° S and the 6,300 m³/s at 41° S on the turned Moon, are under-resolved at 30 km in the same way and are not run. A patch costs about 2–4 hours of machine time a model year, as the machine is loaded. A flood season in the rivers, and the shallow parts where the tide could run faster than in the deep arm, are outside the run. The air's side of the shore, the sea breeze and the spray it carries inland, waits for the coast-water study's cloud-model slice, which has not run.
- **The drainage network as it fills.** The routing here tests three fixed rules, so it gives candidate connections: craters that fill in two to six years and pass their rivers on, three mouths on the south sea, and a path from the sea up the Moscoviense route. The [drainage network study](../drainage_network/README.md) follows each large sea to its own level, with its rim, the basins it joins on the way up, the water and years it takes to fill, the chain of overflows and the rivers' long profiles. It finds the Moscoviense route open only after about 800–900 years, once the lowland to its south has filled with Moscoviense. Two parts of it bear on this study. The full Moscoviense overflow exceeds the south sea's loss, so an ungoverned network raises the sea towards the level at which it joins the near-side ocean, and a sea that joins another shares its salt with it; the salt sea needs a governed share of that flow. And the routing knows no falls, so whether fish or boats pass is read from each river's long profile.
- **The shore's first kilometres.** The spray of the surf and of coarse drops behind a salt shore; the belts along the 10,700 km of shore whose rain falls short of the plants' demand; which hollows beside the sea become salt pans. What would grow on a salt shore is the biosphere lane's question.
- **The spray's yield at lunar gravity.** No study of whitecaps or bursting bubbles at reduced gravity was found by the aerosol study's lookup; the low and high cases here come from two opposite arguments, neither tested. The winds inside 6.5 hours are also outside the record, and the source rises with the 3.4th power of the wind.
- **The living value.** What an ocean of this size adds in marine life, food and culture beside the seawater lakes and aquariums, and what the conservation programmes would do with it, is not assessed here.
- **A weaker sea.** Half strength halves the salt and loses the eels' spawning; the reefs' and the open sea's limits (corals grow at 25–42 g/kg, summaries in the lookup) set how far a cheaper sea can go.
- **The rain.** Every balance here rests on the model's rain at T21, which the climate lane carries as a range; the rivers were not rescaled with it.

## How it is set up

- **The shores** are the coast-water study's: LOLA at 16 pixels per degree above the geoid against the atlas's sea level, each land pixel beside the sea counted with the length of its edges facing it (times π/4 for the staircase), its depth and its height. The belts follow that study's steady flow from the water's edge under the plants' draw, with its desert draw at 301 K and its six cases of ground and reach; the high water is half of geography's typical monthly range at the nearest tide node, spread over the pixel's slope. The rain behind a stretch of shore is the design years' at the nearest land cell of the model, since 82% of the shore lies under the model's water cells.
- **The spray's source** is Gong's (2003) function as Jaeglé et al. (2011) print it, over drops of 0.035–20 µm radius at 80% humidity, with their weight for the water's warmth. The lunar wind is replaced by the Earth wind that puts the same energy into the waves, air density times friction velocity cubed, with a Charnock roughness at each gravity (the aerosol study's marine note, on the research drive under `atmospheric-electricity/aerosol_marine.md`, sections 1.5 and 7.1); 5 m/s over the lunar sea counts as 6.1 m/s. The note's low and high yields multiply the central case by 0.34 and 2.46, and its gentler wind law replaces the 3.41 power by 2.07. The wind is the 10 m wind of the design run's year 10 written again every 6.5 hours (`A28R_301k_design_l15_snow_s938_turned_refit_precession_eddies`, the record the littoral study measured its wind raise on), read at the model's water cells under the sea.
- **The spray's fate** follows each 6.5 hours' salt from each of the sea's 160 model cells as a parcel on the mean wind of the record's lowest two levels, the lowest 1.9 km, along great circles. A parcel loses salt to the ground at a deposition speed plus each size's settling at lunar gravity, over a mixed layer of 1.7 km above water and 5 km above land, and to the rain at a scavenging ratio times the record's rain in the cell under it. The three cases take ratios of 100, 300 and 1,000 and speeds of 0.01, 0.05 and 0.3 cm/s, the aerosol study's three. The year's winds repeat until every parcel is spent, and every gram is accounted for.
- **The sea** is the atlas's body 1417 at the 28% water share, on the 4 pixel/degree grid; its areas by latitude band and its place on the turned Moon follow the frame record's rotation.
- **Rain, evaporation and surface temperature** are the design years, 10–19 of the corrected run on the turned Moon (`A28R_301k_design_l15_snow_s938_turned_refit_precession`), read at the model's water cells under the sea, each atlas cell given to the nearest model cell. The model's sea is a 50 m slab of fresh water.
- **The rivers** are geography's routing of the same years' runoff over LOLA's 16 pixel/degree heights (`geography.drainage.route`), run three times with different outlet waters. The first run reproduces geography's own product, 199,973 m³/s to the waters below the common level. A basin's spill level is the level of the depressions under its cells, which is one value across each basin.
- **The salt** uses the sea chemistry's seawater (Millero et al. 2008) and the seawater lakes study's density (EOS-80).
- **The floor** multiplies the sea's area in each latitude band by stage 1's water held under a sea floor in that band.

## Assumptions and limits

- The climate, the lakes' open-water balance and the sea level stay the design years' in all three routings. A hollow that fills or a sea that spills would change the climate that made its rivers, so the rules bracket where the rivers end and predict no level.
- Salt lowers water's vapour pressure by about 2%, which the evaporation here leaves out; so are the sea's own gravity and the crust's sag, which move its shore by tens of metres.
- The times to fill leave out the growing lakes' evaporation, which is a few km³ a year for the craters and tens for the smaller seas.
- Earth's seas named for comparison are recalled values and serve as reference points.
- The shores' belts carry the coast-water study's limits: 2 km pixels, a draw computed on the earlier design case and the unturned Moon, and a tide on the dry Moon's geoid. The desert here is the design years' on the turned Moon, so the comparison with that study's Moon-wide belts crosses two desert masks.
- The spray is a diagnostic on one model year at T21. Its source was fitted on Earth and its lunar yield is untested; its winds are 6.5-hour means; its parcels stay in the lowest levels and do not spread; its removal rates are choices that bracket Earth's models, in which rain takes 30–40% of sea salt and the ground the rest (a search summary of Gong's global budget) where these cases give 43–52% to the rain. The warmth weight is the printed polynomial; the marine note's own list of its values at 24–28 °C matches it only at 26 °C.
- Nothing here is validated against a lunar observation.

## The checks

[test_run.py](test_run.py), [test_inflows.py](test_inflows.py), [test_shores.py](test_shores.py) and [test_spray.py](test_spray.py): seawater's ions sum to its salinity and its charges balance; the salt agrees with the sea chemistry's 174,000 Gt and the seawater lakes' 35.94 Mt/km³; the sea keeps its area on the model's grid and turns with the frame; the balance reads only the model's water cells; the floor's take grows with time and carries its salt; waters join across the grid's seam; the six seas are named by the atlas's centroids; the routing reproduces geography's discharge; each rule passes more water to the two great seas than the one before; and both products regenerate (the routing's only with `TERLUNA_SOUTH_SEA_REGENERATE=1`). For the shores: a pixel faces the sea beside it and across the seam; the rain is read over a place and at the nearest land; the high water stops at the pixel; a belt ends where a steep shore outruns the plants' reach; the shore's parts add up, and the belts' salt is their draw at seawater's strength. For the spray: the source reproduces the marine note's number fluxes, friction velocities, equal-energy winds and settling speeds; a parcel moves with the wind and crosses the pole; in still air every gram comes down where it rose, each size at its own rate; and the product's shares sum to one. The shores and the spray regenerate with the same switch, the spray in the ocean environment. For the circulation ([test_circulation.py](test_circulation.py), with the set-up's own tests in `climate/tests/test_ocean.py`): a cell is water where the sea covers half of it; the record is carried between its cells and round the seam, and land cells take their nearest water; the Coriolis parameter follows the true latitude; the fluxes keep their size and point upward; the sea's own frame keeps places and directions; the dye and the temperature are weighed on the model's own open cells, which round thin bottom cells to rock; and the recorded trial closes its water budget and holds its water within sane bounds. For the Moon's sinking: Earth's water at Earth's gravity gives the curve as measured; the Moon's factor is gravity's ratio times the viscosities'; the demand in the new forms keeps its whole and its export, sends 12% of it past 400 m where Earth's form sends 30%, and leaves Earth's form unchanged to the last digit, so the paused run's set-up holds; the stored estimate carries the four forms with one total; and ([test_lunar_mixing.py](test_lunar_mixing.py)) the paused run's reading holds its three years, with the dye falling year by year in the upper zone and by under 0.02% from 585 m down, and the diffusivity dropping more than tenfold between 328 and 399 m.
