# The atlas round

The atlas round builds the Open Moon's atlas down to its districts. It began on 16 November 2026 on its own branch, `domain/geography`, and the register's section of that day sets its frame ([the atlas round's frame](../../decisions.md#the-atlas-rounds-frame-16-november-2026)). Each step stands on the one before it, so the round works from the ground up:

1. **The surface redrawn under its water** (below): the floor and the seas' levels once the water's own gravity and the crust's sag and creep have settled, barring erosion.
2. **The drainage, the river network and the globe** on that floor, with the three upper seas filled and spilling and Moscoviense's water divided for the most diversity: the salt sea's share over its natural pass, and the rest through a cut in its northern ridge to Humboldtianum and on to Smythii–Marginis.
3. **The design climate's run on the final surface** at the Moon's settled turn under the final water, 6.97°, about 3–7 hours of machine time once the turned frame and surface are built. Whether the near-side sea may part at its pass is decided on it, and the globe is rebuilt on it.
4. **The south sea's ocean runs** on the new basin and climate, the paused oxygen run among them.
5. **The places**: districts defined by significance and wholeness, after a quick review of the heritage register's written evaluations; the young-Moon landforms to keep, chosen as a set; satoyama-like landscapes, and satoumi on the salt sea's shores; the riparian share of the land against Earth's, with the falls counted on finer heights; the lakes among fresh and soda; and the three-dimensional atlas on the present design climate.

## The surface redrawn under its water

The atlas fills LOLA's floor to one level of the dry Moon's geoid, so that the water below that level covers 28% of the surface. The water changes the floor and the level both: its weight bends the crust down, the mantle creeps away beneath the load, and the water's own gravity lifts the sea surface over deep water. Geography's crustal-loading study measured these for one common level on 4 November ([geography](../../../geography/README.md#the-crusts-response-to-the-water)). The round's frame adds what that study did not have: the three upper seas, Moscoviense, Orientale and Smythii–Marginis, filled to their rims above the common level, and the crust once its response has settled.

[redrawn_surface.py](redrawn_surface.py) solves that surface. Every cell below a common level is water, the level set so that those cells cover 28% of the surface as the atlas's do. Each upper sea stands 1 cm under its rim, the lowest pass from its water to another sea's, over all the land its water joins below the rim, as the [drainage network study](../drainage_network/README.md) defines both. The lakes keep the depths that the turned Moon's corrected drainage gives them and weigh on the crust as a fixed load wherever no sea covers them. The Moon answers through load Love numbers: the crustal-loading study's elastic response at every degree to 359, and the creep that the geology lane computes for the present Moon at degrees 2–50 ([geology](../../../geology/README.md)), its change from the water's arrival to the case's time. The sea-level equation is iterated as geography's study iterates it, and after each solution the catchments and passes are found again on the deformed heights, until no rim moves by more than 5 cm. The results go to [results/redrawn_surface.json](results/redrawn_surface.json) (schema `terluna.research.atlas-round-redrawn-surface/1`) and the grids to the research drive.

The round's surface stands at the crust's settled state, barring erosion (register, 16 November): LOLA's landforms as they are, under the sag and creep the water has finished causing. Under the present Moon's central temperatures the creep never quite stops. It grows by about the same amount every tenfold of time: the degree-2 load Love number h′ gains −0.09 by 1,000 years, −0.32 by 10 million and −0.53 by a billion. The geology lane holds today's temperatures, which it takes to describe the Moon for about 100 million years; after that the cooling interior stiffens the mantle and keeps the sag already made. The design case is therefore the central profile 100 million years after the water arrives, and the other cases bound it or trace the way there:

The water's own gravity does most of the redrawing, within years of the water's arrival. Already in the first years the common level stands 36.8 m above the atlas's, the South Pole–Aitken Sea is 422 m deeper at its deepest and its sill to the Tenebrarum sub-basin lies 313 m down instead of 75. The creep to the settled state then adds less: 2.4 m to the common level, 118 m to the south sea's deepest water, 55 m to the Tenebrarum sill and 17 m to the Crisium passage, as the floor under the south sea's deepest water falls 189 m in all (24 m within years). The first millennium differs from the first years by under a metre in the level and by 21 thousand km³ of water, so the creep that matters runs over the following hundred thousand to hundred million years.

The water moves towards the south. Holding 28% at the common level keeps the area the same, so the shore moves both ways: at the settled state the common level floods 179,000 km² of land and gives up 178,000 km² (a read-only look, `atlas-round/redrawn_surface/look/shore_moves.py` on the research drive). The South Pole–Aitken Sea gains 76,000 km² of new sea along its shores and gives up none, as its own gravity draws water to it and its floor sinks most, while the Near-side Sea gives up 107,000 km² of its shallow margins and floods 24,000; the hollows held at the common level lose 21,000 km² net. Along today's shore the sea stands a median 2.8 m higher against the land, from 56 m lower (5th percentile) to 255 m higher (95th percentile). Nearly all of this comes within years: 69,000 km² gained by the south sea and 106,000 given up by the near side in the first-years case.

Measured from the redrawn common level, Moscoviense's rim stands 96 m lower than on the dry Moon and Smythii–Marginis's 47 m lower, while Orientale's holds within a metre. Part of each fall is the common level's own rise of 39 m; the rest is the ground's sag and the water's gravity about each rim. The three seas' deepest water is 180, 140 and 36 m deeper than on the dry Moon. Filled to their rims at the settled state they cover 844,000, 298,000 and 599,000 km² and hold 1.32, 0.38 and 0.36 million km³ beyond what the atlas holds in the same places at the common level, 2.06 million km³ together. Each joins several seas at its rim, so which way its overflow runs is the drainage's to find on the redrawn floor.

The settled surface takes 16.45 million km³ of water, 3.08 million km³ beyond the atlas's 13.37 million (10.72 million in the seas, 2.65 million in the lakes), a global layer of 81 m. The register's estimate of about 3.0 million km³ for the seas' 28% with the upper seas on top holds. The near side stays one sea: its narrowest join, now the neck across western Procellarum to Cognitum, Humorum and Nubium, lies 69 m under the settled sea, against the atlas's 109 m at Fecunditatis's western channels; whether the near-side sea's own balance holds it there is the design climate's run to show.

The bounds stay close to the settled state except the warm mantle. A billion years under the present temperatures would add 0.17 million km³ and 2 m to the level, and the stiff profile stays within 0.04 million km³ of the first millennium. The warm mantle, the least likely of the three profiles, takes 4.51 million km³ beyond the atlas with the common level 61 m up and the south sea 7.76 km deep, and there Orientale's rim does not settle: filled to one of two passes, its weight sinks the ground until the other is the lower, so its rim cycles between 2,283 and 2,337 m and the case is reported with that range.

What the round's next steps take from here: the deformed heights (the settled case's U and N on the research drive, with its water depths and the upper seas' extents), the common level and the three rims for the drainage on the redrawn floor; the water to deliver, 3.08 million km³ beyond the atlas; and the gaps between the first years and the settled state for holding the surface near its settled shape sooner (below).

## The falls

[falls.py](falls.py) finds where the design surface's large rivers drop steeply, for the globe's marks and white water. Each step of the design routing drops from its cell's water surface, the ground on dry cells and the level on a lake or sea, to its receiver's, on the surface the globe draws, cuts included. Steps on rivers of 100 m³/s or more that fall 30 m a km or more join along the flow into reaches, each ranked by the power of its falling water at lunar gravity. The record, [results/falls.json](results/falls.json), lists the 40 largest with their drop, length, flow, steepest step, the water they end in and the named feature beside them (the IAU gazetteer), and the product on the research drive holds every steep step for the white water *(computed between 1.9 km cells)*.

On the design climate's estimate the design surface holds 836 steep reaches, 145 carrying more than 1 GW and 129 dropping more than 500 m; the largest stay on Moscoviense's overflow river, which carries 26,600 m³/s on it, and the neck's river runs 300 km. The figures that follow are the run at 0.930's (17 November), when the surface held 858 steep reaches: 146 carry more than 1 GW of falling water and 118 drop more than 500 m. The largest lie on the river that carries Moscoviense's overflow over its natural pass to the salt sea, 28,324 m³/s for 542 km, falling 3,449 m in all, much of it as a staircase down the northern rim of the Aitken crater: 901 m in 4.5 km beside Aitken Z (42 GW) and 846 m in 10.2 km into Aitken itself (40 GW). A river of 22,785 m³/s falls 482 m in 7.3 km into the Moscoviense sea near Stein M; others of 5,000–14,000 m³/s fall 700–1,470 m in 5–10 km over crater rims into lakes, near Abul Wafa, Amici Q, Lodygin R, Stetson P and Morse N. The near-side neck is no fall: its 11,032 m³/s falls 95 m in 49 km. A reach's steepest step is the steepest 1.9 km.

**At 59 m the largest drops are cascades** *(computed, on the research drive in `atlas-round/waterfalls/profiles/`)*. The six largest were traced on LOLA's and SELENE's merged 59 m heights (SLDEM2015, 138 MB fetched by range for their windows), with the design surface's own correction added as the 64 px/deg heights take it: steepest descent within 2 km of the routing's path, and a check that fills each pit to its outlet first. None has a 59 m step steeper than 45°; their steepest 60 m run 16–28° and their steepest 500 m 12–26°. The most fall-like is the drop beside Aitken Z, where 802 m of its 912 m comes down one unbroken run of steps steeper than 20°, 1.76 km long and averaging 24.5°: a chute down a crater wall at about the angle a loose slope rests at. The 59 m heights cannot rule out sheer faces of a few tens of metres: on a 25° wall a face must exceed about 32 m to read steeper than 45° and about 75 m to read steeper than 60°, and SLDEM2015's own resolution is coarser than its spacing. As the water first arrives, then, the overflows run as long white-water cascades; falls in the free-falling sense would come with erosion through layered rock, or from the works.

## The waterways

On this Moon a large river is mostly lakes: the design routing fills every hollow on a river's way until it spills, so a watercourse is a chain of long lakes joined by short channels. The sustained-rivers study classes the channels reach by reach. [waterways.py](waterways.py) follows each watercourse whole, so that it can be named, drawn and described as one thing (18 November, after the author asked that the major rivers be named and read clearly). From each mouth of 1,000 m³/s or more it goes upstream along the donor carrying the most water, through lakes and channels alike, until the flow falls under 100 m³/s or it reaches a place the design puts water in: the rim a full sea overflows, a branch of the works, or the near-side sea's neck. A lake is crossed straight from where the water enters to where it leaves, since the routing wanders over a lake's level surface. The record is [results/waterways.json](results/waterways.json) *(a reading of products)*.

There are 21 such waterways. The largest is Hanaqucha's overflow to the salt sea, 30,657 m³/s over 442 km and 7 lakes with six steep reaches. The longest runs 4,157 km to Uamoana through 28 lakes. The two branches the works cut from Hanakosa carry 9,991 m³/s to Dagat na Kaseh and 5,237 m³/s to Yuldustengis, 1,282 and 1,101 km. The natural river that reaches Yuldustengis from the far side's northern highlands is 3,091 km long through 42 lakes and carries 1,275 m³/s at its mouth, less than upstream, since its lakes lose water to the dry air. On the map a waterway's breadth is its lakes', so that river looks broader than the works' branch beside it, which carries four times the water.

## Lakes the ground does not hold

A lake's surface is level, so ground beside a lake that lies below its surface is under the lake too, or the lake stands lower than drawn. [lake_support.py](lake_support.py) checks the design surface's water against its own heights on that rule (18 November, after the 474 m views showed lake shores running in straight steps). Of the lakes' 519,081 shore cells, 72,438 (14.0%) have dry ground beside them more than a metre below the lake's surface. They lie on 2,466 of the 29,740 bodies of touching lake cells, bodies that hold 40.5% of the lakes' area. The dry ground that lies below an adjoining lake's surface and is joined to it across such ground comes to 3.50 million km², 9.2% of the Moon and 69% of the lakes' own area, at a median 760 m below the surface it adjoins and more than 2,366 m below it over a tenth; the largest part, 1.43 million km², is between 30° and 60° S. The seas show three such cells, at the neck, where the design holds the near-side sea's main part over the crest ([results/lake_support.json](results/lake_support.json); a calculation on the design surface and the design routing).

The cause is the routing's lake rule, geography/drainage.py's route, which the redrawn and design routings use unchanged. The rule takes each lowest point's catchment by itself and fills it to the lowest pass on its way to a sea, the level at which the whole hollow round it would run over. A hollow whose floor holds several lowest points is filled piece by piece. A piece with water to spare, most often one a river runs through, stands full to the outer pass; the piece beside it, whose evaporation outruns its inflow, keeps a small lake or none; and nothing but the hollow's floor lies between them. Of the 166,060 pairs of a lake cell and a lower dry neighbour, 165,864 lie in two catchments, and in 141,140 the lake is one that overflows. In a real hollow the full piece would pour into the dry one. Either the whole hollow fills, a larger lake with more evaporation and less water passed downstream, or the water settles on the floor as a lake without an outlet and the river ends there. The design's lakes, their evaporation and the rivers below such hollows therefore differ from what the rule's own physics gives, by an amount this check does not compute: it measures the defect and proposes no remedy.

On the page the defect shows close up. Where the ground beside a lake is lower than the lake, the finer heights cannot place the shore, the water runs to the limit of one cell beyond the design's cells, and the shore is drawn along the cells' edges in steps.

## A corrected lake rule, measured

[lake_rule.py](lake_rule.py) routes the design climate once with a lake rule that fills each hollow as one water, on the design routing's own floor, seas and works, and sets the result beside the design routing (18 November; the measure was asked for before anything in the design changes). **The rule.** The catchments are joined into the tree of the hollows they make. Taking the passes between catchments from the lowest up, a pass between two hollows makes a larger hollow of them, and a pass onto ground that already drains to a sea is where a hollow overflows for good. The design surface's 208,136 catchments make 144,145 larger hollows. A hollow needs a supply to stand full to its pass: its open water's loss beyond the rain on it, and the runoff its drowned ground no longer yields. A hollow that gets less holds the lake its supply keeps, lowest ground first, and passes nothing on. One that gets more stands full and sends the rest over its pass, into the hollow beside it while that one is not full and into the larger hollow the two make once it is, so that two hollows full to the sill between them rise together as one lake. Flows put in at cells (the full seas' overflows, the works' branches, the spill over the near-side sea's neck) join the supply of the hollow they land in.

## Wind-blown sand, a first study

[sand.md](sand.md) asks where on the design surface loose sand gathers into sand seas, dune fields or sheets, how much of it moves and what forms it takes (19 November; [sand.py](sand.py), [results/sand.json](results/sand.json), about six minutes on two processors after a one-minute pass over the design run's years with the climate environment). Its grid product, `atlas-round/sand/sand_16ppd.npz` on the research drive, holds on the design surface's grid the share of each cell's ground under loose sand, the share of that sand that moves, its form, the crests' direction and spacing and the drift's direction, for a central case and five variants. It is a first reckoning with nothing in it observed: the thresholds and the flux law are Earth's relations carried to lunar gravity, the winds are the design run's 3-day means with the part the run cannot hold as a stated range, and the supply and the holding are brackets.

**The air moves sand easily, and the land offers it little sand to move.** At 1.62 m/s² in the run's air over land (1.35 kg/m³) a wind of 3.3 m/s at 10 m over a sand surface starts feldspar grains a quarter of a millimetre across, at 0.61 of Earth's friction velocity, and a wind above the threshold carries 6.65 times the sand it would carry on Earth at the same friction velocity. Bare loose sand in the dry land would drift at about 36 m³ per metre of width a year in the central case, 5 on the run's means alone and about 150 at the gusts' high end *(calculations on Earth's relations)*. Supply and cover then draw the map: most rivers end in lakes, the cratered ground catches loose sand in its small bowls, and plants and the kept biological crust hold the arid and semi-arid land. Ten thousand years after the land dried, 168,749 km² lie under sand in the central case, 0.76% of the land: 155,407 km² as thin sheets, streaks and sand on the floors of small bowls, and 13,342 km² as dunes a few metres high at river ends and on downwind shores, with 26,390 km² bare and moving. Across the cases the area runs from 56,020 km², a thousand years after drying, to 276,383 km² with every supply bracket at its high end. No sand sea forms in any of them: one of 10,000 km² twenty metres deep would take 4 to 7 million years from a single shore or river end. Three regions of the redrawn atlas hold 64% of the central sand: the northern far side's polar margin, the north-west beside the near-side sea, and the dry basalt lowland at 21° N, 40° W, whose sand is dark.

The paper lists four decisions, the inputs it lacked and six places where it disagrees with the record. Two of its findings reach beyond sand. The design run's land carries the climate model's default roughness length of 2 m everywhere, a forest's, so its 10 m wind over land reads about half of what the same air would blow over open ground. ## Limits

- The water arrives at once in the response, where it fills over centuries; the settled state hardly depends on that, the first millennium's state a little more.
- The creep's Love numbers are incompressible and end at degree 50, beyond which the present Moon's thick lid barely flows; the elastic part is compressible and runs to degree 359.
- The lakes keep their depths as a load. The drainage rebuilt on the new floor gives them their own levels.
- The rotational and tidal bulge re-forming about the turned axes moves the sea surface by metres and is left out, as are the water the crust's pores take up and its uneven load, which in the crustal-loading study's relaxed case moves the straits by 64–78 m and the Tenebrarum sill by about 190 m, and the lateral changes in the crust and lithosphere.
- Every cell below the common level counts toward the 28%, as the atlas counts it, so hollows away from the seas are held at the common level until the drainage gives them their own.
- Nothing here is validated against a lunar observation; the Moon carries no load of this size.
- The drainage on the redrawn floor and its works ran on the present design climate on the turned Moon at 8°, whose rain fell on the atlas's waters. [final_drainage.py](final_drainage.py) routes the design climate's estimate beside it, and the design routing, the globe, the moisture map, the districts, the satoyama map and the dryland rivers stand on that; the rivers' losses and the drylands' windows read the earlier climates until they are refreshed. The design state holds the near-side ocean, the south sea and Humboldtianum at the common level whatever their balance, to measure what each lacks or gains; the governed state the round chooses replaces it.
- Cuts are measured between 1.9 km cells: a cut's depth is the cells' heights above the water and its length the path through them. Tunnels are not considered and no cut is priced.
- The first step loaded the crust with the hollows full to the common level; with most of them dry the load there is 1.25 million km³ lighter, a second-order change the next pass of the sea-level equation can take up.
