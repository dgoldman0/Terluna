# Satoyama-like landscapes: what the atlas round looks for

The atlas round looks for satoyama-like landscapes beside the ecosystems already considered: mosaics of managed woodland, fields, ponds, irrigation channels, wetlands and settlements that support one another, adapted to the Moon's warmer climate, with satoumi along the salt South Pole–Aitken Sea's shores ([register](../../decisions.md#the-atlas-rounds-frame-16-november-2026), 16 November). Their criteria are set now, beside the drainage's rebuild, and their candidates are mapped with it and refreshed on the design climate's run on the final surface; their methods are chosen to limit methane. Much of the satoyama-like activity is to lie in the hybrid land, the managed fifth of the land beside the wild (11 October), under extensive management that limits methane and draws the most lasting output from it, with the methane-eating microbes boosted (register, 16 November). This note turns the Earth evidence into criteria that the round's products can test. The evidence is a bounded literature check of 16 November, saved with its sources and working extracts on the research drive (`atlas-round/satoyama_literature.md`); the figures this note relies on from the IPCC's tables, the Sri Lankan heritage proposal and Japan's mapping rule were checked against those extracts. What grows in these landscapes and how they are run stays with the ecology lane; the atlas round finds where the land and the water suit them.

## What the evidence shows

Every system the check found keeps the same order down the slope: woodland on the crests and upper slopes, settlements, gardens and rain-fed fields on the mid-slopes, flooded fields on terraces or valley floors, and a drainage line below, with water moving down through them by gravity and reused on the way. Japan's mapping rule for a satoyama cell asks that farmland, secondary grassland and secondary forest together cover at least half of a square kilometre, with at least two of the three present (Ministry of the Environment 2014). The woodland carried the fields: in the green-manure era a village needed forest and grassland about ten times its farmed area for green manure, fodder and fuel (Takemoto 2024; Ohsawa and Kitazawa 2009).

The closest match to the Moon's warmth is Sri Lanka's dry-zone cascade of village tanks, a recognized agricultural heritage system: an annual mean of about 27.7 °C at its heritage site, against the design climate's 301 K, and 800–2,000 mm of rain a year with a long dry season. A cascade is a chain of 4–10 small reservoirs in a catchment of 15–26 km², each tank's paddy draining into the next; at the heritage site 53 working tanks store about 1.2 m of water over the paddy they irrigate, which covers about 6% of the land, and every tank is ringed by named buffers: a tree belt, a sediment meadow and trap, and an interceptor wetland below the bund (FAO and Sri Lanka's Ministry of Agriculture 2017). The mountain terraces, Hani in Yunnan, Ifugao and Bali's subak, run on continuous outflow from forested upper catchments, with forest about half the land at Hani and terraces about 28%.

## Methane sets the scale of the flooded fields

The flooded paddies and the ponds are the mosaic's methane sources, and the Moon's methane budget is thin. The register holds methane at 1.9 ppm by biological means with technology making up the difference, because the Moon's own sinks would let it settle at 22–29 ppm (domain synthesis). With the lakes and seas kept nutrient-poor, the lake methane study leaves 0.35 Tg of methane a year of room under 1.87 ppm at the removal ledger's high-cost end, and 2.5 Tg at its low-cost end ([lake methane](../lake_methane/README.md)). At the IPCC's default rate for continuously flooded rice without organic inputs, 1.19 kg of methane a hectare a day, a 113-day season gives about 134 kg a hectare, so a square kilometre cropped twice a year gives about 27 t (arithmetic on the IPCC 2019 Refinement's Table 5.11). The whole room would then hold about 13,000 km² of such paddy at the high-cost end and 93,000 km² at the low-cost end, beside whatever the food systems already count, and less once ponds (183 kg a hectare a year) and channels (416) are added. The landscape eligible by terrain and water is far larger, so the methane budget, not the land, bounds the flooded part of these landscapes. The woodland, gardens and rain-fed fields emit little.

The hybrid land shows the scale *(arithmetic on the redrawn floor's drainage and the IPCC's factors)*. The land outside the seas and lakes on the redrawn floor is 23.4 million km², and a fifth of it 4.68 million km², the distributions study's hybrid fifth. Mosaics across it with Sri Lanka's paddy share of 6% would hold 281,000 km² of paddy, and even drained more than once a season and cropped once a year, about 7.4 t of methane per km², that paddy would give 2.1 Tg a year before its ponds and channels: most of the room at the removal ledger's low-cost end, and six times the room at its high-cost end. (The first figure, 2.3 Tg on 5.2 million km², counted the lakes above the common level as land; the candidate map corrected it.) Boosting the methane-eating microbes, at the paddies' oxic surface and roots, in the ponds and at the outlets, is therefore part of the design, beside the drainage and the paddy share the candidates are allowed.

The methods that cut it, with the IPCC's scaling factors and measured costs:

- **Drain the paddies**, more than once a season (×0.55 of continuous flooding) or at least once mid-season (×0.71), keeping the drying mild, the water table no lower than 15 cm, so that yields hold; a meta-analysis found yields fall only where the drying is severe.
- **Keep fields dry between crops**: more than 30 days of flooding before planting multiplies methane by 2.4, and rice after a year of upland crops or dry fallow takes ×0.59. The Sri Lankan and Balinese calendars, upland crops on the drained paddies in the off-season, are of this kind; Ifugao's year-round flooding, which keeps its earthen terraces from cracking, is the highest-emitting of the analogues.
- **Compost the straw** or plough it in early: per tonne, compost carries 0.17 and straw ploughed in more than 30 days ahead 0.19 of the methane of fresh straw ploughed in just before flooding.
- **Hold the nitrogen with the water**: drying raises nitrous oxide, by 105% in a meta-analysis and up to 33 kg a hectare a season on some Indian farms, which can outweigh the methane saved; shallow flooding of 5–7 cm with nitrogen managed alongside keeps both down.
- **Keep the ponds clean and oxygenated**: livestock fenced out of farm ponds cut their methane by 56% or more, and aeration and mixing reduce it in aquaculture ponds; ponds under a tenth of a hectare give about 40% of the world's diffusive open-water methane, so the size of the ponds matters too.
- **Boost the methane-eating microbes** at the sources, which the register's biological means and the aquatic programme's methanotrophs already rely on: inoculated paddies have shown 17–44% less methane in early trials, and how far engineered methanotrophs can raise that is for the ecology lane to size.

A Moon-specific question follows from the light cycle. The paddy's microbes make methane through the 15-day night as by day, so a drainage schedule fitted to the 29.5-day cycle is for the round and the ecology lane to work out.

## The first candidate map

[satoyama_candidates.py](satoyama_candidates.py) tests the criteria on the drainage of the redrawn floor (step 2's design state, with the upper seas full) and the present design climate, and writes [results/satoyama_candidates.json](results/satoyama_candidates.json) with a quick-look picture and every valley's measures on the research drive (`atlas-round/satoyama_candidates/`). Its units are side valleys: the land draining through a cell that drains 10–100 km² into a river that drains more, a lake or a sea, the scale of a tank cascade or a village's valley, like the yatsu valleys of Japan's satoyama. A valley has the order down the slope where gentle floor, under 1 in 20, makes up at least 3% of it in the lower half of its relief, the West Javan village's irrigated share, and land steeper than 15° or in the upper half of its relief makes up at least 30% of it for the woodland. Its rain must lie between 800 and 3,000 mm a year. The flooded fields it could carry are the least of its valley floor, the paddy its own outflow could water for one rice season a year beyond the rain that falls in the season (at the season's higher need of 1,880 mm), and 28% of it, Hani's terraced share.

**About a third of a fifth of the land qualifies, nearly all of it in the equatorial rain belt** *(screened at 1.9 km cells)*. The land outside the seas and lakes, 23.4 million km², holds 549,000 side valleys over 19.3 million km², 82% of it. 58% of them lack the order down the slope at these cells and 70% the rain. 50,600 valleys meet both, 1.57 million km², 6.7% of the land and a third of the hybrid fifth's 4.68 million km². Half their land, 785,000 km², is steep or high enough for the woodland, and 465,000 km² is gentle valley floor. Their rain runs from 880 to 2,210 mm a year (10th to 90th percentile, median 1,370 mm) and the climate model's surface temperature from 24.5 to 28.9 °C, close to the tank cascades' 27.7 °C. They lie where the design climate rains, by latitude on the turned Moon:

**The flooded fields the candidates could carry fit the room at its low-cost end and four times exceed it at its high-cost end** *(arithmetic on the screen and the IPCC's factors)*. The candidates could carry 204,000 km² of flooded fields, 13% of their land; for half the valleys their own outflow, not their floor, sets the limit. Drained more than once a season and cropped once a year, that paddy gives 1.51 Tg of methane a year, and 0.89 Tg with upland crops before each rice crop. The room under 1.87 ppm holds 338,000 km² of such paddy at the removal ledger's low-cost end and 47,000 km² at its high-cost end, so at the low-cost end every flooded field the candidates could carry fits, with about 1 Tg a year left for ponds (18.3 t of methane per km² of pond) and channels, and at the high-cost end only a quarter of them do. At Sri Lanka's paddy share of 6% the candidates would hold 94,000 km² of paddy and give 0.41–0.70 Tg a year.

**The salt sea has little shallow water** *(measured at 1.9 km cells)*. Of the South Pole–Aitken Sea's 2.41 million km², 2,600 km² lies less than 10 m deep and 5,400 km² less than 20 m, along 37,800 km of shore, at every latitude, the most between 15° and 30° (700 km² under 10 m) and between 60° and 75° (640 km²). The basin's walls are steep, and the 76,000 km² the redrawn surface floods along its shores mostly lies deeper, so satoumi beds at eelgrass's 0.5–1.0 m below low water would be narrow fringes at these cells; the falls survey's finer heights and built shallows are the round's to weigh.

What the map leaves open: the slopes at 1.9 km are gentler than the ground within a cell, so the valley floors and the woodland are first estimates; the rain and temperature are the T21 climate model's, and the refresh after the climate run on the final surface replaces them; the falls survey adds the falls on finer heights where they meet the candidates; and which candidates lie in the hybrid land is a choice the map informs, since the hybrid fifth is a share of each zone, unmapped. In the equatorial band the candidates alone would take nearly all of its hybrid fifth, which bears on whether that fifth grows there.

## Gaps the evidence leaves

No slope beyond which only woodland stands; no measured methane budget for a whole satoyama-type landscape; no tank water-surface areas for the Sri Lankan site; no numbers for Japan's irrigation ponds; and several heritage dossiers, among them the Japan Satoyama Satoumi Assessment and UNESCO's nominations for Hani, Ifugao and Bali, which could not be opened, so their figures came through search summaries and are flagged in the saved report.
