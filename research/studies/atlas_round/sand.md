# Wind-blown sand on the Open Moon: a first study

19 November 2026. The register's row of this date places the kinds of ground on the globe and gives sand seas to a study of wind and sediment supply. This paper is that study's first answer. It asks where on the design surface loose sand gathers into sand seas, dune fields or sheets, how much of it moves and what forms it takes. [sand.py](sand.py) computes it and writes [results/sand.json](results/sand.json) (schema `terluna.research.atlas-round-sand/1`) and the grid product `/media/projectspace/terluna-research/atlas-round/sand/sand_16ppd.npz`; [test_sand.py](test_sand.py) checks it; the maps are PNG files in `/media/projectspace/terluna-research/session-tools/agents/atlas_sand/maps/`.

Labels: *project result* is an output of a committed or branch study, named with its file; *literature* is a published value, with how it was read; *calculation* is arithmetic done here, in sand.py unless stated; *inference* is reasoning from these without a calculation; *proposal* is a suggestion for the author to weigh. Figures without a label in parts B, E and F are calculations of sand.py, recorded in results/sand.json.

## The answer

**The Open Moon's air moves sand easily, and the land offers it little sand to move.** In lunar gravity a wind of 3.3 m/s at 10 m over a sand surface starts feldspar grains a quarter of a millimetre across, 2.7 m/s keeps them moving, and a wind above those speeds carries about seven times the sand that Earth's air carries at the same friction velocity. The design climate's sunlit hours bring such winds over most of the dry land. Bare loose sand there would drift at about 5 m³ per metre of width a year under the climate run's 3-day means alone, at about 36 with the unresolved gusts at their central value and at about 150 at their high end; Earth's sand seas of intermediate wind energy lie at 16 to 31.

Supply and cover therefore draw the map. The mantle is a silty sand, and about half of its sand-sized grains are welded glass that breaks up in transport. The cratered ground catches loose sand in its small bowls. Most rivers end in lakes. Plants and crusts hold the arid and semi-arid land. In the central case, ten thousand years after the land dried, 168,749 km² lie under sand, 0.76% of the land. Of that, 155,407 km² are thin sheets, streaks and sand on the floors of small bowls and 13,342 km² are dunes, in small fields at river ends and on downwind shores. A third of the sand lies in the hyper-arid land, on 16% of its ground, and the rest lies thinner over the dry margin of the arid land, on 2.5% of that class's ground. 26,390 km² of the sand, 16%, is bare and moves within a year.

No sand sea forms in any case computed. A sea of 10,000 km² twenty metres deep holds 200 km³, which 100 km of downwind shore delivers in 4 million years and one river end of the hyper-arid land in 7 million. Across the cases the area under sand runs from 56,020 km² a thousand years after drying to 276,383 km² with every supply bracket at its high end. The gusts' whole range moves it only from 148,097 to 176,027 km², because supply limits it; withholding crust upkeep from sand raises it to 249,033 km².

Three findings bear on work beyond this study. The climate run's land is as rough as forest everywhere (a roughness length of 2 m), so its 10 m wind over land reads about half of what the same air would blow over open ground. The record's figures for calm desert winds come from cloud-resolving rings whose land was kept moist; the later rings on the design ground blow about twice as hard by day. And the one route to a sand sea within ten thousand years is a lever the project already holds: the sand in the sediment that the terrain study's loop returns uphill.

## A. What moves sand here

**Grains start to move at 0.61 of Earth's friction velocity, and a wind above the threshold carries 6.65 times the sand it would carry on Earth at the same friction velocity and threshold.** The first figure is the record's 0.6 again; the second follows from the flux law's direct dependence on gravity. Every relation below was fitted on Earth and is carried to 1.62 m/s² unchanged; the last paragraph of this part says how far outside the fitted range that lies.

**Starting.** Shao and Lu's (2000) threshold gives the friction velocity at which wind alone lifts grains of diameter d from a dry loose bed: u*ft = A √(((ρp − ρa)/ρa) g d + γ/(ρa d)), with A = 0.111 and γ, the strength of the forces between grains, 1.65 to 5 × 10⁻⁴ N/m, 3 × 10⁻⁴ centrally *(literature, through the project's aerosol literature note, which read Kok et al. 2012 at the source)*. Gravity enters the weight term and air density both terms. The threshold is lowest at d = √(γ/((ρp − ρa) g)), where it equals A √(2 √(γ (ρp − ρa) g)/ρa): it goes as g to the power ¼ and as the inverse square root of the air's density, whatever the grain's density or the cohesion. For the Moon's grains in the run's own surface air over land, 1.35 kg/m³ *(calculation)*:

The record's 264 µm and 3.2 to 4.0 m/s are the same relation for quartz in air of 1.44 kg/m³; the grain's mineral moves the threshold by 2 to 3%. By size, feldspar of 40, 63, 100, 130, 264, 500, 1,000 and 2,000 µm starts at friction velocities of 0.26, 0.21, 0.18, 0.16, 0.145, 0.16, 0.21 and 0.29 m/s, which over a 1 mm roughness are 10 m winds of 6.1, 4.9, 4.1, 3.7, 3.3, 3.7, 4.8 and 6.6 m/s. The wind therefore picks grains of 0.1 to 0.5 mm first, the coarse half of the mantle's sand, and the mantle's median grain of 40 to 130 µm rises mostly when those grains strike it.

**Keeping on.** Once grains are moving their impacts splash others up, and the motion continues below the starting wind, down to the impact threshold. Bagnold's ratio for Earth, 0.82, is carried over. Longer hops let the wind speed a grain up for longer, which lowers the ratio, and denser air damps hops, which raises it: Mars's is about 0.1 and Titan's above 1 *(literature, Kok et al. 2012, through the aerosol note)*. The lunar value is unmeasured; the aerosol note brackets it at 0.5 to 1.0, which puts the wind that keeps feldspar moving between 1.7 and 3.3 m/s at 10 m over a 1 mm roughness.

**How much a wind carries.** The flux law is Q = C (ρa/g) u*it (u*² − u*it²) in kilograms per metre of width a second above the impact threshold u*it, with C = 5 from Earth's 250 µm sand, a hop of 0.1 m and a speed gain of 1 m/s a hop (Kok et al. 2012, their equation 2.34, after Durán et al. 2011; *literature, through the aerosol note*). Gravity enters twice: as 1/g, because a hop lasts longer and each unit of momentum the wind gives the grains carries them farther, and through the threshold. Air density enters as ρa and through the threshold. At equal friction velocity and threshold the Moon's air carries (1.35/1.225)(9.807/1.624) = 6.65 times Earth's load *(calculation)*; White's older form, Q ∝ ρa u*³/g, has the same factor. A lunar breeze of 4 m/s carries about as much sand as an 8 m/s wind on Earth.

**How far a grain hops.** Without drag a grain that leaves the bed with an upward and a forward speed of v each rises v²/2g and lands 2v²/g downwind. For speeds of one to two friction velocities that is 0.7 to 2.8 cm high and 3 to 11 cm long at a friction velocity of 0.15 m/s, and 1.9 to 7.7 cm high and 8 to 31 cm long at 0.25 m/s *(calculation, an upper bound: drag shortens it)*. At the same lift-off speed a lunar hop is 6.04 times Earth's. At each world's own threshold the lunar grain leaves more slowly, and its hop is about twice Earth's *(calculation: (0.145/0.237)² × 6.04 = 2.3)*.

**The smallest dunes.** A flat bed of sand breaks into dunes at a wavelength set by the saturation length, the distance over which the flux adjusts to a change in the wind. For Earth's dune sand it measures 0.7 m in a wind tunnel and 1.0 ± 0.2 m in the field and hardly changes with wind strength *(literature, Andreotti, Claudin and Pouliquen 2010, read at the source as its preprint)*. That is about 2.2 drag lengths, (ρp/ρa) d, for 180 µm quartz *(calculation: 0.86 m; the factor recalled from the same group's papers)*, and gravity is absent from the drag length. On that scaling the Moon's 264 µm feldspar has a saturation length of 1.2 m and its smallest dunes a spacing of 14 to 23 m, twelve to twenty saturation lengths *(calculation; the multiple recalled from Claudin and Andreotti 2006)*, against Earth's 20 m or so. If the hop's length set the saturation length instead, the lunar figure would about double. Sand of 130 µm gives 7 to 12 m and sand of 500 µm 27 to 44 m.

**The largest dunes.** Dunes merge and grow until their spacing reaches the depth of the layer the Sun stirs each day, whose capping inversion confines the flow over them as a river's surface confines the flow over its bed; Earth's giant dunes run from 300 m apart on cool coasts to 3.5 km in the hot deserts *(literature, Andreotti et al. 2009, search summary)*, and Titan's about 3 km under a boundary layer of that depth *(recalled, Lorenz et al. 2010)*. The Open Moon's sunlit mixed layer averages 5.1 to 5.2 km on the cloud-resolving rings whose land was kept moist and reaches 13.2 km by day on the ring on the design ground *(project results, the desert analysis and climate/crm/README.md)*. Given sand and time its dunes could therefore grow to spacings of 5 to 13 km. A dune a twentieth as high as its spacing λ holds λ²/40 of sand for each metre of crest: a spacing of 1 km takes 25,000 m² a metre, 830 years of a 30 m³/m/yr drift, and a spacing of 5 km takes 21,000 years *(calculation)*. Part C finds a supply too small for either.

**What is carried over, and how far outside its range the Moon lies.** Fitted on Earth and unchanged here: Shao and Lu's A and γ, the impact ratio 0.82, the flux coefficient 5, the sand surface's roughness of 0.1 to 1 mm, the saturation length's 2.2 drag lengths and the dunes' twelve to twenty saturation lengths, and a dune height of a twentieth of the spacing. Gravity lies outside the fitted range by a factor of 6.04. The ratio of grain to air density, 2,020, is close to Earth's 2,160, and the Galileo number √((ρp/ρa − 1) g d³)/ν is 17 for 260 µm feldspar against 38 for Earth's 250 µm quartz, the value of 150 µm sand on Earth *(calculation)*; in those two respects the Moon's sand lies inside Earth's experience. The one measured hint about gravity itself comes from parabolic flights at Mars's pressure: forces between grains weaken as their weight falls, which lowers thresholds further, and a linear fit to those flights gives a lunar threshold of 0.61 to 0.65 of Earth's, beside Shao and Lu's 0.61 *(literature, Musiolik et al. 2018 and Kruss et al. 2020, through the aerosol note)*. No lunar value is clamped to an Earth limit anywhere in the reckoning.

## B. The design's winds as movers of sand

**Carried to a sand surface, the climate run's own 3-day means exceed the threshold 5.5% of the time over land, and bare loose sand would drift at a land mean of 3 m³ per metre a year; with the part the run cannot hold the figure is 10, 32 or 111 at the low, central and high ends of the range the cloud-resolving cases give.** Over the hyper-arid and arid land the four figures are 5, 11, 36 and 150.

**What the run's fields are.** The design climate's run is `A28D_301k_design_l15_snow_s936_settled`, model years 45 to 59, 1,800 output means; its winds are used as the run gives them, at its own 301.0 K, where the design's temperatures and rain are scaled to 300.31 K. Each output is a mean over 143 steps of 30 minutes, 2.98 days, a tenth of a lunar day, on cells of about 170 km. `uas` and `vas` are the model's own 10 m wind (`NWIND10 = 1` in its `fluxmod_namelist`): at every step the wind of the lowest level, at sigma 0.99415 or about 313 m, is brought down on Geleyn's (1988) profile with the stability factor of the model's momentum transfer and its own roughness length, limited to between none and all of that wind, and the output is the mean of those steps (`WIND10_PATCHES` in climate/gcm/exoplasim_run.py). `tauu` and `tauv` are the surface stress of PlaSim's bulk transfer, with Beljaars's gust in unstable air (`NGUST = 1`), as means over the same steps (climate/gcm/README.md, "The surface stress and the 10 m wind in the output"). The roughness length over land is PlaSim's default, 2 m in every land cell: the run's `landmod_namelist` leaves `dz0land` alone and its surface files hold heights and the land mask only *(project result, read in the model's source and the run's folder; climate/crm/README.md states the same)*. ## C. What sand there is

**The land offers the wind about 24 km³ of durable sand in its first ten thousand years in the central case, a layer 0.5 cm thick if spread over the hyper-arid and arid land: 19 km³ at river ends, 3 km³ on downwind shores and 2 km³ from the mantle where it lies bare.** Every rate here is a bracket; the central values are named with their ranges.

**The parent material.** Most of the dry land is feldspathic highland rock with about 5% FeO; mare basalt with about 15% FeO stays dry on the remnants the seas leave; the rock holds almost no free quartz. The mantle is a fine, glassy silty sand with a median grain of 40 to 130 µm and 10 to 20% finer than 20 µm *(project result, the soils study's sections 1.2 and 2.1, with Carrier 2003 by search summary)*. About four tenths of it is sand between 63 µm and 2 mm *(inference from that median; the river deltas study puts 41% of the young regolith's load on the bed)*. A large part of that sand is agglutinate, grains of mineral and rock welded by impact glass, a quarter to three fifths of a mature mantle *(recalled, Lunar Sourcebook)*, and welded glass breaks into its fine parts under the blows of saltation where single crystals and glass beads last. The reckoning takes half of the sand-sized grains as durable, in a bracket of three to seven tenths *(proposal)*. Feldspar and mafic sands wear faster than quartz, and volcanic glass persists under mechanical wear *(literature, Cornwall et al. 2015, search summary)*; no rate per kilometre was found. Two things lengthen a grain's life here *(inference)*: at the Moon's threshold a grain strikes at about 0.6 of the speed it would on Earth, a third of the energy, and it strikes fewer times in each kilometre.

**The mantle where it lies bare.** In the first centuries the raw ground is all source. As the air thickens the wind starts raw mantle at 14, 8 and 4 m/s at 0.1 atm, 0.3 atm and full pressure *(project result, the conservation study's section 6.3)*, lifts the fines, which settle at 0.17 of Earth's speed and travel far, rolls the sand, and leaves a lag of fragments that armours the ground, the pavement the soils study keeps whole in the hyper-arid land. Winnowing about 5 cm of mantle makes a lag, in a bracket of 2 to 20 cm *(proposal)*, and frees 1 cm of durable sand over the ground winnowed (0.2 to 5.6 cm across the brackets). The cratered surface catches it: 19% of the young land lies inside bowls of 10 m and more *(project result, the storm-water study through the soils study)*, and at a mean depth of 0.3 m those bowls hold 5.7 cm of sand spread over all the ground, more than the winnowing frees. The mantle's own sand therefore stays where it started, on the floors of small bowls. It stays loose sand only where no cover forms over it. Under plants and crust it becomes the sandy soil the soils study names for the arid land. Counted so, it is 1.8 km³ in the central case, nearly all in the hyper-arid land.

**Rivers.** The terrain study lowers the land by 79, 77, 57, 35 and 25 m in a million years at 0–15°, 15–30°, 30–45°, 45–60° and beyond 60°, its central case, read here at each band's middle and joined by straight lines between, with slow and fast cases at 0.2 and 2 times that; 41% of the rock settles in dry hollows, 38% in lakes and 21% in the seas *(project result, research/studies/terrain_through_time/README.md)*. At four tenths sand-sized and half of that durable, a square kilometre of catchment sends 13 m³ of durable sand a year to its river's end at 50° and 9 m³ at 70° under the mean rain of its latitude *(calculation)*. The terrain study spreads its erosion as rain to the power 0.31, and the reckoning applies that rule to each river end's rain against the mean rain of the land at its latitude: a hyper-arid end at 70°, under 12 mm a year where the land averages 60 mm, yields 0.61 of that figure. The design routing has 939,245 cells where the land's drainage meets standing water or ends in a dry hollow. Each is credited with its catchment up to 5,000 km², since lakes upstream hold back the rest; the terrain study itself was routed on the older surface and is not rerun here. The wind takes what plants and crusts leave it (part D): 19 km³ in ten thousand years in the central case.

**Shores.** Waves about a metre high on the seas and the wider lakes, and a monthly tide that bares 20,000 to 30,000 km² of flats for days at a time *(project results, the river deltas study and the soils study's section 2.6)*, lay sand where the wind can reach it, and the wind takes it inland on the shores it blows onto. No wave or tide model stands behind a rate. The reckoning offers 0.5 m³ per metre of downwind shore a year on a water 100 km wide or more, in a bracket of 0.1 to 3 (Earth's coastal dunes take 1 to 20 on sandy coasts, *recalled*), and less in proportion on narrower waters: 250,568 shore cells, 3 km³ in ten thousand years after cover.

**Pans and lakes whose levels move.** The closed hollows' floors are clay, cemented by calcite, magnesium clays and opal at 3 to 67 mm a millennium, and their soda crusts raise dust when they dry *(project result, the soils study's sections 2.6 and 3.5)*. They offer the wind silt, clay pellets and salt, the makings of dust and of clay dunes on a pan's downwind rim, and little sand beyond what their rivers bring, which the river ends above already count *(inference)*. The record is silent on how far the design's closed lakes rise and fall, so no bared lake floor is counted.

**What removes sand.** Water takes what blows into it: 2.8 km³ of the 24 in the central case. Wet country weathers feldspar and glass to loam and clay, the top metre through in 3,500 to 36,500 years at the Moon's runoff *(project result, the soils study's section 1.2)*; in dry land little water passes and sand lasts *(inference)*. Carbonate and opal cement sand that lies still: 3 to 67 mm a millennium on hollow floors and about 18 cm a millennium behind a shore where groundwater is drawn up and dried *(project results, the soils study)*, so sand held for millennia sets as hardpan or sandy soil, and only sand that keeps moving stays sand. Rivers bury or rework sand on their fans. Wear to dust is in the durable share.

## D. What holds sand still

**Cover holds nearly all the land: plants stop sand above about 15% cover, which the globe's cover curve passes at an aridity of 0.09, and a kept crust stops it across the arid and semi-arid land, so sand moves freely only in the hyper-arid land, 362,000 km² or 1.6% of the design surface's land once the moisture map's dryness is carried to it smoothly, and on the driest arid margin.**

**Plants.** The globe draws plant cover as 0.02 + 0.98/(1 + (0.22/aridity)^2.2) (`appearance.plant_cover`, a display rule of the round): 3% at the hyper-arid land's mean aridity of 0.028, 6% at the arid class's dry edge of 0.05, 22% at its mean of 0.12, 46% at 0.2, 86% at 0.5 and 97% at 1. On Owens Lake's sand sheets the flux fell exponentially as salt grass thickened and all but ended above 15% cover *(literature, Lancaster and Baas 1998, search summary of the abstract)*; the reckoning takes exp(−cover/0.05), a twentieth left at 15% *(proposal)*. Sand then moves at 54% of its bare rate at 3% cover, 33% at 6%, 10% at 12% and 1% at 22%. The plants are engineered, and Earth's grass is a reference point for them; the response of the Moon's plants to moving sand is a design question.

A second Earth measure reads the same land more freely. Lancaster's (1988) mobility index divides the percentage of the time the wind blows above the threshold by the aridity; dunes are fully active above 200, active with planted hollows and lower slopes from 100 to 200 *(literature, search summary)*, active on their crests alone from 50 to 100 and still below 50 *(recalled)*. The central winds give the hyper-arid land 918, the arid land 202, the semi-arid 61 and the dry sub-humid 28; the run's means alone give 367, 81, 25 and 9 *(calculation)*. By the index the arid land's dunes would be largely active under the central winds, where the cover curve and the Owens Lake rule hold them at 22% cover. The index rests on how Earth's dune plants thin as sand moves; the curve gives the cover of settled ground. The map follows the curve, which the globe draws, and the difference is an open input.

**Crusts.** The soils study keeps the open ground of the arid and semi-arid land under a biological crust between sparse plants, watched and reseeded by machines; this is that study's proposal, and no register row yet chooses it. It keeps none in the hyper-arid land, where a crust has too few wet hours to grow. Between the two the reckoning lets the kept crust's share of the open ground rise smoothly with the dryness: a tenth at an aridity of 0.029, half at 0.05, the divide between the hyper-arid and the arid class, and nine tenths at 0.087 *(proposal)*. A full crust raises the threshold 4.8 times by the mean of Earth's wind-tunnel data, to a 10 m wind of 16 m/s over sand, and a crust one or five years after a disturbance holds to 9.3 and 17 m/s *(project result, the soils study's section 2.2, from Rodríguez-Caballero et al. 2022 and Belnap and Gillette 1997)*. A full crust therefore holds sand against the steady winds of every case, whose 99th percentile over sand is at most 6.6 m/s, and against all but the strongest storm gusts, which reach 14 m/s over sand in the central case and 17 m/s in the high one; a crust a year old gives way above 9.3 m/s. A crust grows on sand that lies still. Sand that floods, waves or traffic have just laid is loose until the crust returns, and trampling lowers a crust's threshold by 73 to 92%. The reckoning takes a tenth of crusted land's sand as fresh and loose at any time, and lets the wind take three tenths of river and shore sand before the crust returns *(proposals, in brackets of 0.03 to 0.3 and 0.1 to 1)*. Whether the upkeep reaches sand at all is the lever of part G, and the variant `opensand` withholds it.

**Wetting.** A sand surface stays damp for about a day after a storm leaves 10 mm *(proposal, in a bracket of 6 to 72 hours)*: 1.6% of the time in the arid land, 7% in the semi-arid and 47% in the humid *(calculation from the run's rain)*. The air does not wet sand. The deserts' nights at 301 K run 57 to 60% humid where dew needs about 90%, and the design-ground ring's days 38% *(project results, the coast-water study through the soils study, and climate/crm/README.md)*. Between 40 and 65% humidity Earth's thresholds move little and in both directions (Ravi et al. 2006, through the aerosol note); the aerosol note's bound of 1.0 to 1.5 on the threshold is left out centrally.

**The stony lag.** In the hyper-arid land the pavement holds the mantle beneath it, so the lag ends the mantle's supply and lets loose sand pass over it, lodging among the stones and in the bowls. Its upkeep is restraint, traffic on set ways *(project result, the soils study's section 2.1)*.

**Shelter.** On the 1.9 km grid a cell lies in the lee where the ground within 15 km upwind rises above it by more than 3 in 100 of the distance, fully at 10 in 100, the reach of the parted flow behind a rim, about ten heights *(recalled)*; gentle ground 150 m or more below the mean within 10 km counts as a floor. Sheltered ground slows the drift and keeps what arrives.

## E. Where sand gathers

**The drift runs from each pole towards the equator across the polar dry lands, steadily, and meets the opposing flow of the middle latitudes in belts of convergence at 40 to 60° N and 30 to 50° S; with the supply found here those belts would take millions of years to gather a sand sea, and what forms in ten thousand is local: sand in the bowls, and dune fields within tens of kilometres of the river ends and shores that feed them.**

**Pathways at the run's scale.** Poleward of 60° nine tenths of the central drift is net and heads for the equator: towards 187 to 190° clockwise from north in the north and towards 317 to 336° in the south, in the turned Moon's frame, whose directions differ from today's by a few degrees away from the poles and by tens of degrees close to them. At 50 to 60° the net drift still heads equatorward (towards 149° in the north and 354° in the south); at 40 to 50° N it heads towards 61°, east-north-east, and at 30 to 40° S towards 163°, polewards. The flows meet between: 85% of the land at 50 to 60° N and 65% at 40 to 50° N lies under converging drift, and 79% at 40 to 50° S and 66% at 30 to 40° S. The map `sand_drift_potential.png` shows the directions cell by cell. A flux at the wind's full capacity would lay down 0.13 m in a thousand years at the 90th percentile of the land and at most 0.5 m, in the hyper-arid lowland near 21° N, 40° W (region L15) and at 54° N, 49° E (L04), 20° S on the prime meridian (L27), 43° S, 132° W (L37) and 44° N, 103° E (L06).

**Where the pathways end.** They end in water, which takes 2.8 km³ of the central case's 24; in wet country, where cover stops the sand at the first cells it reaches; and in shelter. The convergence belts lie in arid and semi-arid land, which the crust and the plants hold, so sand that left the polar deserts would stop at their edge. The cratered ground takes a toll on the way: every cell's bowls fill before sand passes on.

**Time.** Supply sets the pace, and the wind could do the work many times over: a front 100 km wide at the central drift carries a cubic kilometre in 363 years. One river end in the hyper-arid land at 70°, with 5,000 km² behind it, delivers about 26,900 m³ of durable sand a year, and 100 km of downwind sea shore 50,000 m³: a dune field of 100 km² ten metres deep, 1 km³, in about 37,000 and 20,000 years. A sand sea of 10,000 km² twenty metres deep takes 7.4 and 4.0 million years from one such source, and would need about 740 such river ends or 400 such shores feeding one basin to form in ten thousand years.

**The stage shown.** The first centuries on raw ground are the winnowing: the fines leave, the lag forms, the mantle's sand settles in the bowls, and the map is the variant `young`, a thousand years on, with deep bowls and a tenth of the rivers' sand. The central map is the land at ten thousand years, where the soils study sets the end of the young land and the start of the mature; the record gives no date for the surface the atlas shows, and this is an assumption to confirm. In the mature land beyond, the river ends and shores keep delivering at the same rates while the bowls fill and the hollows wear down: the dune fields grow in proportion to time, and sand seas wait for hundreds of thousands of years and for a way through the cover.

## F. A first map

**The central map puts sand on 0.76% of the land: a thin speckle of sand-floored bowls over the hyper-arid land, with sand on 16% of its ground in all, and small dune fields at river ends and on downwind shores, 13,342 km² in all, their dunes a few metres high and a median 91 m from crest to crest.** Its sand is 13% on mare rock by area, dark basalt sand, and the rest pale feldspar and glass.

**The product.** `sand_16ppd.npz` holds, on the design surface's grid (2,880 rows from 90° N, 5,760 columns from 0° E, on today's Moon), `sand_share`, `active_share`, `form`, `crest_azimuth_deg`, `crest_spacing_m` and `drift_azimuth_deg` for the central case and the same six with the suffix `__nogust`, `__highgust`, `__opensand`, `__young` and `__ample`. It adds `sand_thickness_m` for each case (the cell's sand spread evenly, bulk metres); `drift_potential_m2_yr` for the central, `__nogust` and `__highgust` winds, `drift_resultant_m2_yr` and `drift_direction_deg` (the net drift and its direction over bare loose sand on all land, central); and `basalt_share`, `plant_cover` and `shelter`. Its `metadata` holds the schema, the grid, the frame, the hashes, the evidence statement and the reading rule. Form 1 covers sheets, streaks and sand on bowl floors; form 5 covers dunes held under plants or under crust; crest azimuths run from 0 to 180° and are NaN for sheets and star dunes.

**The frame.** The run's winds are on the turned Moon. Each product cell's place is turned with the design surface's `rotation_today_to_turned`; each drift vector is turned as a vector in three dimensions before it is interpolated and resolved along today's east and north. The test checks a wind blowing along a turned parallel at eight places, against a finite step along that parallel and against the bearing of the turned pole, which lies a quarter turn to its left.

**How the map is made.** The scheme is a screening on the 1.9 km grid. The climate's fields (the dryness, the rain, the drift, its direction and the time above the threshold) reach that grid as the round's look carries its climate: regridded at 4 cells a degree on the turned Moon and smoothed over 0.7 of the run's spacing, so that no edge of a 170 km cell is left in them; the dryness is the moisture map's rain over demand, continuous, and the cover and the crust's hold are smooth functions of it. The scheme places the offered sand: the mantle's on all bare ground, the rivers' at their mouths, the shores' on the cells the central drift blows onto from water. Where a river end's or a shore's sand would stand more than 2 m deep on one cell it is laid over the land round it, since the cell stood for a fan or a beach. Sand first fills the cell's bowls, whose floors it covers as the square root of the share filled. What is over moves downwind along the case's net drift, from cell to neighbouring cell, as far as the drift at each place could carry a body of its thickness in the land's age (a thin streak travels far, a thick dune field slowly, a cell keeps at most 30 m, and no body makes more than 200 moves), with cover and shelter scaling the drift; each cell holds back a twentieth of a moving body at every move, up to four fifths in full shelter; sand that reaches water is lost. At each move a share of the body steps to either side, half of what the net drift lacks of the total, within 0.02 and 0.3, so that sand fans out where the winds vary. At the end the carried sand and the bowls' sand are shared over 4.7 km, since the scheme places neither to a cell. Free sand of thickness h covers 1 − exp(−h/1.5 m) of the ground. A body under half a metre deep where the scheme sets it down is drawn as a sheet or streaks and a deeper one as dunes, and a cell takes the form of the bodies most of its free sand came from: crescent and transverse dunes where the net drift is 0.8 or more of the total, linear between 0.3 and 0.8, star below 0.3 (Fryberger and Dean's classes, *recalled*), and held dunes where less than a fifth of the sand is bare and moving. Crest spacings follow from the sand at hand and the time, between the smallest dunes' 14 m and the mixed layer's 5.1 km.

**What is the land's and what is the method's.** The edges the map keeps are the land's: shores, where sand starts or is lost; the lee of rims and scarps and the floors below them, where shelter changes within a cell or two; and the ends of pathways in water. Its gradients over tens of kilometres are the climate's, at the smoothing above. A first version carried the climate's classes cell by cell, moved sand along single lines of cells and left a river end's sand on the cell of its mouth; it showed straight edges at the classes' bounds, bars along the moves and square blocks at the top of the scale, and those were the method's. The check is the share of neighbouring cell pairs, away from water and from changes of shelter of more than 0.2, whose `sand_share` differs by more than 0.03: 8 in a million of all such pairs in the central case and 3 in 100,000 of those that hold sand, with a largest step of 0.046, against 2.0%, 11.7% and 0.84 in that first version (across the six cases none to 0.2% of the pairs that hold sand, against 4.8 to 13.3%; the steps that remain are the margins of the ample case's dune fields). The first version's areas were 129,587 km² under sand in the central case with 4,669 km² of dunes, and 112,324, 138,187, 224,208, 54,254 and 240,911 km² in the cases nogust, highgust, opensand, young and ample. The areas under sand rose because the crust's hold now grows through the arid margin, where the first version set it whole at the class's edge, and because thin sand shared over more ground covers more of it; the dunes' area rose because a cell now takes its form from the bodies its sand came from.

## G. Decisions for the author and open inputs

Where sand lies follows from the winds, the supply and the cover. Four things a lever controls are open.

**1. Whether sand is kept open as a wild land of its own.** It is open because the soils study's crust upkeep is a proposal for all open ground of the arid and semi-arid land, and sand can only move where that upkeep stops. The options: (a) the crust is kept on sand as on other ground, the central case, 168,749 km² of sand with 26,390 km² moving, most of the moving sand in the hyper-arid land; (b) the upkeep stops at sand bodies, the case `opensand`, 249,033 km² with 48,374 km² moving and 21,307 km² of dunes; (c) chosen dune fields are kept open and the rest held. Recommendation: (b) in the wild land, with sand held near ways, settlements and waters that it would foul. The register asks that the drylands' soils vary to diversify their biomes (1 November), open sand is a ground of its own kind, the areas are under 1% of the land, and leaving it open is upkeep saved. It costs blowing sand on any set way that crosses a field, and 3.3 km³ more sand into the waters in ten thousand years. It decides which of the two fields the globe draws, and the soils follow-up would add open sand to the soils it keeps.

**2. Whether to make a sand sea.** It is open because the globe's dry land wants patches at the scale wind makes on Earth, and the winds and the supply here make none in ten thousand years. The lever is the terrain study's sediment loop, which dredges the lakes' deltas and returns the rock uphill: the lakes' 38% of 3.55 Gt a year holds about 0.17 km³ of durable sand a year *(calculation: 0.38 × 3.55 Gt × 0.4 × 0.5 at 1,600 kg/m³)*. Set down in a convergence of the hyper-arid land it would build a sea of 10,000 km² twenty metres deep in about 1,200 years, and the winds found here would shape it into dunes within centuries and hold it together where they converge. The options: (a) none, the loop returns everything as a fall of dust, as the terrain study has it; (b) one or two placed seas, in the polar margin (L06, L03) or the basalt lowland (L15); (c) feeding the natural dune fields at river ends. Recommendation: (a) for the first design, and (b) as a study if the dry land still reads as one colour once the stony ground, the pans and the cover are drawn. Hauling 200 km³ of sand 500 km by rail at the loop's 107 kJ a tonne-kilometre takes 1.7 × 10¹⁹ J, 0.45 GW through those 1,200 years *(calculation)*, the order of the whole loop's 0.2 to 11 GW. It blocks nothing now.

**3. Whether to compute the desert's wind over open ground.** It is open because the winds span the whole range of this study, a drift potential of 5 to 150 m³/m/yr in the dry land, on two-dimensional rings at one latitude with a land of 10 cm roughness, and a new cloud-resolving run is the author's call. The options: (a) carry the range; (b) one three-dimensional box on the design ground in the polar dry land, with a smooth surface and its 10 m wind written step by step. Recommendation: (b) when the climate work next takes up the dry land. The sand's area hardly depends on it, since supply limits that, while the sand's reach, the dunes' forms and their directions do. The flat rings took 11.5 to 12.3 wall hours each on 2 to 4 threads *(project result, the desert analysis)*; a box costs more. It blocks nothing now.

**4. What roughness the climate's land should have.** It is open because the design run's land is as rough as forest everywhere, deserts included, which halves its 10 m wind over open ground and shapes its stress, its evaporation and its heat everywhere on land. The options: (a) leave it and carry winds to each surface afterwards, as here; (b) let the roughness follow the land's cover in the next runs. Recommendation: raise it with the climate work as a question, since a change moves the design climate itself and belongs with the model's other refinements. This study needs only (a).

**Inputs this study lacked**, in the order of what each would change:

1. The grain sizes of the developed dry soils and the share of sand-sized grains that last in saltation: the mantle's sand is half welded glass, and the supply scales with what survives.
2. The rivers' sand at their ends on the design surface: the terrain study's routing on the older surface, rerun on the design routing with its lakes, in place of a capped catchment.
3. A rate for shore sand from the design seas' waves and monthly tide, and how far the closed lakes' levels move.
4. The wind at 10 m over open ground below the run's scales (decision 3), and sea, lake and slope breezes.
5. How the engineered plants answer moving sand, and whether an engineered crust can take a moving sand surface: the cover curve and Lancaster's index disagree about the arid land.
6. The impact threshold and the flux coefficient at lunar gravity in dense air, by experiment or by a grain-scale model; with them the depth of mantle the wind winnows before a lag forms.
7. The age of the surface the atlas shows.
8. Cementing of sand in dry land, and the threshold's change between 40 and 65% humidity.

**Where this study disagrees with the record.**

- The record's calm desert winds (the desert analysis's section 1.6: a median of 0.9 to 1.1 m/s at 10 m over the 45° rings' land, 2.8 at the 90th percentile and 3.9 at the 99th) are the rings whose land was kept moist, as that note's own caution says. The rings rerun on the design ground on 16 and 17 October give 1.8 to 1.9, 4.2 to 4.3 and 5.5 to 5.6 m/s, about twice the wind by day, because dry ground heats a mixed layer 13 km deep that brings the wind aloft down. The dry land's air is calm at night and breezy through the sunlit hours.
- The same note's "at most 8.1 to 9.2 m/s" for storm gusts takes its 9.2 from the southern ring's water; the land's highest are 8.1 and 8.3 m/s.
- The desert analysis's section 3.6 has the fog desert's winds passing the threshold only in their windiest percent of hours and in storm gusts. That compares the rings' wind over a 10 cm roughness with the wind that starts grains. Over sand the same air blows 1.15 to 1.27 times harder at 10 m, and moving grains keep moving down to 0.82 of the starting wind; the moist rings then exceed the threshold more than a tenth of the time and the run's own 3-day means 5.5% of it.
- The soils study weighs wind against "the storms' gusts of 5–9 m/s". Those are each storm's highest wind on the moist rings, and a land point meets one about 0.5% of the time; outside the wet tropics the steady wind of the sunlit hours moves more sand than the storms do, 26 of the central 27 m³/m/yr at 60 to 75°.
- The air over land is 1.35 kg/m³ in the run, lighter than the 1.44 and 1.40 the record uses, because the land stands high; thresholds rise 2 to 3%.
- The soils study's zones (hyper-arid 4.2% of the land at 19 mm of rain a year) are an earlier run's; this study uses the final moisture map (2.7% of the run's land at 12 mm), whose dryness, carried smoothly to the design surface, leaves 362,000 km² of it hyper-arid.
