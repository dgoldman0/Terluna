# Sky, land and people on the Open Moon

What an affluent population takes from the Open Moon's skies, land and living systems over the near horizons: the
500-year build, the first thousand years and the several thousand after. The author asked on 9 October 2026 for the
aerial biomes to come almost first ("the skies are a massive potential set of biomes in and of themselves"), and set
the population as "affluent by modern standards but norm for the population of the future", 20–30 billion across the
Solar System, most of them on Earth, the Moon and the array.

Numbers marked *screen* come from [people.py](people.py) and its product [results/people.json](results/people.json)
(schema `terluna.biosphere.ecology-people/1`); *derived* marks arithmetic on cited values; sources are in
[people_sources.json](people_sources.json). **Earth's practice** is how affluent countries feed, house, power and light
themselves today. **The lunar design** provides an affluent life by other means: a plant-rich diet with fermented
foods, sealed waste, denitrification that ends in N2, compact cities, shielded light and fusion power; its per-person
figures are design targets.

## In brief

- The sky holds 9.2×10⁸ km³ of air above freezing, 24 km deep over the ground on average, with 5–10 times Earth's
  water vapour and cosmic-ray ionization below Earth's sea level to 90 km. Lift allows flyers six times Earth's in
  every length. Phosphorus aloft is scarce, so the sky's food webs eat what the land and seas send up.
- Rain-fed land equals 2.5–5.7 million km² at the crop model's yields, 83% of it within 30° of the equator.
- Each billion people living by Earth's practice grazes 1.2 times the fog desert, crops 6–36% of the rain-fed land
  and puts a third to three-fifths of the dry land under a light-polluted sky.
- Each billion living by the lunar design crops 2.5–16% of the rain-fed land and lights 3–6% of the dry land, and
  0.035–0.084% more dimming answers its heat. These are costs per billion; how many people the Moon's settings hold,
  on the ground and aloft, comes from provisioning's comparison in the shared population cases.
- The vegetated Moon's own ethylene settles near 90–230 ppb with Earth's soil uptake, above the 50 ppb that costs
  cereals a third or more of their yield, whatever the population.

## The sky's biomes

The airspace meets the definition and functional role of a habitat (Diehl 2013), and on the Open Moon it is the
largest one.

1. **The habitat.** Air above freezing fills 9.2×10⁸ km³; the mean air reaches 0 °C at 25.7 km. Each square metre of
   ground carries 73.6 t of air, 7.1 Earth columns, and the whole air, 3.0×10¹⁸ kg, is 59% of Earth's (Trenberth &
   Smith 2005) on 7.4% of its area (*screen*). Over land the day's mixed layer averages 5.3 km and reaches 9.9 km, with
   cloud base near 4.2 km; at night it is 0.4 km deep. Storms top out at 22 km at the median (the CM1 equatorial ring).
2. **Radiation.** The shield removes the UV-B before sunlight enters the air. Cosmic-ray ionization is 2.8% of Earth's
   sea level at the ground, 3–4% through the lowest 22 km, 7% in the flight band and 16% at 45–90 km (*screen*, the
   electricity work's muon column). The July decision that high-altitude life is sparse rests now on the cold above
   26 km, the dry air and the scarce nutrients.
3. **Water.** The CM1 rings hold 130–240 mm of water vapour, 5–10 times Earth's 25 mm (Trenberth & Smith 2005), and
   turn it over in 98–158 days where it rains, against Earth's 8.9 (van der Ent & Tuinenburg 2017) (*screen*). Pollen
   stays aloft 19–45 days and spores 100–250 (first screen). Cloud fills 2–3% of the air at 5–30 km by day, and
   bacteria stay active in cloud water on Earth (Amato et al. 2019). *Flat, two-dimensional rings.*
4. **Light.** With the Sun overhead 49% of the light arrives directly at the ground, 55% at 10 km and 70–75% in the
   flight band. The Sun sets 6° lower at 10 km and 11–13° lower in the flight band, so a flyer keeps it 12 and 22–25
   hours longer at each end of the equatorial day, setting 2 and 11–18 times brighter (*screen*, clear sky, 550 nm).
   *The twilit sky aloft needs the illumination solver.*
5. **Lift.** A flyer 6.04 times larger in every length, at the same speed in air of the same density (found at
   8.9 km), carries the same stresses (the sky-ship study), and its power margin holds: wingbeats six times slower on
   216 times the muscle give the 36 times the power it needs (*derived*). Earth's largest flyers carried over reach
   60–66 m across and 44–55 t (table 2). The same animal at the ground hovers on 6.3% of Earth's power and flies a
   kilometre on 17% of the energy (*screen*). Hydrogen lifts 1.31 kg per m³ at the ground and 0.63–0.74 kg in the
   flight band, and a gas cell floats when its skin weighs under 0.44 kg/m² per metre of radius, as Sagan & Salpeter's
   "floaters" do in any dense air (1976). *Mechanics solid; physiology at these sizes is a design question.*
6. **Food.** The inherited phosphorus-fallout analogue supports 0.03–1.2 g C per m² a year in a single assimilation
   pass at Redfield C:P, at most four ten-thousandths of the land stand's 2,800–3,200 g C (*screen*). This is neither
   a measured upward lunar supply nor a ceiling on nutrient-retaining aerial producers. The
   [follow-up evaluation](../../research/studies/aerial_ecology/README.md) separates recycled production, nutrient
   stock, natural losses and harvest return. Surface-connected food webs also eat what rises from below:
   pollen, spores, nectar, insects (3.5 trillion a year cross southern Britain at 150–1,200 m; Hu et al. 2016) and the
   sea–forest migrants (the ecology register, H1–H4). Liquid water must be available and retained; water vapour alone
   is insufficient for these microbial rates. Nitrogen fixation needs energy and trace-element cofactors.
7. **Links.** Pollen in a light wind travels about 4,800 km, spores cross the Moon and plumed seeds reach 2.7 times as
   far as on Earth (first screen). Flight at a sixth of Earth's energy per kilometre lets a moth fly the 950 km round
   trip to the median sea distance on a quarter of its mass in sugar (the ecology register), so migrants carry
   phosphorus and trace elements from the seas to the forests. The seas' sulfur and iodine reach the land through the
   air, and pathogens and engineered genes travel the same routes.
8. **People in the sky.** At the summit metropolis's busy hour a flyer passes within 20 m of a point about 10 times an
   hour in the wing band (30–300 m above the ground) and 15 times in the sky-boat band (0.3–1.5 km) (*screen*, from the
   sky-fleet study). Regional ships at 22–27 km, liners at 35–45 km, winged liners at 55 km and platforms at 70 km fly
   above the storms in small numbers. The tower rises from the summit's 11.6 km through the storm layer and the cool air
   to its crown at 35.6 km, which launches upward lightning in some 100–120 episodes a year (the joint synthesis). The
   fleet's hydrogen releases, 480–7,100 t a year, barely change the air's.

## The land

9. **Kinds.** Dry land covers 61.7% of the Moon, 23.4 million km²: 16% of Earth's land, 22% of its habitable land and
   46% of its farmland (*screen*; Ritchie & Roser 2019). The wet land, raining at least 0.5 mm a day, is 35.6% of the
   Moon, the fog desert at 30–60° 16.4% and the polar dry land 9.6% (the aerosol study); rain-fed lakes add 10.3% and
   the seas 28%. Of the dry land, 54% lies on the Earthlit near side.
10. **Water.** Earth's crop sites make 1.05–2.36 g of carbon of gross production per kg of water (Tang et al. 2014),
    and the design air's CO2 pressure, 48.6 Pa against 40.5, raises that a fifth (*derived*). Rain then supports 26–58%
    of the crop model's growth within 15° of the equator, 15–34% at 15–30°, 8–18% at 30–45° and 1–3% at 45–60°
    (*screen*), so the land holds the equivalent of 2.5–5.7 million km² at the model's yields. Used whole, the rivers'
    229,410 m³/s would irrigate 1.8–4.1 million km² more. The humid days (a vapour deficit of 0.84 kPa over wet land)
    raise the efficiency further. *The plant model has no water yet.*
11. **Food land.** The day fruit at the model's potential feeds a person on 59–81 m² (*screen*, as the provisioning
    screen found); clouds, losses of a quarter to a third (FAO 2011) and a varied plant diet make that 129–342 m². Each
    diet keeps Earth's ratios to a vegan diet (Peters et al. 2016; Poore & Nemecek 2018): a plant-rich diet takes
    139–393 m² of crops and at most 1,000 m² of grazing; today's average diet 160–424 m² and 4,130 m² grazed; an
    affluent US diet 337–893 m² and 7,400 m² grazed, or 574–1,700 m² of crops with every animal fed on them (Alexander
    et al. 2016; Shepon et al. 2016) (*derived*). Grazing goes on the fog desert at Earth's grazed area a person.
    Earth's crops feed about six people a hectare (Cassidy et al. 2013).
12. **Settlement.** A person takes 25 m² at the metropolis's density, 100 m² in a compact town and 143–357 m² in the
    cities of affluent countries (Angel et al. 2010).

## Nutrients, gases and heat

13. **Nitrogen.** People in the US eat 5.2 kg N a year, and feeding them loses 22–30 kg to the environment (the
    Netherlands to the US; Leach et al. 2012), which Earth's practice must replace. The lunar design, with grains, roots
    and legumes turning 40–60% of field nitrogen into food (Leach et al. 2012) and half to four-fifths of excreted N
    returned, needs 4.5–10 kg (*screen*). From electrolytic hydrogen, at 12 kWh per kg N (IEA 2021), that takes 6–14 W
    a person by design and 31–42 W at Earth's practice. The bulk silicate Moon holds 0.019 ppm of molybdenum (Sossi et
    al. 2024), a sixtieth of Earth's upper crust (Rudnick & Gao 2003); dressings of 15–120 g/ha (Kovács et al. 2026)
    over all the dry land take 35,000–281,000 t, 0.14–1.1 years of the world's mine output (USGS 2025) (*screen*).
14. **Phosphorus.** People excrete 0.44–0.60 kg P a year, and Earth's chain loses four times what reaches food (Cordell
    et al. 2009): 1.8–2.4 kg a person, so 0.6–2.4 billion people lose as much as the whole land loses naturally,
    1.5–4.2 Tg a year (*screen*). Returning sewage, food waste and manure and holding losses to 10–30% of intake brings
    it to 0.04–0.18 kg; urine and faeces could supply 22% of Earth's phosphorus demand (Mihelcic et al. 2011).
15. **Methane and N2O.** Agriculture and waste emit 28–30 kg of methane a person a year on Earth (Saunois et al. 2020).
    With soils the only sink, each billion people at that rate add 6–28 ppm at steady state, against the wild
    biosphere's own 20 ppm (first screen); the design target, 0.3–3 kg with no flooded paddies, no landfills, sealed
    digesters and ruminants absent or inhibited, adds 0.06–2.8 ppm a billion. Earth's anthropogenic N2O, 0.7–1.0 kg N a
    person a year (Tian et al. 2020), adds 0.23–0.33 ppm per billion people every thousand years, with no sink; the
    design's fields, emitting 0.2–0.5% of their nitrogen (IPCC 2019), add 0.006–0.02 ppm. Their warming in this deep
    column is uncomputed.
16. **Ethylene.** Earth's land emits 12–30 Tg of ethylene a year naturally (Sawada & Totsuka 1986), and at that rate per
    area the Moon's dry land emits 2.7–6.7 Tg. With soils the only sink, at the uptake estimated for Earth's soils
    (Sawada et al. 1986), it settles at 94–233 ppb with a lifetime of 107 years, above the 50 ppb that costs wheat 36%
    and rice 63% of their yield (Klassen & Bugbee 2002); soils ten times faster hold it at 9–23 ppb (*screen*). The
    plants' emission and the soils' uptake are design variables of the whole biosphere.
17. **Heat.** A terawatt used on the Moon adds 0.026 W/m², and 38–61 TW would warm it 1 K unanswered (*screen*).
    Under the planned climate added dimming answers it, 0.53% a kelvin (the GCM's 1.9 K per 1%). Each billion people
    at the US's 9.3 kW a person (EIA) needs 0.08–0.13% more dimming, and at the design's 4–6 kW (2 kW of final energy
    from fusion at a third to half efficiency; needs stop rising near 60 GJ a year, Vogel et al. 2021) 0.035–0.084%.

## What each billion people takes

18. **Earth's practice.** Each billion people grazes 7.4 million km², 1.2 times the fog desert, and crops 6–36% of the
    rain-fed land, or 10–71% with every animal fed on crops. It puts 34–61% of the dry land under a light-polluted sky,
    loses phosphorus at 42–163% of the land's natural loss, and adds N2O at 55–106% and methane at 48–51% of the wild
    biosphere's sources (table 4; *screen*).
19. **The lunar design.** Each billion people crops 2.5–16% of the rain-fed land, and a tenth of a hectare of grazing a
    person takes 16% of the fog desert. It lights 3.4–6.1% of the dry land, and its phosphorus, N2O and methane come to
    0.5–12% of the land's and the wild biosphere's (*screen*). The crops' water use and the diet set most of the range.
20. **The sky before the land.** Lit as affluent countries light themselves, 7,900–14,000 m² of land a person under
    light-polluted skies (*derived*; Falchi et al. 2016; World Bank), each billion people lights more land than any
    diet crops. Cities at the metropolis's density cover 0.11% of the dry land per billion people, and their low sky
    meets a passing flyer every four to six minutes at the busy hour (*screen*). People's light reaches the aerial
    biomes' night before their crops fill the land, and their traffic reaches the sky locally.

## Living together

21. **The night.** The far side's equatorial land is the darkest, 0.001 lux at midnight, and holds the summit
    metropolis; the near side's dry land keeps 0.07–4.9 lux at the night's darkest under the Earth (the joint synthesis,
    corrected on main on 9 October). Shielded, warm and dimmed lamps keep the
    darkest zones for the biomes and far-side astronomy.
22. **Corridors.** Sea–forest migrants reach the forests at dusk for the fruit fall, past coastal cities. One lit
    installation in New York drew about 1.1 million migrating birds over seven nights in seven years, at up to 150 times
    their usual density (Van Doren et al. 2017), and buildings kill 365–988 million birds a year in the US (Loss et al.
    2014). Dark routes, little low traffic across them at dusk and towers lit for aircraft keep the conveyor working.
23. **Climates and rhythms.** The comfortable land lies poleward of 30° in both climate models, and the rain that grows
    food falls within 30° of the equator, so cities suit the cooler ground, poleward of 30° and on high ground such as the
    summit's, farms the wet tropics, and sky ships run between them. Crops ripen at sunset and forests drop their fruit at dusk, when the
    migrants come, so people's harvest, light and travel peak in the same hours.
24. **Seas and places.** Thirty per cent of the seas kept is 3.2 million km² (*derived*); the monthly tide's
    20,000–30,000 km² of flats are the most productive coast. The kept land includes the conservation programmes' sites
    and land left unassigned and unmanaged, as the conservation study's principles ask. Half kept follows Half-Earth
    (Wilson 2016; Dinerstein et al. 2019), 30% the Kunming–Montreal target (CBD 2022); on Earth 44% of the land needs
    conservation attention (Allan et al. 2022).

## Horizons

25. **The 500-year build.** Methane reaches a quarter to three-quarters of its steady level and the wild biosphere's N2O
    adds 0.16–0.21 ppm (*screen*); ethylene approaches its steady level over one to three centuries once plants cover
    the land. Natural soil forms at 0.017–0.036 mm a year (Montgomery 2007), 8.5–18 mm in the build, so soils are built
    by organic loading, fines and pioneer crusts.
26. **The first thousand years.** From 0.1 billion at the build's end, growth of 0.5–1% a year reaches 1 billion in
    230–460 years and 10 billion in 460–920 (*derived*), so the costs per billion above arrive within this horizon.
27. **Several thousand years.** The wild biosphere's N2O adds 1.6–2.1 ppm in 5,000 years and 10 billion people at
    Earth's practice 12–17 ppm more; the land's phosphorus runs down without returns, and the seas turn rich in
    magnesium over 18,000–180,000 years (the ecology register, G2).

## Open questions, in order of what they settle

1. **Water in the crops.** Stomata and a soil water bucket in the plant model, with the lunar air's CO2 and humidity,
   set the rain-fed food land within a factor of two, and with it the lunar design's capacity.
2. **Ethylene and the living air.** The plants' emission, the soils' uptake and any sink from NOx chemistry
   (questions.md, 1) decide whether cereals grow at all.
3. **The gases' warming.** Methane at tens of ppm and N2O at ppm in a column 7.6 times Earth's (questions.md, 3) decide
   whether the gases bind before land, and how strict the design targets must be.
4. **The night's requirement and a city's skyglow** in this tall, scattering air (the illumination solver), with
   thresholds by function for the sky's and the land's animals: whether light binds before land.
5. **What lives aloft.** Water from cloud and humid air, phosphorus kept within floating and flying organisms,
   aerophytes' gas, life cycles timed to the lunar day, and how many aerial animals the land's insects, pollen and fruit
   can feed.
6. **The affluent diet.** Its animal share, grazing on the fog desert at its own productivity, and fermented and
   microbial foods (Leger et al. 2021).
7. **Sharing the sky.** Corridors, quiet hours and lighting rules for traffic over migration routes, and the tower's
   reach from the storm layer to the flight band.
8. **Soils and the return limb.** How fast built soils form, and the phosphorus, potassium and molybdenum returns.
9. **People's ecology in each setting.** What a billion people take from the land, sky and wild biosphere in each
   way of living, on the ground and aloft, for provisioning's comparison in the shared population cases, and its
   path through the first thousand years.
