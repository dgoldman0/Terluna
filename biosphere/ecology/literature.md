# What the literature says for the Open Moon's ecology

A cross-field reading of 9 October 2026, by topic, with what each finding means here. Full citations, DOIs and how far
each source was read are in [sources.json](sources.json); the search's complete register (136 sources) is kept with
the domain's run records on the research drive (`research/runs/ecology/literature_register.md`).

## Ecosystems on fresh basalt

- **Nitrogen first, phosphorus later.** On Hawaii's basalt the young soils are short of nitrogen and the old ones
  of phosphorus. After 4 million years the forests are fed by sea-spray cations and by phosphorus in dust from Asia,
  6,000 km away (Chadwick et al. 1999). The Moon has no continental dust, so its old soils' phosphorus is a designed
  return.
- **Soil forms slowly.** Natural soil forms at 0.017–0.036 mm a year (Montgomery 2007). Lasting humus builds at
  0.7% of plant production (Schlesinger 1990). Fine-material delivery and organic loading make soil fast; weathering
  in place does not.
- **Pioneers and lock-in.** The first colonists of fresh Icelandic basalt were nitrogen fixers and chemolithotrophs
  (Kelly et al. 2014). Biological crusts fix about 49 Tg N a year, nearly half of Earth's land fixation (Elbert et al.
  2012). A moss mat arrested Hekla's succession for 170–700 years (Vilmundardóttir et al. 2018). Priority effects
  apply in full on a world assembled from introductions, so the order of introduction shapes each region's
  outcome.
- **Biological weathering.** Cyanobacteria release basalt's Ca, Mg, Si and K over five times faster than abiotic
  leaching, and work nine times better on basalt than on rhyolite (Olsson-Francis et al. 2012). Microbial leaching of
  basalt was the same on the ISS at microgravity, Mars and Earth gravity (Cockell et al. 2020).
- **Apollo soil.** Plants grow in Apollo soils, slowly and with salt, metal and oxidative stress, worst in mature soil
  rich in agglutinate and nanophase iron (Paul et al. 2022). A worked, hydrated surface should be far milder; ionic
  and oxidative tolerance is the axis to engineer.

## Nitrogen

- **Molybdenum limits fixation.** Molybdenum alone limits free-living fixation in weathered tropical soils (Barron et
  al. 2009), and limitation is widespread, with the vanadium and iron-only nitrogenases contributing. The bulk
  silicate Moon carries about 0.019 ppm molybdenum (Sossi et al. 2024).
- **Nitrogen partial pressure is ample.** Free-living fixers grow down to 5 mbar of N2 (Klingler et al. 1989); the
  Moon's air holds about 990 mbar.
- **Lightning.** Earth's lightning fixes 5 ± 3 Tg N a year (Schumann & Huntrieser 2007). Scaled to the Moon's
  1/350 flash rate, that is 0.02–0.4% of a biome's fixation, consistent with the joint synthesis's at most 0.2%.

## Trace gases without OH

- **Methane.** Soils take up 30 (11–49) Tg of methane a year, against 553 Tg removed by the air's chemistry (Saunois
  et al. 2020). Freshwaters alone emit at least 103 Tg a year, offsetting a quarter of the land's carbon sink
  (Bastviken et al. 2011). With soils as the only sink, lake depth, wetland extent and anoxic volume become variables
  of the air.
- **Hydrogen is easy.** Soil bacteria from 51 phyla carry high-affinity hydrogenases and remove about three-quarters
  of Earth's atmospheric H2, about 60 Tg a year (Greening et al. 2016; Grinter et al. 2023; Ehhalt & Rohrer 2009).
- **Carbon monoxide.** Soils take 10–25% of the global flux, and warm soils turn from net uptake to net release above
  about 30 °C (King 1999), which could happen late in a lunar afternoon.
- **N2O.** Its 116-year lifetime on Earth comes from stratospheric photolysis and excited oxygen (Prather et al.
  2015), and soils take up at most 0.3 Tg N a year (Schlesinger 2013). Natural soils and oceans emit about 5.6 and
  3.4 Tg N a year (Tian et al. 2020).
- **Ethylene.** Closed crop stands reach 40–120 ppb (Wheeler et al. 1996). Fifty ppb held continuously costs wheat
  36% and rice 63% of yield, and 1,000 ppb sterilizes; one wheat cultivar was unaffected at 50 ppb (Klassen & Bugbee
  2002).
- **Waste design.** MELiSSA suppresses methanogenesis in its waste loop by design, which suits a world where methane
  lives for centuries.

## Light

- **Vitamin D.** The previtamin D3 action spectrum peaks at 295–300 nm and ends near 315 nm (MacLaughlin et al.
  1982; CIE 174:2006); the in-vivo spectrum lies 5 nm shorter (Young et al. 2021). Cats and dogs are obligate
  dietary-vitamin-D mammals; poultry gain from UV-A; basking reptiles depend most on UV-B.
- **Bee and bird vision.** The hymenopteran UV receptor peaks at 340–344 nm (Peitsch et al. 1992). Floral UV
  patterning follows the UV a habitat receives (Koski & Ashman 2015). Insects orient to polarized moonlight (Dacke et
  al. 2003).
- **Plant UV signalling.** UVR8 perceives UV-B and UV-A up to 350 nm (Rai et al. 2020). Crops under UV-blocking
  films grow as well or better, with 15–35% less flavonoid and anthocyanin and fewer pests (Katsoulas et al. 2020).
- **Disinfection.** Solar disinfection of water needs more than 108 kJ m⁻² at 295–385 nm (Ubomba-Jaswa et al. 2009).
- **Photodegradation.** Cutting total radiation cut dry-land litter decomposition by 60% (Austin & Vivanco 2006). It
  acts through lignin across the UV and visible (Austin & Ballaré 2010).
- **Light at night.** Full moonlight on Earth is 0.05–0.3 lux (Kyba et al. 2017). Melatonin in fishes and rodents is
  suppressed at 0.01–0.03 lux (Grubisic et al. 2019), and biological effects of light at night appear across taxa
  (Sanders et al. 2021).
- **Lunar cues.** Coral spawning keys on the dark interval between sunset and moonrise (Lin et al. 2021). Zooplankton
  sink en masse each month in the Arctic (Last et al. 2016).

## The long day and night

- **Continuous light.** Tomato's continuous-light injury comes from circadian asynchrony (Velez-Ramirez et al. 2017),
  and one locus gives tolerance with up to 20% more yield (Velez-Ramirez et al. 2014).
- **Starch timing.** Plants time starch to last until the expected dawn and run out about 24 hours after the last one
  (Graf et al. 2010).
- **Evergreen through long winters.** Shedding a canopy costs more carbon than evergreen respiration through polar
  winters (Royer et al. 2003).
- **Polar night is busy.** At 79° N most trophic levels stay active, with diversity and juveniles higher in some
  habitats in winter (Berge et al. 2015). Algae endure months to years of darkness (McMinn & Martin 2013).

## Gravity and the dense air

- **Plant gravity sensing.** The threshold lies at 0.1–0.3 g (Kiss 2014). Simulated Moon gravity unbalanced root
  meristem growth and proliferation more than simulated weightlessness (Manzano et al. 2018).
- **Animals.** Lunar gravity prevented muscle atrophy in mice but not the fibre-type shift (Hayashi et al. 2023).
- **Tree height.** The gravity term of the water-column limit scales as 1/g (Koch et al. 2004).
- **Flight.** Hummingbirds hold power reserves to 133 W/kg (Chai & Dudley 1995).
- **Dispersal.** Long-distance seed dispersal runs on uplift above the canopy (Nathan et al. 2002), and transgenic
  pollen moved genes 21 km (Watrud et al. 2004).
- **Containment.** Synthetic auxotrophy resists escape by mutation and gene transfer (Mandell et al. 2015; Rovner et
  al. 2015).

## Fire

- **Oxygen thresholds at Earth gravity.** Fire is greatly suppressed below 18.5% O2 and off below 16% (Belcher et
  al. 2010), measured near 1 atm.
- **Fraction or partial pressure.** NASA's analysis leaves open whether flammability follows the oxygen fraction or
  its partial pressure (Harper et al. 2016).
- **Lunar gravity.** It lowers materials' oxygen limit by 2–6 points (Ferkul & Olson 2011, tabled in the port fire
  study).
- **Fire-kept biomes.** Grassy biomes kept open by fire hold over 70% of Earth's burnt area, in climates wet enough
  for forest (Bond & Zaloumis 2016).

## New waters

- **New reservoirs run eutrophic.** One ran 3.6 times its phosphorus, 17 times its phytoplankton and 33 times its
  zooplankton for 6–14 years (Paterson et al. 2019).
- **Anoxia** follows how long the water stays stratified (Jane et al. 2023).
- **Assembled systems.** Biosphere 2's biome fragments interacted in ways their source biomes did not predict (Cohen
  & Tilman 1996, abstract unverified). Novel host–pathogen pairings form readily, governed by taxonomic breadth
  (Bufford et al. 2016).

## Gaps the search found

No study exists of:
- any organism under a light–dark cycle near 708 hours;
- flammability at 17.5% oxygen and 1.2 atm;
- melatonin or activity thresholds under 2–3 lux held all night;
- vertebrate vitamin D under a hard cut-off near 320 nm;
- partial-gravity plants beyond seedling meristems.

Each is a candidate for original work here.

## Naming and classification

- **The codes name real organisms.** The ICN (Madrid Code, 2025), ICZN (1999), ICNP (2022 revision) and SeqCode (2026)
  tie names to types: preserved specimens, living type strains or deposited genomes. The ICZN excludes names for
  hypothetical concepts (Art. 1.3.1), so names for the Open Moon's designs are proposals in the codes' forms.
- **Engineered organisms keep their species.** JCVI-syn1.0 and JCVI-syn3.0 are strains of *Mycoplasma mycoides*
  (Gibson et al. 2010; Hutchison et al. 2016). Sc2.0 is a redesigned *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* with version-controlled
  edits (Richardson et al. 2017). A crop's insertion is an event with a unique identifier, as maize MON810 carries
  MON-ØØ81Ø-6.
- **Genome types and registration.** The SeqCode types prokaryotes by genome sequences and publishes a name only when
  it is registered; the ICN rejected sequence types at Madrid and sent them to a committee for 2029 (Turland 2025).
- **Clades by definition.** Under the PhyloCode a clade is an ancestor and all its descendants, and a name can be
  defined from a directly specified ancestor (Note 9.5.1), which suits registered founders.
- **Life forms and sizes.**
  - Raunkiær (1934) classed plants by where their surviving buds sit through the unfavourable season, and
    phanerophytes by height.
  - Plankton is classed by decades of size (Sieburth et al. 1978).
  - Aeroplankton names wind-carried organisms, small animals included (Ptatscheck et al. 2018).
- **Names from every language.** The ICZN allows roots from any language (Art. 11.3); the ICN can reject new names
  derogatory to a group of people from 2026 (Art. 51.2).

## Floating giants

- **Wood loses strength under sustained load.** Clear Douglas fir carries about 62% of its five-minute strength for ten
  years and 58% for a century (Wood 1951, the Madison curve), so a cellulose skin or tendon is sized for the load it
  holds between renewals.
- **Tendon collagen creeps.** Wallaby tail tendons rupture under sustained stress of 10 MPa and above (Wang & Ker
  1995), which holds collagen-tendoned round floaters to 100–200 m.
- **Fish secrete gas against great pressure.** Deep-sea fish fill their swim bladders with oxygen at over 100
  atmospheres through a counter-current rete 7–13 mm long (Scholander & van Dam 1954; Scholander 1954), driven by the
  gas gland's acid output and the Root effect (Pelster 2015).
- **Guanine plates seal the bladder.** Removing the silvery guanine layer raises the wall's gas permeability about a
  hundredfold (Denton, Liddicoat & Taylor 1972); the plates are under 20 nm thick and up to 50 µm wide (Pinsk et al.
  2022).
- **A colonial floater makes its own float gas.** The Portuguese man-of-war's gas gland makes carbon monoxide from
  serine, 0.5–13% of its float gas (Wittenberg 1960), and siphonophore colonies divide their work among zooids budded
  in growth zones (Dunn & Wagner 2006).
- **Floaters already sail.** The man-of-war sails up to 50–55° off downwind with its tentacles and polyps as a sea
  anchor (Iosilevskii & Weihs 2009), and a wing on a long tether can steer a stratospheric balloon across the wind
  (Aaron, Heun & Nock 2002).
- **Animals drink vapour.** A desert cockroach takes water from air above 82.5% relative humidity (O'Donnell 1977),
  and tick larvae hold their weight down to about 80–85% (Knülle 1966).
- **Mistletoes draw water and minerals from their hosts.** Xylem-tapping hemiparasites take water and nutrients
  through the haustorium and photosynthesise (Glatzel & Geils 2009), the route for a canopy nursery.
- **Hydrogen's flammable range.** Hydrogen burns in air from 4.0% to 75%, and no hydrogen–nitrogen–air mixture burns
  below about 5% oxygen (Coward & Jones 1952; Zabetakis 1965), so in the lunar air the upper limit is about 71%.
