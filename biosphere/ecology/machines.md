# Machines in the living world

The author set the wild under heavy automation: extensively monitored, constantly evaluated and maintained, with the
machines part of the whole world's technobiosphere
([register](../../research/decisions.md#machines-in-the-living-world-12-october-2026)). This brief sets out what the
machines do and the estimates behind the author's choices. Estimates are *derived* from the project's products;
published work is cited as published and was not re-read in full for this brief.

## What the machines do

- **Sensing.** On the ground: sound recorders, cameras, DNA samplers for air and water, soil and gas-flux stations,
  seismometers and sea buoys. Aloft, a mesh of aerostats drifting near 30 km carries radar for flyers, aerophytes and
  towns, cameras resolving about 16 cm, thermal infrared for the long night and samplers for pollen and spores
  ([provisioning's network brief](../../provisioning/network.md)).
- **Evaluation.** A world twin on the array's computing, coarse everywhere with fine nests where something is
  happening. Provisioning's computing study finds that a metre-scale weather twin of the whole Moon would fill the
  array, while control takes megawatts.
- **Hands.** Upkeep of the parks; interventions in the wild where the evaluation calls for them; and a watch on what
  wind and flyers carry between regions, which stay distinct only loosely.
- **Uptime.** The whole system is engineered for extreme uptime (provisioning register, 12 October).

## How the machines join the living world

The author chose all of these:
1. **Machines that behave.** Robots accepted into cockroach groups steer their collective choices (Halloy et al.
   2007). Robots have carried signals between groups of bees and fish (Bonnet et al. 2019). A robotic falcon drove off
   flocks of corvids, gulls, starlings and lapwings better than a drone or conventional methods, with no sign of
   habituation during the study (Storms et al. 2022). Virtual-fence collars herd cattle with sound cues.
2. **A shared signal vocabulary.** The organisms are engineered, so they can carry innate responses to a few signals:
   keep out, gather, disperse, danger above. The signals sit mostly outside human senses, in channels such as
   ultrasound, infrared and scents too faint for people, so that their use comes first. Three design points:
   - an unbacked signal fades by habituation, as scarecrows do, so each needs a real consequence behind it;
   - selection wears away a costly response that never pays, so the signals should pay the animal;
   - a biological code cannot be locked, so anyone with an emitter could herd animals; regional dialects, and signals
     that work only with a machine's behaviour, answer that.
3. **Organisms that report.** Spinach fitted with nanosensors reported nitroaromatic explosives to an infrared camera
   (Wong et al. 2017); engineered organisms can carry such reporting in their own make-up. A DNA watermark, as in the
   first synthetic genome (Gibson et al. 2010), lets DNA sampled from air and water tell which lineage passed and
   where it came from.
4. **Machines in ecological roles.** Carrying minerals back uphill beside the sea-to-forest migrants, where the Moon
   lacks the rock cycle's return; controlled disturbance, such as grazing patterns and clearings, wherever the fire
   question ([questions](questions.md), item 12) calls for fire or grazing; and shepherds in the sky. The design winds
   gather drifting towns and aerophytes onto shared routes, within weeks near the ground and within years at 40–60 km
   (provisioning's zonal winds note), and on those tracks towns give way to sky reefs (provisioning register).
5. **Machines that compost when lost.** Electronics can be made to dissolve (Hwang et al. 2012), and sensors can be
   sown by the wind like seeds (Kim et al. 2021; Iyer et al. 2022).
6. **Handling.** Machines handle animals and steer them. They may kill animals, to cull a population, end suffering or
   protect people, under sustainable practices and the ethical standards already considered for conservation, with
   people overseeing the system constantly.

## What may live wild

Pollen stays aloft for 19–45 days and spores for 100–250 ([README](README.md), finding 10), so whatever lives wild can
cross the Moon within weeks. The author set no containment: the biomes are shaped by climate and the parks by
intensive manicuring. Wild designs carry no hard limits, and regions stay distinct only loosely. The starting
constraints, adopted by the author:
- **Climate fit:** each design does best where it is placed and has no advantage everywhere, which keeps regions
  loosely distinct.
- **Harmless Moon-wide:** no traits harmful at the Moon's scale, such as pathogens of other organisms or of people, or
  strong allergens in pollen that stays aloft for weeks.
- **Within the air's sinks:** each design's metabolism keeps its N2O, methane and ethylene within what the air's sinks
  remove.
- **Closed cycles:** designs return what they take, phosphorus above all.
- **Ready for the machines:** each carries the shared signals, reporting traits and a DNA watermark.
- **Robust to evolution:** no safety feature rests on a genetic circuit that evolution can switch off.

## N2O and methane

The author chose biological means coupled with advanced technologies to make up the difference, and set aside a
window in the shield near 200–240 nm, which would make ozone and heat the upper air.
- **Sources and pace.** Earth-like soils and seas would emit 1.0–1.3 Tg N a year, raising N2O by 0.3–0.4 ppb a year
  (finding 7); farms add 0.05–0.17 ppb a year at 8 billion under the power-heavy mix (provisioning's food study). N2O
  reaches Earth's present 0.34 ppm in about a thousand years and 3–4 ppm in ten thousand.
- **What leaves it alone.** Nitrogen fixation draws N2 from the air and feeds the soils the nitrogen that N2O comes
  from. N2O breaks only under light below about 240 nm, so the local UV-B for vitamin D leaves it alone. Lamps below
  240 nm in open air would lose nearly every photon to oxygen and water, so breaking down the N2O that way would take
  hundreds of terawatts (*derived*); they would also harm people, animals and plants and make ozone.
- **Biology.** Complete denitrification, with the N2O reductase NosZ in the soils' microbes, is a requirement
  (finding 7). On Earth one N2O-respiring bacterium, grown in biogas digestate spread on fields, cut N2O emissions by
  50–95% depending on the soil (Hiis et al. 2024). Soils take up at most 2% of Earth's N2O sources (Schlesinger
  2013), so a soil sink would have to far outdo nature. The seas could take up about as much as the sources release,
  at around a part per million, if their surface microbes consumed N2O as fast as it dissolved (*derived*); Earth's
  N2O consumers need water low in oxygen, so that is a design question.
- **Technology.** Treatment works best where the gases are concentrated: waste and wastewater, manure, sealed farms
  and food plants, and industry, where nitric-acid plants already destroy most of their N2O with catalysts. Removal
  from open air at parts per million comes last.
- **Methane** has soils as its only sink too (finding 25), and the heritage cattle the author allowed on the
  homesteads bring back the ruminants the lunar design left out (provisioning register).
- **Their warming:** Earth's present levels force 4.7 W/m² all-sky in the Moon's deep column and the central steady
  state 7.9–8.3 W/m², with N2O the larger lever per ppm ([questions](questions.md), item 3).
