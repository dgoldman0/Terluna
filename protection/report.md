# Lunar Atmospheric Protection: Design and Feasibility

## 1. Assessment

**A lunar protection system has a plausible route within the industrial capabilities required to construct a 1.2-atmosphere Moon. A complete habitable, billion-year atmospheric solution has not yet been established.** The useful distinction is between component designs whose material, force and power requirements can be calculated, and the coupled atmospheric and plasma behavior that determines whether those components achieve the environmental objective.

The reference architecture retains a solar-filter complex with an industrial hub, lunar magnetic protection, and an expanding network of protected destinations and corridors. It places the optical aperture approximately 78,000 km sunward of the Moon and holds it there with continuous propulsion. Regional magnetic installations on the Moon provide a calculable initial field; free-flying nodes extend protection where traffic and habitation justify them. These are proposed engineering developments of the original architecture, rather than recovered specifications.[1]

| Filter positioning | 6.11 × 10^12 kg including power equipment and fuel buffer; 179 TW mean electrical power | Force and propulsion mass feedback close under stated assumptions; plume isolation remains a critical condition |
| Propellant supply | 278,000 kg/s; 8.79 × 10^21 kg per billion years | Small flow relative to initial terraforming, substantial cumulative resource commitment |
| Lunar magnetic stations | Four regional 100 km radius installations; 2.14 × 10^14 kg ideal conductor and support estimate | Planning allowance of roughly 3 × 10^14–3 × 10^15 kg; enormous but below atmospheric inventory |
| Near-space corridors | Traffic-dependent large magnetic nodes plus shielded habitats | Incremental deployment is reasonable; dose reduction and orbital maintenance need explicit designs |
| Broad cislunar radiation protection | Depends on geometry, particle rigidity and current paths | A separate major program; cannot be inferred from solar-wind shielding performance |

The proposed optical stack transmits approximately **97.8% of visible sunlight** and **96.3% within 400–2,500 nm**, weighted with a measured solar reference spectrum. Its total solar transmission is lower: approximately **88–91% before support and power-collector obscuration**, within the model's spectral bounds. This difference directly affects the lower-atmosphere climate design.

The lunar core is therefore a defensible engineering research program. The present calculations support conditional compatibility with the initial terraform's resource and power envelope. They do not support a promise of construction before 2526, or a claim that setting the exobase to 250 K alone guarantees a billion-year lifetime.

## 2. Requirements and comparison baseline

The environmental objective is an open lunar atmosphere at **121,590 Pa**, with a habitable lower atmosphere and a cold, weakly escaping upper atmosphere. The reference dry mixture contains 82.5% nitrogen and 17.5% oxygen by number, giving an oxygen partial pressure of 0.21 atm. Surface temperature is provisionally 288 K. The preferred exobase temperature is approximately 250 K; 260 K is a candidate limit to investigate, without an established universal retention threshold.

The previous spherical atmospheric calculation supplies the comparison inventory: **3.14 × 10^18 kg total**, including **2.53 × 10^18 kg nitrogen** and **6.13 × 10^17 kg oxygen**. Building that inventory in 500 years requires an average addition of **1.99 × 10^8 kg/s**. These are project-model outputs, not a measured lunar atmosphere. The same analysis produced representative nitrogen transport electrical requirements of **33–235 PW**, depending on propulsion and transfer assumptions. That range excludes some source-development, industrial and thermal costs, and should not be treated as a complete project energy estimate.[2]

At this pressure and temperature, the reference density is approximately 1.46 kg/m³. Low gravity reduces aerodynamic flight speed and structural weight; balloon mass lift still follows displaced-gas density because gravity acts on both the balloon and displaced air. The shielding design retains this aerial-environment objective. It does not reduce the pressure to simplify retention.

The reference molecular exobase is at 2.81 lunar radii. The screen is provisionally sized to protect a radius of **three lunar radii**, or 5,212 km from the Moon's center. That is a design assumption requiring atmospheric confirmation. A hotter or more extended atmosphere, or a high-altitude atomic population important to loss, may require a larger optical footprint.

The principal acceptance variable is **total species-resolved escape and replenishment**, averaged across solar cycles and rare disturbances. Thermal escape, photochemical escape, ion outflow, sputtering and water loss must be treated together, with consistent energy accounting.[3]

| Long-term average loss | Inventory consumed in 1 billion years | Meaning for this project |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 kg/s | 3.16 × 10^16 kg; about 1% of initial atmosphere | Strong intrinsic retention |
| 10 kg/s | 3.16 × 10^17 kg; about 10% | Useful initial retention target |
| 100 kg/s | 3.16 × 10^18 kg; about one atmosphere | Continuous replacement becomes necessary |
| 1,000 kg/s | 3.16 × 10^19 kg; about ten atmospheres | Still only 0.0005% of initial construction flow, but composition and supply matter |

These are inventory calculations. A tolerable replenishment rate does not by itself establish a cold exobase, stable climate, or acceptable water loss.

## 3. System architecture and hub functions

The optical and charged-particle subsystems have different physical jobs. EUV and X-ray photons require absorption, reflection or diffraction. Magnetic fields act on charged particles. Plasma can mediate a solar-wind obstacle, but a low-density magnetic bubble cannot be credited with arbitrary EUV rejection.

The solar-filter complex should contain a lightweight optical swarm, power and propulsion modules, metrology, replacement stores, and servicing vehicles. Its associated industrial hub handles fabrication, propellant conditioning, freight routing and repairs. Heavy inhabited and industrial facilities should occupy economical trajectories and service the moving aperture, rather than adding their entire mass to the continuously propelled optical structure. The original Earth–Sun L1/L2 hubs remain useful transport and power infrastructure.[1]

| Location or asset | Primary function | Required connection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lunar-sunward optical aperture | Suppress direct solar heating and dissociation bands | Continuous Sun–Moon alignment, power, fuel, repair and spectral monitoring |
| Lunar orbital depots | Local logistics and emergency replacement | Regular cargo transfers and optical-swarm servicing |
| Earth–Sun L1/L2 hubs | Interplanetary freight, industry, power and outer-system links | Scheduled transport; they need not geometrically coincide with the lunar aperture |
| Regional lunar magnetic installations | Supply the initial large-scale magnetic moment | Anchors, cryogenic systems, power, quench management and plasma diagnostics |
| Free-flying magnetic/plasma nodes | Shape selected solar-wind interactions and protect traffic destinations | Explicit orbits, force balance, power, material replacement and radiation transport models |

Protection should expand around useful destinations and trajectories. The initial milestone is the lunar atmosphere and occupied surface. Later milestones are orbital settlements, transfer routes and broader cislunar coverage. Each extension needs a stated particle spectrum and dose objective, rather than an undifferentiated label of radiation protection.

## 4. A concrete optical material candidate

A useful first coupon is a **10 µm fused-silica film with a 1 µm titania absorbing layer and five antireflection layers**. Silica and titania are accessible material families, with measured optical constants. The calculation uses Siefke and colleagues' author-supplied titania data, Malitson's silica dispersion and the CXRO atomic scattering-factor tables for the higher-energy range.[4][5][6] The numerical titania input is preserved from the refractiveindex.info database.[20]

The titania measurements came from a particular deposited film. Applying those optical constants to another thickness, deposition process or mixed coating is an extrapolation to test experimentally. The porous and mixed layers below use an effective-medium model; they are proposed fabrication targets.

| Order from Sun toward Moon | Material model | Thickness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 50% silica / 50% void effective medium | 0.136 µm |
| 2 | Silica | 0.110 µm |
| 3 | 50% silica / 50% titania effective medium | 0.081 µm |
| 4 | Titania absorber | 1.000 µm |
| 5 | 50% silica / 50% titania buried matching layer | 0.080 µm |
| 6 | Silica substrate | 10.000 µm |
| 7 | 50% silica / 50% void rear matching layer | 0.126 µm |

A complex-index Fresnel calculation at normal incidence gives 96.4% transmission averaged over a 5,772 K Planck spectrum within 400–2,500 nm. Reweighting with TSIS-1 HSRS gives **96.3% in that band and 97.8% from 400–700 nm**. The uncoated two-material stack gives only about 77.2% under the Planck weighting, so interface engineering is consequential.[7]

The main material mass is about 26 g/m² before thin matching layers. The engineering reference allocates **50 g/m² to film, coatings, local framing and metrology**, excluding the separately modeled power/propulsion plant and fuel buffer. This allowance is a target, not a completed structural bill of materials. A 250 g/m² sensitivity case remains within the earlier terraform's power range.

The thin film must be divided into replaceable, locally supported cells. A single mechanically continuous sheet thousands of kilometers across is unnecessary. Local tension, thermal expansion, electrostatic charging, material fatigue, optical degradation and micrometeoroid repair determine whether the areal-mass target is achievable.

## 5. Spectral performance and lower-atmosphere climate

The tabulated UV results use Beer–Lambert absorption through the titania layer. Extremely small calculated transmission indicates that defects and coverage dominate practical performance; it does not establish an equivalent whole-system rejection factor.

| Wavelength or photon energy | Calculated intact-material transmission | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 121.6 nm Lyman-alpha | Far below 10^-10 through 1 µm titania | Strong direct-beam rejection is materially plausible |
| 150–250 nm | Far below 10^-10 at tabulated wavelengths | Strong rejection includes oxygen-dissociating UV bands |
| 300 nm | Approximately 6 × 10^-17 through titania | This design also removes much near-UV |
| 350 nm | Approximately 0.076 through titania | UVA and total sunlight are modified |
| 0.5 keV | 1.9 × 10^-8 through silica plus titania | Strong soft-X-ray attenuation |
| 1 keV | 1.1 × 10^-4 | Meaningful residual requiring spectral weighting |
| 2 keV | 0.018 | Incomplete attenuation |
| 5 keV / 10 keV | 0.62 / 0.94 | Thin-film protection is weak at these energies |

The calculation does **not** yet provide measured compound optical constants across the entire 25–120 nm gap between the two modeled spectral ranges. Silica's published behavior supports strong short-wavelength absorption, but a flight design requires direct broadband measurements of the actual multilayer, including that gap and absorption-edge structure.[8] The CXRO calculation is an independent-atom absorption estimate, not a complete multilayer scattering calculation at every X-ray edge.

Increasing the silica thickness to 100 µm reduces modeled 5 keV transmission to about 0.041, while 10 keV transmission remains approximately 0.66. This heavier variant helps with some flare photons but does not become a general hard-radiation shield. Its mass and continuous propulsion requirements increase accordingly. A measured flare spectrum must decide whether permanent thickness, separate supplementary filters, or episodic tolerable loss is the best choice.

Integrating the calculated stack over TSIS wavelengths 210–2,730 nm admits approximately **1,204 W/m²**. Allowing the unmodeled remainder of a 1,361 W/m² total to transmit anywhere from zero to unity bounds total transmission at roughly **88.4–91.0%**. These bounds concern the optical model, with coarse solar sampling and neglected silica absorption in that interval; they are not certification tolerances. Power collectors and opaque supports reduce whole-aperture transmission further.

At 90% transmission and albedo 0.30, absorbed sunlight averaged over the lunar sphere is about 214 W/m², compared with 238 W/m² for the same albedo without the filter. The corresponding effective radiating temperature is about 248 K. A 288 K surface would require approximately 40 K of greenhouse warming in this illustrative balance. This is not an obvious energy contradiction, but the actual greenhouse, clouds, water circulation and 29.53-day solar cycle must be solved together.

Removing UV also changes ozone production, radical chemistry and atmospheric oxidizing capacity. The coating thickness and band edges should therefore be optimized against the atmospheric model. Maximum UV rejection and maximum visible transmission alone are insufficient objectives.

## 6. Optical geometry and positioning

The Sun has angular radius approximately 0.00465 radians at the Moon. A filter a distance d sunward of a protected region of radius R must have radius approximately **R + 0.00465d** to cover the full solar disk throughout that region. At d = 78,000 km and R = 5,212 km, the resulting aperture diameter is **11,150 km**, with area **9.76 × 10^13 m²**.

The screen must follow the Moon while remaining sunward. There is no stationary lunar L1 point that continuously maintains this Sun-facing geometry through the lunar month. The proposed trajectory is an actively held formation. The force calculation evaluates Earth and Moon gravity, differential solar gravity and the annual rotation of the Sun direction over lunar phase; it is an instantaneous phase sweep, not a full ephemeris propagation.

A sweep of distances from 20,000 to 180,000 km finds the lowest reference propellant flow near **78,000 km**. The monthly mean holding acceleration is 0.946 mm/s² and the maximum is 1.356 mm/s². A conservative 0.020 mm/s² solar-pressure allowance and a 25% peak-force margin give a design maximum of **1.720 mm/s²**. Sampling lunar distances of 363,300 and 405,500 km changes mean acceleration by roughly +14% and −9%; operational averages will consequently differ from the circular reference.

| Protected atmospheric radius | Required screen diameter | Mean power at reference areal mass and propulsion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 lunar radii | 7,675 km | 85 TW; too small for the existing reference atmosphere |
| 3 lunar radii | 11,150 km | 179 TW |
| 4 lunar radii | 14,625 km | 308 TW |
| 5 lunar radii | 18,099 km | 472 TW |

The 78,000 km screen is outside the approximate lunar Hill radius. This is acceptable for a continuously propelled formation, but it cannot be described as a passive lunar satellite. A complete trajectory design must include lunar inclination and eccentricity, Earth's shadow, perturbations across the aperture, collisions, cargo arrival momentum and failure dispersal.

Earth-shadow passages also require an energy or trajectory strategy. The nominal reference includes no electrical storage reserve. Adding storage sufficient for two hours at installed peak thrust power, at an assumed usable 1 MJ/kg, increases total held mass to **1.15 × 10^13 kg**, mean propulsion power to **338 TW**, and propellant flow to **525,000 kg/s**. This sensitivity includes the storage mass feeding back into thrust. It does not certify a particular eclipse profile; controlled coasting, power sharing or external power could reduce storage requirements. A fuel buffer alone cannot supply electrical power.

The optical footprint also intersects Earth's solar rays during some conjunction geometries. Both the compact screen and a broad L1 aperture therefore require an Earth–Moon illumination assessment. This coupling cannot be removed merely by labeling the screen lunar infrastructure.

## 7. Propulsion, power and mass closure

The reference propulsion specification is 30 km/s effective exhaust velocity, 70% electrical-to-jet efficiency, and paired exhaust directions canted 45 degrees from the required resultant axis. The cant allows room to route plumes away from the Moon and incurs a factor of 1/cos(45°) in total thrust and propellant. It is a power allowance for plume routing, not proof of plume isolation.

The complete electrical supply, processing, thruster and thermal package is assigned **300 W/kg at installed peak power**. That is an advanced system requirement, not a demonstrated current integrated spacecraft capability. High-power electric thrusters exist experimentally; the scale, lifetime and mass of the proposed distributed system greatly exceed those demonstrations.[9]

Power-system mass increases the thrust needed to hold the system, so it must be solved simultaneously. With base mass M0, mean acceleration a, design peak acceleration a_peak, exhaust speed v, efficiency eta, specific power kappa, fuel-buffer duration t and cant angle beta:

**M = M0 / [1 − a_peak v / (2 eta kappa cos beta) − at / (v cos beta)].**

If the denominator is zero or negative, the assumed design cannot hold its own power plant and fuel. With a seven-day fuel buffer, the reference requires kappa greater than approximately **54 W/kg** to close mathematically. Operation close to that limit is very mass-expensive.

At 600 K and emissivity 0.9, rejecting 30% of peak electrical input requires approximately 1.44 × 10^10 m² of effective emitting area. This is an illustrative thruster-loss radiator budget; photovoltaic conversion heat, wiring loss and cold equipment need their own temperature-compatible paths. The specific-power allocation must include all those burdens without double-counting the external fuel-production plant.

At 100 W/kg, the 50 g/m² design grows to 1.08 × 10^13 kg and 317 TW mean power. At 250 g/m² and 100 W/kg, it needs 1.58 PW and 2.46 million kg/s of propellant. Those values remain below the earlier 33–235 PW transport examples, although the least demanding original transport case leaves considerably less margin. The sensitivity calculation therefore supports a useful engineering envelope rather than a single fragile mass assumption.

## 8. Exhaust isolation and photon leakage

The protected atmosphere subtends a radius of about **3.83 degrees** from the screen. Properly directed narrow plumes can geometrically miss it, but electric-propulsion exhaust includes charge-exchange ions, fast neutrals and broad angular tails. Published kinetic plume modeling shows that these populations depend on the surrounding neutral and plasma environment; laboratory chamber results also require correction for facility effects.[10]

The reference exhaust carries **125 TW of kinetic power**. Suppose, only as an energy diagnostic, that 10% of energy deposited in the escape-producing region becomes escape work. At three lunar radii the Moon-only binding energy is approximately 0.94 MJ/kg. Limiting this contribution to 10 kg/s would then require less than **94 MW** of deposited incident energy, or about **7.5 × 10^-7 of the total exhaust kinetic power**. Tidal reductions in binding energy tighten that allowance somewhat.

This is a demanding condition. The proposed implementation uses paired outward-directed propulsion, narrow-beam engines where necessary, local neutral-gas capture and a measured angular exclusion region around the Moon. Magnetic protection may divert charged exhaust, but cannot be credited with removing fast neutrals. Coating contamination and the destination of spent propellant must also be traced over later heliocentric motion.

If this condition proves impractical, credible alternatives include propulsion units farther outside the optical footprint with tensioned load paths, externally delivered momentum, different exhaust species/velocity, or a much larger aperture on less demanding trajectories. None receives a free mass or efficiency credit here. The near-lunar screen is presently **conditional on sufficiently clean propulsion**, not a completed preferred flight design.

For photons, system transmission is approximately **f + (1 − f)T_material**, where f is uncovered aperture fraction. If material transmission is 10^-6 and f is 10^-4, leakage is about 10^-4 rather than 10^-6. An initial design range of 10^-5–10^-4 residual direct high-energy illumination is a useful modeling target, not a derived universal requirement. The climate/escape calculation must ultimately allocate different leakage limits by wavelength and time.

About 976,000 cells of 10 × 10 km would tile the nominal aperture. A missing complete cell is roughly 10^-6 of total area. Overlapping edges, replacement overlays, optical inspection and local failure isolation should keep maintenance from opening large persistent holes. Finite-Sun ray tracing must also verify local illumination through gaps; a small global leakage fraction can conceal a concentrated leak.

## 9. Residual heating and the cold exobase

The screen reduces direct solar irradiation. It does not actively refrigerate every atmospheric layer. The energy equation must still include residual photons, photoelectrons, chemical energy, infrared cooling, conduction, wave heating, particles and energy transferred upward from the lower atmosphere. The 250 K target is an output to achieve, rather than a temperature that can simply be entered into the final escape calculation.[3]

There is a relevant source of radiation outside the screen's direct beam. Interplanetary hydrogen produces diffuse Lyman-alpha emission. A published heliospheric analysis discusses approximately **800–1,000 Rayleigh at 1 AU**.[11] Using an isotropic 1,000 Rayleigh field as a diagnostic gives about 4.08 µW/m² onto a plane from its facing hemisphere, or **1.39 GW incident on a sphere of three lunar radii**.

Converting 10% of all that energy into escape work would correspond to about 150 kg/s. This deliberately simplified energy conversion is **not a prediction of escape**: Lyman-alpha couples to particular species and does not ionize bulk nitrogen and oxygen in the way shorter wavelengths do. The result establishes that diffuse irradiation and chemistry must be included when assessing a stringent 10 kg/s total-loss target. A Sun-facing filter cannot suppress an all-sky field by the same factor as direct sunlight.

The filter's own thermal emission also returns some energy toward the Moon. For absorbed sunlight of 10–100 W/m² and two-face emissivity 0.2–0.8, the simple radiative-equilibrium temperature spans roughly 102–258 K. At the reference geometry, 50 W/m² absorption produces approximately **0.13 W/m²** of infrared irradiation on a plane near the Moon's center. This is small relative to sunlight, but spectral cooling and heating calculations should retain it.

A coupled atmospheric solution must establish the molecular and atomic composition through the homopause and exobase, a cold trap that limits water transport upward, non-LTE cooling where applicable, and the response to the filtered day/night cycle. The preceding molecular-only model gave 0.128 kg/s at approximately 250 K and 0.343 kg/s at 260 K, but atomic enrichment can alter escape much more than that temperature increment.[2]

The engineering decision is whether the solution achieves low loss intrinsically, or requires continuous modest replenishment. Both can fit within the construction industry's capabilities. They are different environmental outcomes, particularly for water and lighter atmospheric species.

## 10. Lunar magnetic protection: a calculable reference

A field that supplies magnetic pressure comparable to solar-wind dynamic pressure can create an obstacle to the wind. For pressures of 2, 20 and 100 nPa, the corresponding magnetic fields are approximately **71, 224 and 501 nT**. This is a pressure-balance criterion, not a cosmic-ray dose criterion. Laboratory and lunar studies of mini-magnetospheres support physical solar-wind deflection, while leaving substantial scaling and topology questions.[12]

An initial hardware reference uses **four regional superconducting installations**, each with a 100 km radius current loop, and a combined dipole moment of **1.5 × 10^21 A m²**. Two installations lie near each lunar pole, centered about 200 km from the spin axis. All moments are parallel. Their current paths close locally within each installation; no Moon-encircling current ring is assumed.

This geometry is introduced to obtain a concrete conductor and structural budget. Other arrangements of anchored and orbital nodes could replace it if they achieve better plasma behavior or construction economics. Anchoring removes the continuous stationkeeping burden from the largest initial magnets. The terrain, tunnel, tower and anchorage design remains substantial.

The conductor model assumes engineering current density of 100 A/mm² and composite density 6,000 kg/m³. The support model assumes working specific strength of 1 MJ/kg. These are specified design properties, including practical derating in the eventual material qualification. Actual supports must handle terrain, gravity, local bending, joints, temperature and failure loads in addition to ideal hoop tension.

Modern REBCO development provides a material foothold: the SPARC model-coil program demonstrated 20.1 T and 40.5 kA, with substantial structural loading. That does not demonstrate a regional lunar coil, its aggregate conductor length, or safe management of its vastly greater stored energy.[13]

## 11. Field geometry, forces and maintenance

Treating the four installations as displaced dipoles, a spherical sampling calculation gives a **minimum vacuum field of 637 nT at three lunar radii**. The source loops are small compared with their distance to those evaluation points, so the dipole approximation is useful there. The field is strongly nonuniform; the maximum on the same sphere is approximately 3.95 µT.

| Applied scalar pressure | Minimum radius where modeled field pressure matches it | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 nPa | 12,470 km from lunar center | Typical-pressure size diagnostic |
| 20 nPa | 8,170 km | Compressed size diagnostic |
| 100 nPa | 5,850 km | Still outside the 5,212 km optical/atmospheric reference radius |

These radii are **not simulated magnetopause surfaces**. Reconnection, cusps, tail geometry, ionospheric currents and plasma loading alter the boundary and transport. A magnetic field can open escape channels as well as reduce direct wind access; planetary escape research explicitly demonstrates why magnetization alone is insufficient.[14]

The ideal four-installation conductor-plus-support estimate is **2.14 × 10^14 kg**. A preliminary planning allowance of **3 × 10^14–3 × 10^15 kg** accommodates some additional construction and equipment. This is a range for industrial comparison, rather than a probabilistic cost estimate. At its upper end it is approximately 0.10% of atmospheric mass.

Magnetic support forces cannot be eliminated by assuming that the field is nearly lossless. For a loop, the self-inductance and hoop force grow with ampere-turns and geometry; their support mass is usually more consequential than the superconducting material alone. Magnetic-energy and virial arguments provide a useful check against underestimating those forces.[15]

Four effective cold winding surfaces with a 1 km bundle radius have roughly 1.58 × 10^10 m² of area. Illustrative heat leaks of 0.01–0.1 W/m² and refrigeration coefficients of performance of 0.002–0.01 produce electrical requirements of roughly **16–790 GW**, before uncertain support penetrations and joint heating. Achieving those heat leaks within a habitable atmosphere requires sealed vacuum cryostats and thermal intercepts. The model does not assume that exposed lunar equipment remains cold after terraforming.

The four calculated self-energies sum to **1.68 × 10^20 J**. Mutual inductance changes the total. Dividing a coil into switches does not automatically divide the available fault energy: the relevant expression is one-half I-transpose L I for the coupled current system. Quench containment, energy recovery, remote siting and gradual replacement are essential design tasks. This is a genuine engineering difficulty even though the initial charging energy is affordable to an industry operating at petawatt scales.

## 12. Distributed nodes and useful corridors

The original incremental-node idea remains useful if each stage has a measurable function. Early nodes can protect instruments, depots and selected inhabited facilities from solar-wind interaction or some solar-particle exposure. Later nodes can increase magnetic path length along busy routes. Broad continuous coverage is a later design objective, not a prerequisite for retaining the lunar atmosphere.

Particle rigidity distinguishes those roles. For a proton, magnetic bending depends on momentum per charge; the approximate deflection is **theta = 0.2998 BL / rigidity**, using BL in tesla-metres and rigidity in GV. The following values are calculated for one radian of bending, not for a specified dose reduction.

| Proton kinetic energy | Required BL for one radian | Gyroradius at 71 nT | Gyroradius at 50 µT |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 MeV | 0.145 T m | 2,040 km | 2.89 km |
| 100 MeV | 1.48 T m | 20,900 km | 29.7 km |
| 1 GeV | 5.66 T m | 79,700 km | 113 km |
| 10 GeV | 36.4 T m | 512,000 km | 727 km |

As one explicit scale example, a **2,000 km radius loop** producing 50 µT at its isolated center requires 1.59 × 10^8 ampere-turns. The same conductor/support model gives approximately **4.04 × 10^11 kg per station** and 2.44 × 10^17 J stored energy. Two hundred such stations have an ideal aggregate conductor/support mass near **8.09 × 10^13 kg**. This is a finite, potentially affordable material budget relative to terraforming; the stations are physically enormous.

This example does not establish a protected corridor. Overlap changes fields and mutual forces; trajectories and stationkeeping may dominate maintenance. A static row between Earth and Moon is not a family of free Keplerian orbits. The more credible implementation first optimizes transfer trajectories and occupied destinations, then places field systems on compatible trajectories, accepting variable corridor geometry where appropriate.

Plasma inflation may reduce the coil size required for some solar-wind barriers. It is an option to validate, without assigning it unmeasured amplification factors for high-energy particles. Plasma sources, electron/ion distributions, current closure, losses and external pressure all enter the balance. A nominal 50 µT field has magnetic pressure near 0.001 Pa, vastly above a few nPa of solar-wind ram pressure; such a field requires its own forces and current architecture.

## 13. Galactic cosmic rays and broad cislunar coverage

Magnetic bending alone does not establish a habitable free-space dose. Cosmic rays arrive over a broad angular and energy distribution and include heavy nuclei. Trapped particles, precipitation, support structures and secondary radiation can affect the result. Published Monte Carlo studies of superconducting shields find configurations where material-generated secondaries offset benefits from primary-particle deflection.[16]

Neutral photons and neutrons are not magnetically deflected. The lunar atmosphere is therefore the principal surface radiation resource: the reference has an average mass column of approximately **7,866 g/cm²**, around 7.6 terrestrial sea-level columns. That is a powerful shielding input, though exact surface and high-altitude doses require particle transport through the actual atmosphere. The column decreases markedly with altitude; it cannot be assigned unchanged to every airborne habitat.

It is also incorrect to demand Earth-strength magnetic field everywhere in a cislunar shield and conclude immediately that the energy is impossible. For an ideal dipole, the equatorial vertical cutoff approximately follows **R_c = 0.2998 Br/4** in GV. Maintaining a given cutoff while increasing system radius permits field strength to decrease as 1/r. The characteristic volume-field energy then scales approximately with radius, not radius cubed. This diagnostic does not include polar access or engineering field geometry.

At radius 400,000 km, a 20 GV equatorial vertical cutoff corresponds to about **0.667 µT**, with a uniform-volume magnetic-energy scale of **4.75 × 10^19 J**. Holding 50 µT throughout the same volume would instead give approximately **1.07 × 10^24 J**. Neither figure is a hardware design; they show why coverage must be specified by particle acceptance rather than a copied field strength.

Generating the ideal dipole moment for the 20 GV example requires about **4.27 × 10^26 A m²**. Illustratively summing 1,000 km radius node moments gives the following standalone conductor/support costs. These omit mutual inductance and field cancellation and must not be interpreted as optimized full-region designs.

| Number of 1,000 km radius nodes | Summed ideal conductor/support mass | Fraction of initial atmospheric mass |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1,000 | 9.56 × 10^19 kg | About 30 atmospheres |
| 10,000 | 9.61 × 10^18 kg | About 3 atmospheres |
| 100,000 | 1.01 × 10^18 kg | About 0.32 atmospheres |

The large costs arise from trying to create a regional dipole with many relatively small current loops. Longer effective current paths, different topology, or targeted coverage could change them substantially. Broad cislunar radiation protection is thus an open optimization problem that can be comparable to the terraform, while useful local protection can be far smaller.

Shielded occupied volumes remain a useful complement. A 100 m radius spherical habitat with 1–10 tonnes/m² of shielding needs roughly 1.3 × 10^8–1.3 × 10^9 kg of shielding material. Those columns are comparison inputs, not certified dose specifications. Protecting occupied destinations can proceed before free space between all destinations meets an Earth-surface-like dose target.

## 14. Construction envelope and factories

The dominant initial terraform challenge remains obtaining and delivering the atmospheric inventory while disposing of mining products and transfer heat. Nitrogen is especially consequential. Bennu sample analysis measured nitrogen-rich material but does not establish a project-sized mine: applying approximately 0.24 wt% nitrogen to this atmosphere requires roughly **1.05 × 10^21 kg of feedstock at ideal recovery**. Concentrated outer-system sources could change the excavation requirement while increasing transport and source-development demands.[17]

| Capability | Initial atmospheric construction | Reference lunar protection core |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bulk inventory | 3.14 × 10^18 kg of atmosphere; additional water and industrial material | About 6 × 10^12 kg held optical equipment plus roughly 3 × 10^14–3 × 10^15 kg magnetic installations |
| Continuous mass flow | 199 million kg/s over 500 years | 0.278 million kg/s screen fuel; approximately 0.095–0.95 million kg/s magnet replacement if 100-year life |
| Electrical scale | 33–235 PW representative transport cases, plus other industry | 0.179 PW screen mean; 0.028 PW illustrative fuel processing/transport; roughly 0.01–0.10 PW magnet rebuilding at 100 MJ/kg and 100-year life |
| Precision production | Propulsion, power, extraction, thermal and autonomous logistics hardware | Large optical area, deposited coatings, very long superconducting conductors and monitored structural systems |
| Irreversible resource consumption | Imported atmosphere and lost processing material | Exhaust dominates the reference long-term mass loss; hardware recycling reduces fresh feed |
| New difficult behavior | Safe bulk delivery and stable planetary atmosphere | Clean screen exhaust, durable aperture coverage, global plasma topology and very large coupled magnetic faults |

Building the optical aperture over 150 years requires approximately **6.51 × 10^11 m²/year**, or **20,600 m²/s** of finished film. In geometric throughput alone that is equivalent to roughly 206 lines each processing a 100 m wide web at 1 m/s. This comparison establishes a factory scale; it does not assert that 100 m wide precision multilayer coating and deployment lines exist. Multiple slower, narrower lines could supply the same area.

At 50 g/m², the corresponding film/support mass flow is approximately 1,030 kg/s. An illustrative 100 MJ/kg production energy gives about **103 GW** over the construction interval. Manufacturing energy can exceed or fall below that assumption; film precision, yield, packaging and deployment matter more than raw silica mass.

An approximately 10 × 10 km optical cell carries about 5 million kg of base allocation and roughly 6.3 million kg including proportional power and fuel. Its share of mean electrical power is about 183 MW and fuel consumption about 0.29 kg/s. Each cell can distribute thrust across many smaller engines and subpanels; the calculation does not require a single 183 MW thruster.

Constructing 3 × 10^14–3 × 10^15 kg of magnetic installations over 150 years requires approximately **63,000–634,000 kg/s** of finished equipment and supports. That is a demanding high-quality manufacturing program, but its mass flow is approximately 0.03–0.32% of the initial average gas addition. The comparison supports shared industrial infrastructure while highlighting different product quality requirements.

## 15. Billion-year operation and resource renewal

The billion-year objective requires continuous replacement and adaptation. It cannot reasonably mean that a film, computer, cryostat or magnet survives unchanged for a billion years. The relevant quantities are annual replacement throughput, recoverable material fraction, dissipated energy, fresh feed and the ability to rebuild the infrastructure that performs the rebuilding.

Replacing the base filter every 100 years requires about **1,547 kg/s** of gross production. Replacing all its dry held hardware on the same interval raises this to about 1,883 kg/s. For magnetic installations at 3 × 10^14–3 × 10^15 kg, 100-year replacement requires roughly **95,000–951,000 kg/s**. At 99.9% material recovery, fresh magnetic feed becomes approximately 95–951 kg/s; the gross refining and manufacturing work still has to occur.

The reference screen exhaust is a different matter. At 278,000 kg/s, a billion years consumes **8.79 × 10^21 kg**, or approximately **12% of the Moon's current mass**. With the two-hour peak-power storage variant, that rises to about **23%**. That cannot be dismissed as negligible and would be a poor reason to excavate the Moon itself. A sustainable design uses large extraterrestrial reservoirs of suitable common propellant or eventually adopts a different momentum architecture. Xenon cannot be assumed to be the long-term working fluid at this scale.

Candidate common-material thrusters must demonstrate the required exhaust velocity, efficiency, erosion life and plume cleanliness with the chosen propellant. Water-derived species, oxygen, nitrogen, metals or other materials have different ionization, feed-system and contamination behavior. The reference 30 km/s and 70% efficiency are not guaranteed for every feedstock.

Propellant recapture does not automatically remove consumption: capturing the exhaust transfers its momentum somewhere, and returning it requires another complete force and energy balance. Solar-wind momentum, beamed energy or externally accelerated pellets could change the architecture, but a claimed closed cycle must include the external momentum reservoir.

For the reference hardware, an illustrative maintenance industry operates at a few tenths of a petawatt and around a million kg/s of gross material processing. These are below the initial terraform's modeled power and delivery flows. Long-term steady capability can therefore be smaller than the initial construction peak, even though its cumulative resource use becomes much larger.

Solar luminosity, high-energy output and the Earth–Moon orbit evolve over the service interval. The filter's thermal passband and geometry must be adjustable, with continuing atmospheric monitoring. Solar high-energy evolution and bolometric brightening are different processes and cannot be represented by a single constant attenuation factor.[18] Continuing industrial and institutional capacity for geological time is an additional condition that engineering calculations cannot forecast with confidence.

## 16. Construction within five centuries

A completion deadline before **2526** is not excluded by a demonstrated minimum physical timescale in these component calculations. It is also not a justified forecast. The initial terraform already assumes an extraordinary expansion in autonomous extraterrestrial industry, power generation and mass transport. Lunar protection adds substantial precision manufacturing and control requirements, with lower bulk throughput than atmosphere delivery.

The following is a conditional development sequence measured from 2026. Date ranges are planning allocations, not predictions of technical progress.

| Interval | Required work | Milestone that must be achieved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Years 0–50 | Atmospheric/photochemical modeling; broadband filter coupons; plume and plasma experiments | A compatible spectral window and measurable loss budget; no unexplained dependence on an assumed 250 K boundary |
| Years 30–120 | Orbital optical demonstrators; common-propellant electric propulsion; autonomous lunar extraction and manufacturing | Durable square-kilometer-class optical production and integrated power/propulsion performance with acceptable contamination |
| Years 80–220 | Regional factories, depots, industrial power, larger magnetic and optical demonstrators | Reproducible construction and replacement of complete subsystems, including their manufacturing equipment |
| Years 150–350 | Deploy protection infrastructure, establish source mines and high-throughput transfers | Shield coverage and service logistics verified before introducing the vulnerable large atmosphere |
| Years 250–480 | Controlled atmosphere buildup, surface conditioning and climate commissioning | Stable atmosphere under measured filtered illumination, solar disturbances and maintenance outages |
| Years 480–500 | Commission long-term operations and reserve capacity | All critical replacement chains running continuously with realistic stocks and redundancy |

The schedule becomes credible only after concrete industrial milestones. For scale, growing a finished-hardware production rate from 10^4 to 10^8 kg/s over 300 years requires about **3.1% sustained annual compound growth**. That arithmetic does not establish feasibility: the industry must obtain energy, resources, automation, heat rejection and market or program support for every doubling. Beginning from a much smaller seed raises the required growth.

The protection system does not appear to require a fundamentally higher energy civilization than the initial atmosphere build. It does require successful integration before the main atmosphere is exposed. The five-century plan should therefore be paced by coupled environmental proof and industrial reproduction, rather than by a calendar commitment made from component scaling alone.

## 17. Decision gates and design changes

| Gate | Quantitative or observable test | Consequence if unmet |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atmospheric closure | Habitable lower climate, cold upper atmosphere and quantified species losses under the actual filtered spectrum | Change spectrum, composition, water management or replenishment strategy; the lifetime claim remains unsupported |
| Optical material | Measured 25–120 nm behavior plus full UV/X-ray/visible/IR transmission, temperature response and aging | Change film materials or thickness; revise total sunlight and propulsion mass |
| Aperture coverage | Wavelength-dependent leakage and failure recovery demonstrated across finite-Sun geometry | Add overlap, repair capacity or additional optical layers |
| Propulsion closure | Integrated specific power comfortably above the approximately 54 W/kg mathematical threshold at reference exhaust conditions | Change exhaust velocity, power architecture, areal mass or positioning |
| Plume isolation | Deposited power compatible with the atmospheric energy budget; reference 10 kg/s diagnostic implies below approximately 10^8 W | Relocate thrust production, change engines or choose another optical architecture |
| Magnetic hardware | Structural, cryogenic and coupled-fault design within a revised mass allowance | Change coil size, number, siting or use another field topology |
| Plasma protection | Species-resolved loss with cusps, reconnection, solar-wind extremes and screen exhaust | Reconfigure fields; do not assume that stronger magnetization always improves retention |
| Corridor radiation | Monte Carlo dose and particle trajectories over realistic occupied routes | Adjust node placement and retain local material shielding |
| Long-term industry | Closed replacement chains and fresh-resource supply, including irreversible exhaust | Revise service architecture and reserve capacity |

These gates make the present design actionable. The highest-priority scientific task is the coupled atmospheric calculation with a measured candidate filter. The highest-priority engineering tasks are plume isolation, low-mass aperture maintenance and safe regional magnet construction. Broad cislunar cosmic-ray protection should advance through separate demonstrations while the lunar core is developed.

## 18. Alternatives and comparison boundaries

A screen that follows the Moon while remaining approximately **1.5 million km sunward** has a larger aperture and a higher holding cost. With the same film allocation, 45-degree exhaust cant, specific power and fuel buffer, the simplified model gives approximately **3.42 PW mean power** and **5.31 million kg/s propellant**. Its billion-year exhaust consumption exceeds two lunar masses. This makes a Moon-following optical screen near the Earth–Sun L1 distance less attractive in the present propulsion formulation.

A broad Earth–Sun L1 aperture can avoid following the Moon's monthly projected motion by covering the whole ray bundle. A conservative full disk requires radius about **420,000 km**, and approximately **2.77 × 10^16 kg** at 50 g/m². A narrow projected-orbit band is about **839,000 × 101,000 km**, with roughly **4.24 × 10^15 kg** of film allocation. These are geometrical aperture estimates only. L1 is not a rigid support, and a static sheet or swarm spanning that region does not automatically have a dynamically viable trajectory.

Solar-radiation-pressure control of distributed shade elements has a published conceptual basis, but applying it to a lunar spectral filter requires its own orbit and force solution.[19] A broad system may trade more hardware for less consumable propulsion. Its optical coupling to Earth, flexible dynamics, orientation and long-term maintenance prevent selecting it on aperture mass alone.

The reference near-lunar design is selected here because its continuous force and propulsion mass can be explicitly budgeted. It is not established as the global optimum. A complete design competition should compare lifetime resource use, plume impact, fabrication area, Earth illumination and the cost of trajectory control across all three architecture families.

## 19. Model scope and verification

The accompanying model contains original calculations for multilayer transmission, material absorption, measured-solar-spectrum weighting, finite-Sun aperture geometry, lunar-phase holding forces, propulsion mass feedback, coil energy and support, displaced magnetic sources, particle rigidity, replacement throughput and residual-heating diagnostics. Input optical and solar data are retained with source links and hashes. The outputs use SI units with explicit wavelength and energy labels.

The optical calculation was checked against vacuum transmission and the analytic lossless single-slab Fabry–Perot result, agreeing to floating-point precision. The multilayer calculation conserves energy to numerical precision. Increasing lunar-phase resolution from 2,048 to 8,192 points changes the peak holding acceleration by less than one part per million. Those checks establish implementation consistency, not physical validation of unmeasured coatings or a future propulsion system.

The atmospheric inventory and original transport-power comparison are inherited from the preceding integrated feasibility model. This study does not rerun that full model or replace its climate, chemistry and source-development limitations. The new calculations provide more concrete protection hardware and service budgets, while leaving the coupled environmental performance explicitly open.

No climate GCM, non-LTE photochemical model, full lunar ephemeris optimization, flexible-structure simulation, global kinetic/MHD model, high-energy particle-transport model, mining-reserve survey or probabilistic lifetime reliability simulation is represented as completed. Those models are required to promote the conditional design into a verified engineering proposal.

The justified conclusion is **conditional feasibility of the lunar protection core within the initial terraform's broad industrial envelope**, with clean propulsion and coupled atmospheric behavior as potential design-changing constraints. Maintaining the equipment is a smaller ongoing industrial task than constructing the atmosphere, while a billion years of consumable supply is a major cumulative commitment. Extending protection to useful near-space routes remains plausible; Earth-surface-like radiation conditions throughout the Earth–Moon region remain a separate unresolved objective.
