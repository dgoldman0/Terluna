At the edge of a lunar lake, tables are being carried toward the water. Low sunlight catches the reeds and the rims of bowls; across the water, long shadows lie in the folds of a crater rim. Someone opens a covered dish, and the smell of bread reaches the path where people are arriving from the landing. Over the trees, Earth is coming into clearer view, a blue-and-white curve with clouds stretched above an ocean. Here, the last arrivals are finding places beside the water.

By the time the music begins, the earliest arrivals have finished their meal and shadows have crossed the terrace and climbed the walls behind it. People leave to walk beside the reeds, return for another song, and find their companions still talking farther along the table. A ferry approaches above the lake, its broad envelope catching sunlight along its curved flank. It brings people down from homes among the heights, then rises again with passengers bound for the forest.

From its rail, the shore opens into inlets and narrow waterways threaded through the reeds. Roofs recede beneath the cabin as the ferry draws alongside the crowns, where paths and gardens appear at several levels through the leaves. Beyond the forest margin, gardens and roofs hang beneath the broad curves of buoyant envelopes, their tether lines descending toward clearings along the shore. A glider banks toward a landing below them, briefly showing the full breadth of its wings. A person looking out from a kitchen can watch that arrival across an expanse of air, with the lake visible through the space beneath the neighboring homes.

At the forest landing, a path winds among the branches. In their forks, small wet gardens grow in dark mats of roots and fallen leaves, and a fern leans over water in which the sky’s reflection trembles. Rain caught high in the crowns drains through these gardens, gathering along leaf tips before falling into the growth below. Looking over the handrail, you can follow hanging roots toward another level of foliage. Farther down, the path passes under a garden you have just crossed.

The path descends beneath successive roofs of foliage. Openings in the leaves narrow overhead, while the smell of wet bark and decaying wood grows stronger around the lower trunks. The handrail is damp beneath your palm. Beyond it, trunks rise through the gardens and disappear among the leaves; water runs down their bark, gathers at a ledge, then falls again. Through a break beside the path, the sound of drops striking a pool reaches you before the water becomes visible. The walkway bends around the trunk toward it, passing a fallen limb where a few fungi shine faintly along the wood. Above this dim interior, sunlight still falls across the upper leaves. Below the walkway, fallen leaves have softened into a dark bed threaded with fine roots.

A stream flows from the pool toward the lake. Where the trees thin, it threads between reed beds toward open water. After hours beneath the leaves, the sky beyond the forest is still bright. Music reaches the path from the terrace. A few of the tables have been cleared, and people are making room for those coming back from the water. High above, the gardens lie in a final band of sunlight, while lamps mark the landings beneath the airborne homes.

Communities could make these long transitions recurring occasions, with many ordinary days between one sunset and the next sunrise. Behind shaded bedroom windows, people would keep their own hours through the long daylight; during the lunar night, they would cook, work, and visit one another in lamplit rooms. The return of dawn could bring another reason to assemble beside the lake. A child first brought across the lake asleep against a parent’s shoulder could, years later, take the ferry to meet the family, knowing which terrace they would choose and how late they liked to stay.

From that familiar landing, the child could one day leave to see the rest of the Moon. An aerial route might cross pale highlands where cloud lies against the slopes and gardens grow around sheltered settlements. Through breaks in the cloud, terraces would appear below, with footpaths descending between buildings into mist. Farther on, a town beside an inland sea could be preparing for dawn. Someone who had grown up knowing every turn of the forest path would arrive with a bag, an address, and directions to a house overlooking a different shore. At the window, they could watch the first light reach a landscape they had yet to explore.

Building those shores would require an industry extending across the Solar System, with the capacity to supply a world’s air and water and keep its protective structures in service. The water itself could be recovered as ice beneath the faint sunlight of distant bodies, carried inward and released into prepared basins. As clouds formed over the lakes, it could fall across the highlands and collect along the leaves outside someone’s window. At a sink in an airborne home, a person could wash their hands in water that had crossed interplanetary space, while rain fell past the gardens toward the lake below.

The first people working toward that world might know its shores through drawings and its gardens through plants in a chamber, learning which communities can live through its long nights. Their experiments would help determine which of those places could eventually be made. Much later, someone visiting Earth could look up between the roofs and point toward the place they had once lived. Within that small bright disk would be the lake they crossed as a child, friends living above another shore, and a familiar landing where the path turns beneath the trees. They could remember the damp handrail, the sound of the hidden pool, and the long evenings when there was time to walk that way before supper.
