# Sport on the Open Moon

**7 November 2026.** The aim is one good sport that people on the Moon want to play and watch, physically, and that translates into a competitive e-sport. This study comes at it from the body before any rules, as the dance work did: what a person can do at a sixth of Earth's gravity in dense air, measured against Earth for the same body, and what that points to for play, leaving the choice of a sport to the author. The first round (7 November) was closed-form mechanics for two bodies ([envelope.py](envelope.py), product [results/envelope.json](results/envelope.json)) and measurements of the dance's one solved whole phrase ([whole_body.py](whole_body.py), [results/whole_body.json](results/whole_body.json)). The second (8 November) builds what lies between a body and a game: a vocabulary of athletic moves, an engine for where players can be and when, and four contests between players, from which it draws candidate forms of play (below, "What emerges when bodies meet a ball"). The author's directions are in the decisions register, section "Sport from the body, 7 November 2026".

## Where the earlier sports went wrong

The sports work of 31 October to 1 November produced three candidate disciplines for an early community's hall (a gate circuit, aerial figures and an aerial catch game: [research/studies/world_economy/sports_concepts.md](../../research/studies/world_economy/sports_concepts.md)), a brainstorm of three more (Span, Holdfast and Weave; the author rejected Holdfast and Weave and judged Span weak) and two adversarial screens of an aerial territorial handball (v0.1, v0.2). The sports work's retrospective handoff (`terluna_lunar_sports_esports_handoff.md`, through 6 November, sha256 `59fd0058…3883`) and the two screens' reports were read for this study; their identities and hashes, and the checks behind the points below, are on the research drive in `session-tools/sports_diagnosis/`.

Those designs were games for points on a court. Each player was a centre of mass with a launch speed and a friction circle, so the work had no vocabulary of lunar moves worth doing. Rules and fixtures had to supply the lunar character (score only by an airborne catch inside a gate in the air, receive and pass within one jump, one launch a possession), and the screens found the cheapest legal score, a stationary jump beside the goal. The contest sat in the slow ballistic arcs, settled at take-off, while hands and ball carried almost no choice. Elevated gates, lit panels, light trails and names arrived before any play did, and the work reached the author as rules and point plots. The dance went the other way, from the body's envelope to a vocabulary of units and a grammar, then to phrases solved on a whole body and judged from renders, and its most convincing result, the hanging salto, came from the body.

The point model also got the physics wrong in five places, each quantified below: the v0.2 braking test left out the grip a landing has; the defenders' ground movement left out balance; the ground shots had no thrower; contact between bodies in the air was a hazard without a mechanism; and the flight was empty time.

## What a body can do

Figures are for the dance's body model (1.64 m, 55 kg), with the larger athlete (1.80 m, 80 kg, that body scaled) in brackets where it differs, at the shared gravity and the design run's air (1.40 kg/m^3), on an athletic floor with a friction coefficient of 0.8. Earth's figures are for the same body. Each rests on the assumptions stated beside it in envelope.py and in [sources.json](sources.json).

**Two clocks.** Everything the centre of mass does runs on gravity's clock: the balance time √(h/g) is 0.75 s on the Moon (0.78 s) against 0.30 s on Earth. The limbs, and anything they throw or strike, keep Earth's tempo. A player's position changes slowly and a player's hands act fast.

**Getting going starts with a lean.** Before a step can push, the body has to lean off its feet, and moving the centre of mass the first 0.1 m sideways that way takes 0.88 s on the Moon against 0.36 s on Earth. Once pushing at the grip's limit, reaching 2 m/s takes 1.5 s and 1.5 m (Earth 0.25 s and 0.25 m), and 4 m/s takes 3.1 s and 6.2 m.

**Travel turns and stops in its landings, and a sharp cut costs a jump.** A stride's stance can change the travel by up to 1.1-1.4 m/s at the grip's limit (3.2-3.6 on Earth), because the floor's grip acts on the stance's vertical impulse, which carries the landing and the next take-off. Over a whole stride the floor's push averages the body's weight, so a run still stops in about v²/2μg whatever the gait: 1.5 m from 2 m/s, 3.5 m from 3 m/s and 6.2 m from 4 m/s (Earth 0.25, 0.57 and 1.0 m). A player already coming down from a flight gets that flight back as braking: a landing at 2.5 m/s absorbed over 0.4 m can take up to 2.4 m/s off the travel, with about one Earth body weight of push. The v0.2 receiver, caught 2 m up and landing at 4 m/s along, stops about 1.9 m after touchdown, 8.1 m after the catch, where the screen had 12.4 m. A steady turn at 3 m/s needs a radius of 6.9 m (Earth 1.2 m; 4.6 m at a grip of 1.2), and a floor banked 24 degrees holds a 5 m circle at 4 m/s. A cut through one 0.33 s stance at 3 m/s needs a bounce of 0.15 m for 30 degrees, 0.8 m for 60 and 1.75 m for 90 (Earth: under 0.06 m for all three); at 4 m/s a 90-degree cut needs 3.3 m, more than the highest jump. At speed, a cut is a jump.

**A standing jump announces itself; a rebound does not.** The legs leave the floor at 3.05 m/s (3.14), rising 2.86 m (3.03) for 3.75 s (3.86), where on Earth they leave at 2.38 m/s for a 0.29 m rise and 0.49 s. Before a standing jump the body must sink, and it sinks no faster than g: a 0.29 m dip takes at least 0.63 s (Earth 0.31 s), so the highest standing jump takes at least 0.87 s from the dip's start to lift-off (Earth 0.62 s); a lower jump sinks less (second round, below). A jump out of a landing needs no dip. A sideways hop from a wide ready stance can push sideways at about 0.47 of its push upward, set by the stance's geometry: 2.3 m/s up gives 1.1 m/s sideways and covers 3.0 m in 2.8 s of flight (Earth 0.5 m in 0.47 s). Every hop fixes its arc: a 0.3 m rise holds the player for 1.2 s, a 1 m rise for 2.2 s, the highest jump for 3.75 s.

**The flight holds a phrase.** The highest jump's 3.75 s holds five shapes of 0.75 s. One layout somersault across it needs 15.7 N m s (26.7) and turns at 0.27 revolutions a second, where Earth's best flight needs 121 N m s and turns at 2.1; the take-off can give up to 47 N m s (78), and the solved hanging salto took 14.7. Hands, arms and shape act at Earth's tempo throughout; the arc itself stays fixed, and only contact changes it.

**Landings are gentle in force and hard in attitude.** A landing at 2.5 m/s absorbed over 0.4 m pushes 5.8 lunar body weights, 0.96 Earth body weights, where the same landing on Earth pushes 1.8; a 3 m drop lands like a 0.5 m one on Earth. What a landing does punish is turning: angular momentum picked up at the top of a 2.3 m/s jump keeps turning the body for the 1.42 s of the descent (Earth 0.23 s). A light bump of 5 N m s turns a laid-out body 43 degrees by the landing (25; Earth 7), and the same body tucked 171 degrees (98; Earth 28).

**Throws need anchoring.** The hand's push on a 0.45 kg handball over 0.15 s pushes the thrower back and turns them. With the feet under the body, the floor holds that turning push only up to a release of 3.0 m/s (4.3), against 18 m/s (26) on Earth; a long split stance, with the rear foot 0.5 m behind the centre of mass, holds 15 m/s (22), and a throw timed into a landing, when the floor pushes hardest, holds 17 m/s (25). A 10 m/s release asks 0.42 of the floor's grip just to keep the feet still (Earth 0.07). Thrown in the air at 10 m/s from overhead, the ball's reaction turns the thrower 31 degrees by the landing laid out (123 tucked); a chest pass near the centre of mass turns them 12 degrees. A caught ball does the same in reverse. A kick swings the leg with about 22 N m s of angular momentum (39); one foot can hold 8.9 N m of moment against it on the Moon (54 on Earth), so the arms and trunk take nearly all of it.

**Balls stay up and arrive softly.** In the design air a basketball falls at most 8.2 m/s, a handball 8.8, a football 7.5, a volleyball 6.2, a soft ball of 100 g 4.0 and a shuttlecock 2.5 (Earth 22, 23, 20, 16, 10 and 6.7). Released at 10 m/s from 2 m, a handball carries 35 m in 6.8 s and arrives at 6 m/s (Earth 11 m in 1.5 s; without air 64 m); a 5 m/s lob carries 14 m and hangs 4.3 s. Air takes 45% of the vacuum range at 10 m/s and 63% at 15, so a hard throw gains little: 52 m at 15 m/s. Receivers have seconds, and passes can be long.

**Contact shoves.** Two players closing at 2 m/s change speed by about 1 m/s each, with Earth's energy at that speed (55 J for two 55 kg bodies); taking it up through the grip of the feet takes 0.77 s and slides each 0.38 m (Earth 0.13 s and 0.06 m), and at 3 m/s, 1.15 s and 0.87 m. A standing push is limited to the pusher's grip, 71 N (104) against 431 N (628) on Earth. In the air, two players closing at 1 m/s move each other's landing by 0.71 m (Earth 0.12 m). A teammate's two-arm push of 350 N over 0.6 m throws a 55 kg player 1.75 m up for 2.9 s and an 80 kg player 1.0 m for 2.2 s; on Earth it lifts neither.

**Getting down and getting up both take twice Earth's time.** A body sinks no faster than g, and a rise slows no faster than g, since the floor cannot pull: lowering the centre of mass 0.3 m and stopping takes 0.64 s (Earth 0.32), rising 0.3 m and stopping 0.65 s (0.37), and reaching down 0.45 m toward the floor 0.78 s (0.39). A topple from 2 to 35 degrees takes 2.7 s (1.1). Changes of height that end in motion, a jump up or a drop into a landing, stay quick at their muscle end.

**What microgravity lacks.** Without weight there is no grip, no stop and no turn without a surface to push against, no arc to read and no return to play.

## What the solved whole body did

The dance's whole phrase of 6 November (*Under the Canopy, with the hanging salto*, the 55 kg body on the dance floor's 0.5 grip) is the one solved whole phrase with the body kept out of itself. Its flights fall freely within 1-4% of g, the grand jete's long flight at 0.92 of it, the engine's Euler step showing. On 7 November the salto and the run, jete and carve were solved again with landings costed, and on 8 November a check landing on both worlds and the salto landing on the balls of the feet, as the last two bullets record; [results/whole_body.json](results/whole_body.json) measures all ten motions.

- **Strides.** Each stance of the run changed the travel by 0.30-0.55 m/s and turned it 6-14 degrees, using 37-66% of what its vertical impulse allowed at the floor's grip, with peaks of 3.7-7.0 lunar body weights (0.6-1.2 of Earth's).
- **Rebound and standing take-offs.** Out of a stride landing at 0.58 m/s down, the jete left at 1.55 m/s up 0.42 s later, at the engine's 900 N cap on one foot. The salto's standing take-off sank 0.22 m over 0.88 s and pushed for 0.18 s.
- **Hard landings are rigid.** The engine stops a landing foot with an instant impulse. At the jete's carve landing that impulse took 1.60 of the 1.86 m/s of vertical speed at once, and 0.46 m/s of the travel, a sideways share of 0.29 that stays inside the floor's 0.5 though nothing in the engine checks it; at the salto's landing it took 2.20 of 2.27 m/s. The stance's own pushes then peaked at 408 N and 185 N, and the body sank 6-10 cm. The engine had no landing stroke.
- **Landings costed (7 November).** Each landing impulse now keeps the friction cone and, where flights integrate with Runge-Kutta, is costed on what it does to the centre of mass (humanities/dance/README.md, "Landings with a stroke"). On the engine's Euler step the optimiser met the cost by floating down on the integration error, flying the salto at 0.39 of g and the jete at 0.50. With exact flights, a flat-footed salto landing on straight legs still let the impulse take half the speed; touching down on the balls of the feet and lowering the heels after 0.15 s, the impulse took 0.40 m/s of 2.25 m/s, inside the grip, and the body ended still over its feet. The stance then stopped the rest within 0.1 s at 17 lunar body weights (2.85 of Earth's), sinking 5 cm: the body did not sink through a stroke (next bullet). Since 7 November the engine's flights integrate with Runge-Kutta by default, the author's choice for the drafting stages.
- **Landing loads (8 November).** The engine now costs the floor's push through each landing in the body's Earth weight, the same on either world, at a weight with which a check landing on Earth lands as softly as people asked to land softly (humanities/dance/README.md, "Landing loads"). On the Moon that check, a jump that touches down on the balls of the feet at 2.2 m/s and sinks on them into a deep bend, pushes at most 8.3 lunar body weights (1.38 of Earth's), sinking 23 cm in 0.28 s and standing still 1.2 s after touchdown; without the cost it pushed 12.3 (2.04) and sank 16 cm. People landing at that speed on Earth push 2.7 Earth body weights landing softly and 3.8 landing naturally (Verniba et al. 2017), so a lunar landing taken through a stroke loads the legs about half as much as a soft landing on Earth. What had kept the engine's landings rigid was their shapes: on flat feet a deep bend needs more ankle than the body has, and straight legs on steep feet land as a column. Braking landings while travelling, the grand jete's and the carve's, still wait for their solve.

## Where the body points for play

These are readings of the envelope for the author to choose from; no sport follows from them by itself.

1. **Commitment is the currency.** The quick ways to move are jumps, and each fixes its arc for one to four seconds; on the ground the balance clock and the grip make repositioning slow. A player on the floor holds options and a player in the air has spent them, so feints, baiting and who commits first carry the play, as between a goalkeeper and a penalty taker on Earth, here for every player all the time.
2. **The dip is a tell, and bouncing is readiness.** The highest standing jump shows itself 0.6-0.9 s ahead, and a lower one less (a 1 m lunar rise 0.38 s, the second round's finding); a jump out of a landing or from a held crouch does not. Players who keep moving in low strides, or hold a ready crouch, are the quick ones.
3. **Landings steer.** Travel bends and brakes in landings, and a sharp cut at speed costs a jump. Play that lives in curves, arcs and bounds suits the floor; play that asks for Earth's cuts fights it. Banked floors and grippier shoes tighten the curves.
4. **The contest belongs to the hands.** Catching, passing, striking and shape changes run at Earth's tempo inside the slow arcs; the arcs carry positioning and commitment.
5. **Throws are soft and long, with power from the floor's push.** A player standing still throws gently; strong throws come from a long stance, a landing, or the air at the price of spin. Balls hang four to seven seconds and arrive at 4-6 m/s.
6. **Shared air is the hazard.** For the same bump at the top of the same jump, bodies meeting in the air turn about six times as far by the landing as on Earth, and landing attitude is where injuries would come from, since the loads stay mild. Play that puts opponents in the air together needs the body model's test of bumps and landings before it is chosen; contact on the ground shoves.
7. **Teammates can launch each other.** A teammate's push sends a player 1-1.75 m up, and a hold of a sixth of the weight is easy; ordinary players can boost one another, which on Earth takes trained bases and flyers, as in cheerleading and acrobatic gymnastics.
8. **Low play is slow.** Reaching a ball on the floor from standing takes 0.8 s; play at hand height is fast, so the ball belongs in the air and the hands.
9. **The e-sport.** The two clocks map onto controls: commitment inputs (when and where to launch, how to land) and fast actions inside the arc (catch, pass, shape, strike). An authentic game runs the body's physics in real time, which the dance engine's planned tracking controller also needs.

### Moves

The vocabulary holds 24 moves in the dance grammar's form, in four families: getting set and going, jumps, the ball, and contact. Each enters from a state (a posture, a momentum and the ball) and leaves in another, and carries the three times a contest turns on. Its tell is how long an opponent can see it coming before it can no longer be changed; its commitment, how long it then holds the body to its course; its recovery, how long before the next quick action. Every move can follow from the ready crouch and lead back to it. The moves come from court and field sports, athletics, gymnastics, cheerleading and the martial arts, timed by the envelope's mechanics. Six take their lunar form only on the Moon (the bound, the cut through a bounce, the running leap, the throw into a landing, the pass in the air and the teammate's boost), and four more change their role there (the drop step, the jump from the crouch, the chest pass and the shove).

**A held crouch is readiness.** Sinking into the wide crouch takes 0.47 s (0.23), since a body sinks no faster than g. Held, the crouch pushes sideways at 0.47 g from the first instant and jumps with no dip: a 1 m jump leaves the floor 0.17 s after the decision, less than a reaction time, with the hands reaching 3.25 m, and its best reaches 4.4 m. From upright the body must first tip: the first 0.1 m sideways takes 0.88 s (0.36) and the first metre 2.1 s (0.9), of which a quarter-second drop step saves 0.6 s.

**Above a walk, travel bounds, and every bound commits.** A foot can stay on the floor only below about √(gh), 1.2 m/s (3.0); faster, the gait leaves the floor. Each stance can change the travel by up to 1.1 m/s, from 0.7 on the dance floor to 1.6 at a grip of 1.2 (Earth 4.2), so building or shedding 3 m/s takes 2.3 s and 3.5 m (0.38 s and 0.57 m) and a steady turn at 3 m/s needs a 6.9 m radius (1.2 m). A 5 cm bound flies half a second, holding the runner to its arc.

**At speed a cut is a jump with a tell.** Turning 45 degrees at 3 m/s needs a bounce of 0.42 m, 1.27 m on the dance floor and 0.15 m at a grip of 1.2, where an Earth stride turns it with no bounce. The higher bound into the cut shows it coming 1.4 s ahead, though not which way, and the bound out holds the cutter as long.

**Jumps start cheaply and end slowly.** A jump sinks only as deep as its height needs. From upright a 1 m rise leaves after a 5 cm dip, 0.38 s after the jumper starts; the highest, 2.86 m, sinks 0.28 m and shows itself 0.87 s ahead, where Earth's best (0.29 m) shows itself 0.62 s ahead. Every jump then holds the player in its arc, 2.2 s for a 1 m rise.

**The stance sets the throw.** With the feet under the body the floor holds a chest pass of 9.9 m/s, where an overhand throw from the same stance holds 3.0, because the chest pass pushes close to the centre of mass. A long split stance holds an overhand throw of 14.9 m/s and a landing 17.3 m/s; in the air the arm reaches its own 20 m/s but turns the thrower, a 10 m/s release overhead by 31 degrees laid out and 123 tucked by the landing. A catch takes the same balance in reverse. Low balls are slow: reaching the floor takes 0.78 s and rising back 0.64 s.

**Contact pushes little and makes players face it.** A push leans the pusher back over the rear foot, so the lean limits it before the grip, to 0.43 of the pusher's weight on every floor (39 N; 234 N on Earth). Squared up in the wide crouch a player holds only 0.09 of the weight, and turned side-on 0.30. A teammate's two-arm push throws a 55 kg player 1.75 m up, which on Earth lifts no one. A topple takes 2.0 s to pass 35 degrees, and the whole fall and rise 3.5 s.

### Who can be where, and when

The reach engine moves a player's centre of mass under the least of three limits: the floor's grip, the lean about the support's edge, and the legs' sprint profile (7 N/kg at a standstill, falling to nothing at 8.5 m/s). The hands reach a band around the centre of mass, on the floor or by a jump timed to the point. From the ready crouch the centre of mass can be 0.3-0.5 m away after 1 s (1.6-2.5 m), 1.9-2.2 m after 2 s (7.0-8.4) and 4.8-5.2 m after 3 s (13.9-15.4), and the hands reach a point 3 m to the side after 2.1 s (1.0). On Earth the legs set that pace; on the Moon the grip does, up to 6-7 m/s, so a grippier floor makes lunar players quicker and leaves Earth's as they are. A ball crosses ground faster: a 15 m chest pass arrives in 1.8 s (0.9) and one from a split stance in 1.2 s, and a 12 m lob at 60 degrees hangs 5.6 s (2.2).

The same arc lasts 2.46 times as long on the Moon, and over that arc the two worlds' players compare differently at its two ends. In the first second of a lunar arc a player covers what an Earth player covers in the matching 0.4 s, about 0.4 m, because a start runs on gravity's clock on both worlds. Over a long arc the lunar player covers much more: 9.3 m in a 4 s lunar arc against 5.9 m in Earth's 1.6 s, and 21.7 m against 11.4 m over 6 s, more on grippier floors. A grip-limited body's reach, μgt²/2, is the same on both worlds over matching arcs, and Earth's players fall short of it because their legs limit them first. Short flights therefore favour the ball, and long flights the players.

### Four contests

**A pass against an interceptor.** A passer and a receiver stand 6, 10 or 15 m apart, and an interceptor takes each place of a grid beside the pass line. The passer throws any pass its stance holds, flat or lobbed, at the receiver or a few degrees to either side. Receiver and interceptor start 0.2 s after the release. A pass is safe if the receiver catches it at least 0.3 s before the interceptor's hands could first touch it. A reacting interceptor controls almost no floor on either world: 0-1% of the grid on the Moon, at most 8% on Earth. On Earth the ball is too fast for it; on the Moon its own start is too slow, though the ball is slower too. Only guessing gains floor. Started 1 s before the release, an interceptor controls 14-52% of the floor around the pass line on the Moon and 71-88% on Earth; started 0.3 s before, 4-15% (13-35%). On the Moon the grip decides much of this: at 15 m a 1 s guess controls 26% of the grid on the dance floor and 52% at a grip of 1.2. A fake pays more on the Moon. When it draws the guessing interceptor into a jump just before the release (0.7 m, or Earth's best from the crouch, 0.22 m), the lunar jump holds the interceptor 1.9 s in the air and leaves it 1-2% of the floor; Earth's lasts 0.4 s, and the interceptor keeps 1-32%, most of its guess at 15 m. The best safe passes on the Moon arrive in 1.5-3.2 s.

**A lob contested.** A ball is lobbed 12 m at 45, 60 or 70 degrees, a defender waits 3 m short of its landing or 3 m beside it, and a second player starts from each place of a grid around the landing. A high lob gathers a crowd. The first touch waits for the ball to come down into the jumpers' reach, so everyone who can be under the ball by then touches it within 0.3 s of the first and contests it. On the Moon that crowd reaches 11 m from the spot at 60 degrees and 22.5 m at 70 (378 and 1,570 m²; Earth 8.9 and 13.1 m, 245 and 530 m²), growing with the grip: 15.7 m on the dance floor and 28.7 m at 1.2 for the high lob, where Earth's hardly changes. The player who arrives first holds position, with 1-3 s to spare under the ball (0.5-1 s on Earth); 78-100% of first touches are jumps. A flatter lob goes to whoever is near the top of its arc: at 45 degrees only players within 2.6 m of that spot can contest it (5.6 m on Earth). The thrower's choice of height alone changes who gets the ball for 62-90% of the starting places (65-68% on Earth).

**A chase.** A pursuer and an evader start 4 m apart, both already moving, on an open floor or in square courts of 10, 20 and 40 m. Each strategy is a simple rule. The pursuer heads for where the evader will be, and the evader runs a loop inside the walls at the speed its turning allows, or flees by look-ahead. Each sees the other 0.2 s late. Equal players never close a gap on an open floor. In the courts a lunar pursuer tags the loop-runner in 13-21 s in the 10 and 20 m courts, and in the 40 m court not within a minute on the two lower grips (22 s at 1.2). Cuts and feints mostly hand the pursuer a read, because the bound into a cut is visible and the bound out cannot steer. They shorten the chase to 7-14 s in most courts, and a hop helps only when cornered in the 10 m court. The larger body's longer reach (0.66 m) tags in the 40 m court, where the dance body's closest approach, 0.65 m, stays just outside its own 0.60 m. Earth's loop-runner escaped every court within a minute against this pursuer, which overshoots at Earth's speeds. The tag times rate these rules, not the best play.

**Shoving for position.** Two players meet over a spot. The steady push is the lean's limit on every floor, so the grip changes nothing, and facing changes the hold fivefold. Equal players braced in the split stance stalemate. Moving a squared-up equal 0.5 m takes 1.7 s (0.7), and the larger body moves the braced dance body 0.5 m in 2.4 s (1.0), while the dance body cannot move it back. Deepening a lean by 0.2 m takes 0.6-0.8 s (0.24-0.33). When the opponent yields, the pusher lurches 0.2 m and settles in 2.2 s (Earth 1.2 m with a long step, 1.6 s). During a jumper's take-off push the floor holds more than a lunar shove, so the shove is absorbed (on Earth it is not). In the air a 0.2 s shove at the top of a jump moves the landing 0.19 m and turns the body 25 degrees laid out or 99 tucked by the landing (Earth 15 and 61).

### What stays open

A contest has depth while the outcome is still open after both sides have acted: no move dominates, decisions come with time to read and answer them, waiting has a cost and a value, and a fake can pay. Read that way, the four contests split.

The high ball is the open contest the Moon makes. Its long hang turns the moment of the throw into a gathering: players read the arc for seconds, choose where to stand, arrive and hold position, then commit to a leap whose timing and height decide the touch. The thrower's choice of height decides how many can contest, from a precise ball to one player near the top of a flat arc to a crowd of everyone within 20 m. Where the earlier designs' contests were settled at take-off, here only the ball's arc is. It is also where the shared-air hazard lives: a shove in the air turns a tucked body 99 degrees by the landing.

Passing is the safe way to move a ball. A passer always has a safe pass against one reacting defender, so defending is positioning before the ball moves and guessing, which on the Moon a fake undoes. That makes the pass a contest of commitments read through stances, open only while defenders have reason to guess. With one defender against a passer and a receiver, the passer always has a safe pass, and guessing gains the defender little.

The chase and the shove are slow and readable. Evasion by cutting gives the pursuer a read, so chases turn on space and speed. Contact moves positions slowly and weakly, so arriving first decides position more than pushing does. Both matter as parts of a ball game (getting under the ball, holding position) more than as games of their own.

### Forms of play for the author to judge

These are candidate play loops drawn from the contests, unnamed; none is chosen, and each would need the whole-body checks below before it is.

1. **The high-ball contest.** Play moves by high throws that hang 5-7 s, and possession goes to a clean catch under the ball. Teams read the arc, gather, hold position and time their leaps, choosing between a crouch jump with no tell (hands to 4.4 m) and a deeper full jump (5.1 m). A teammate's boost launches a flyer 1.75 m without the flyer's own push; combined with the flyer's jump it could go higher, which these screens do not model. The Moon gives it the hang, a crowd area three times Earth's, leaps that take the hands to 4-5 m, and contests slow enough to read. A viewer sees a ball climbing, a crowd converging and settling, and a leap and a catch high above. It maps onto game controls as positioning and leap timing on gravity's clock and the catch on the hands' clock. Earth's nearest kin are Australian football's marks, rugby's lineouts and basketball's rebounds.
2. **Passing against a defence that must commit.** The ball moves by passes, which a reacting defender cannot stop, so defenders commit to lanes before the ball moves, and the passer reads the commitments and fakes against them. The stance tells the pass: a chest pass of up to 10 m/s from the feet shows nothing, a split-stance throw of 15 m/s shows its step back, and a throw into a landing is timed by an arc everyone can see. It maps onto controls as anticipation, fakes and the choice of throw. Earth's nearest kin are handball, netball and ultimate. The screen found the passer safe against one defender, so the form stays open only with more defenders than that, which a game of several players a side would test.
3. **The two together.** Passing carries the ball safely, and a team chooses the moment to put it into the air, where the contest opens. The rhythm alternates quick reads with slow gatherings, which suits both players and viewers, and the commitment controls of the second form meet the gathering of the first.
4. **Bounding raids.** A chase-based loop of raids across a wide floor, where turns take room and cuts are tells. The screen suggests it is the weakest of the four: evasion gives the pursuer reads, and chases are slow.

## Moving by obstacles (8 November)

A paintball arena is built of obstacles, and on the Moon obstacles are the fast way to move. The floor's grip caps a stance's change of velocity at about μ g on average, so a cut at speed is a visible jump. A foot or a hand pushing on a wall or a box presses into it, so its grip grows with the push. [surfaces.py](surfaces.py) times the moves that use surfaces and routes a player across an arena with them. [parkour.py](parkour.py) writes those moves as units in the athletic vocabulary's form ([vocabulary/parkour_units.json](vocabulary/parkour_units.json), product [results/parkour.json](results/parkour.json)) and joins them to the athletic units in one graph through the ready crouch. Two postures join the athletic ones: hanging from a ledge or bar, and low behind cover. Figures are for the dance body at a grip of 0.8, Earth's in brackets.

**Walls make the sharp turns.** Running at 3 m/s and meeting a wall 45° from its normal, a foot's push changes the velocity by 5.3 m/s in 0.38 s and turns the travel 101°, or up to 6.7 m/s and 168°. One floor stance can change it by 0.43 m/s, and the floor's cut through the same turn needs a 2.1 m bounce and 6.8 s. The wall gives twelve times a stance's change on the Moon, against twice on Earth, because the stance scales with g and the push does not. Reversing at 4 m/s toward a goal behind the runner takes 2.5 s off a wall against 7.0 s on the floor (Earth 2.2 against 2.0 s).

**Walls also lift.** Running into a wall at 3 m/s and kicking off it throws the body 7.5 m up, the hands to 9.7 m (Earth 1.2 m and 3.4 m, close to what parkour runners reach). Stepping up and staying at the wall lifts it 1.8 m (Earth 0.3 m). A flat wall throws a runner off, but a curved one holds a run round it once friction on its push carries the weight. On a 4.7 m radius that needs 3.1 m/s on the Moon, inside the legs' range, and 7.6 m/s on Earth, beyond it. Minetti and colleagues (2024) had runners at simulated lunar gravity hold 5.4-6.5 m/s on such a wall.

**Ledges and drops: catching is easy and stopping is slow.** The arms catch a descent of up to 2.8 m/s on a ledge (Earth 1.7) and throw the body up over it in a fifth of a second. The body then coasts to rest on gravity's clock, 1.5 s in all (1.0). A 3 m drop lands like a 0.5 m drop on Earth: 1.4 Earth body weights in the legs, 0.5 with a roll (Earth 8.5 and 3.1). A precision jump can stop on a 0.2 m edge across a gap of up to 5.8 m (1.3 m). A bar caught at 3 m/s releases the body into a 1.9 s flight that lands 5.4 m beyond it, but spins it 308° on the way unless its shape slows it (Earth 51°), a hazard for a game's landings.

**Into cover.** Dropping into a slide takes 0.83 s, since the sink runs on gravity's clock. A slide from 3 m/s then runs 4.7 m for 3.8 s (Earth 0.36 m), carrying a player 7 m into cover low the whole way. With the hands on cover the arms pull the body down 0.5 m in 0.36 s, half the 0.83 s its weight alone takes; on Earth the pull saves 0.08 s. Rising back into the crouch takes 0.75 s.

**Vaults keep the run.** Over a box 1 m high and 1.5 m long at 3 m/s, a speed vault flies 1.5 s and keeps the speed (Earth 0.8 s). A kong vault's hands push forward and send the body on 1.4 m/s faster, landing 3.5 m beyond the box. A lunar runner clears a 1.2 m box that the model's Earth runner cannot. That limit on Earth, where trained runners do vault such boxes, suggests the model understates the arms' push.

**Cover to cover.** Across two arenas, a route search over up to three surface moves compares three ways of crossing: around the boxes on the floor, through them with the moves, and an open floor with no boxes.
- **Detours cost more on the Moon,** 3.7-4.4 s against 1.6-3.3 s on Earth, because corners are slow there.
- **A 1 m barrier across the way:** a kong vault recovers nearly all the detour, crossing in 5.0 s against 9.3 s round it and 4.9 s on an open floor (Earth 2.95, 4.6 and 3.0 s).
- **A 3 m wall:** climbing over is slower on the Moon than running round it, because the climb's stop and the drop both run on gravity's clock.
- **Wall pushes:** in an arena of bunkers, a low box, a 3 m wall and a bar, no surface move gained on the Moon, whose route has no sharp turn at speed. Two wall pushes saved 1.3 s on Earth.

Near cover a lunar runner is still slow: reaching 3 m/s takes 2.3 s. The search is a heuristic over few moves, so its times can be off by tenths of a second.

**For a game.** A lunar arena's obstacles do the work an Earth floor does.
- **Walls** turn and lift players sharply.
- **Low boxes** let a run keep its speed.
- **Curved walls** carry a run round them.
- **Cover** is reached by a long slide or a pull-down.

The moves' commitments are in the units. Wall runs, drops, bar releases and vault flights hold the player to an arc for one to three seconds, and the [projectiles study](projectiles/README.md) shows a player in the air is a sure hit. What the screens leave out:
- the reach engine has no hanging posture;
- the moves are screens of single bodies;
- no contest yet uses surfaces;
- the arms' pull (1.5 Earth body weights), the legs' yield and the walls' grip are assumptions (surfaces.py, [sources.json](sources.json)).
