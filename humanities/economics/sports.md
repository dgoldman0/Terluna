# Professional lunar sports as an early export industry

12 November 2026. This study asks how a small lunar settlement could sustain human
professional sport by selling entertainment to audiences elsewhere. It develops
the economics for Terluna researchers who know the proposed lunar environment but
need an explicit account of audiences, clubs, costs and trade. The contribution is
conditional competition accounts and a programme for testing demand. The candidate
sports and their commercial audiences remain to be established.

A resident league can sell the same competition to far more people than can live
beside its arena. Earth audiences could number in the tens or hundreds of millions
while only dozens of athletes compete on the Moon. At an illustrative average of
7.5 U of earned annual lunar receipts per fan across viewing, memberships, royalties
and sponsorship, ten million fans support 75 million U of exports, one hundred
million support 750 million U, and five hundred million support 3.75 billion U.
These are explicit scales of opportunity, with costs and ownership traced below;
the study does not assign them probabilities or infer demand from population alone.

The physical programme can include several sports and visiting clubs. A worked
four-competition circuit registers 32 club or athlete organizations and 256
athletes, of whom only 32 live on the Moon throughout the year. With 40 other
resident workers, two venues and 200 guest beds, it accommodates two annual visits
by each of the 224 visiting athletes and 32 visiting support workers. Its assumed
80 million distinct fans generate 600 million U of annual exports. The separate
one-million-paying-audience case finds the operating threshold of a smaller,
entirely resident league; it does not set the possible scale of Earth support.

## The commercial product and its physical basis

Human competition gives viewers uncertain outcomes, recognizable people and skills
worth following. A continuing season adds standings, rivalries, improvement and
athlete careers. These are proposed mechanisms for retaining an audience after
the first-event novelty fades. Their effectiveness depends on the actual play,
how well a broadcast communicates it, and whether people return voluntarily.

The [sport mechanics study](../sport/README.md) finds a useful combination of slow
centre-of-mass travel and fast hand and limb movement. One modeled 55 kg athlete
rises 2.86 metres and stays airborne for 3.75 seconds in a standing jump. That
creates time for catches and changes of body shape, while the flight path remains
largely committed at take-off. These are numerical screens with specified bodies,
floor grip and air; they establish neither safe participation nor spectator appeal.

The newer [projectile study](../sport/projectiles/README.md) also changes the
commercial reading of parkour and paintball. Its idealized shooter can lead a
predictable airborne target, and reactive escape at ordinary arena distances is
limited. Cover, routes, timing and opportunities to push against obstacles would
therefore have to sustain the contest. Extended jumps alone do not establish an
entertaining game. The aerial ball, obstacle and precision disciplines remain
candidates; the financial model chooses none of them and does not revive rejected
sport designs as accepted programmes.

An enclosed, breathable hall can support early development before planetary air
and open seas exist. Its pressure systems, clearances, surfaces and emergency
provision need their own design. Surfing, outdoor aerial sport and landscape-scale
events belong to later environmental stages. No revenue from those later
conditions is needed for the resident-league case.

## A large fan economy around a small lunar league

The addressable audience is the population able and willing to follow the sport,
including Earth residents who could never travel to its arena. Four teams could
represent fan communities far larger than their host settlement. Fans can support
athletes, follow club histories and treat the league as part of humanity's presence
on the Moon. Such attachment could support memberships, purchases and patronage
alongside the viewing product. Its strength and persistence are commercial
hypotheses to test; the settlement's headcount supplies no ceiling on them.

The support scenarios distinguish several transactions. Twenty percent of fans
buy viewing at the reference 20 U annual net rights yield; 5% buy additional
membership services yielding 40 U each. Members are a subset of paid viewers, so
the model does not count them as extra unique fans. Membership pays for additional
club access, programming or community services; merely renaming an included
subscription benefit would create no additional revenue. Net game and merchandise
royalties average 1 U per fan across buyers and non-buyers, and separate sponsor
services average 0.5 U. Together these produce 7.5 U annually per fan reaching the
lunar producers. These are modest individual amounts whose aggregate becomes
large when the audience is large; their feasibility still depends on price,
participation and the services actually delivered.

| Conditional fan following | Paid viewing equivalents | Annual earned exports | Dedicated capital | Annual cash before debt |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 million fans | 2 million | 75 million U | 60 million U | 14.168 million U |
| 100 million fans | 20 million | 750 million U | 180 million U | 223.568 million U |
| 500 million fans | 100 million | 3.75 billion U | 300 million U | 1.170768 billion U |

Each case keeps the same 51-match season and resident workforce, buys commercial
and production services equal to 35% of remote receipts, pays employees at least
20% of those receipts, and separately pays audience replacement, host services,
venue operations, event costs and renewal. Much of the expanded production and
distribution workforce can therefore work on Earth through the purchased services.
The 100-million-fan case allocates 150 million U annually to resident compensation;
large fan support is shared with participants rather than assuming unchanged
athlete wages as the property becomes valuable. Further hiring, a larger season
or more expensive rights could change the margins. The model establishes an
economic possibility at a stated output, not proof that this output can hold such
an audience indefinitely.

Fans can also supply money for reasons beyond a purchased service. If 1% of these
fans make annual gifts of 20 U, recurring patron transfers are 2 million, 20 million
or 100 million U respectively. Those transfers can sustain a programme or subsidize
access but remain outside earned revenue and value added. If 0.2% make a one-time
investment of 250 U, the corresponding capital is 5 million, 50 million or
250 million U. An equity investment carries ownership and risk; a loan requires
repayment. Prepaid memberships instead commit future services and cannot be counted
again as fresh cash when delivered. These are alternative funding arrangements to
be chosen and costed, not investor commitments or recurring operating income.

The commercial hypothesis is consequently broader than paid broadcasting alone.
An internationally followed league could finance local wages, suppliers and capital
from many small transactions and a smaller number of substantial patrons and
partners. Establishing that possibility does not require a large resident market.
It requires a product, institutions and relationships that Earth fans want to keep
supporting, and contracts that allow the people producing it on the Moon to share
in its success.

## Several sports, many clubs and visiting athletes

A lunar sporting industry need not depend on one sport or on a roster born or
permanently resident on the Moon. Earth-based clubs could recruit and train at
home, maintain their own fan communities, and travel for lunar competition blocks.
Resident athletes and staff could provide continuity, instruction and venue
knowledge between those visits. A visiting athlete still needs lunar adaptation
and physical qualification; training on Earth and in a digital game does not
establish readiness for the lunar event.

Four families give the programme different forms of skill and audience appeal:

| Candidate family | Competitive attraction | Development needed |
|---|---|---|
| Aerial team ball sport | Passing, positioning, contested catches and club rivalries | A complete playable contest, shared-air separation and whole-body trials |
| Obstacle and movement team competitions | Routes, cover, timing and coordinated movement, including a paintball-like candidate | An arena whose decisions remain interesting despite predictable flights; qualified equipment and landings |
| Martial arts and paired contests | Timing, balance, grip, controlled contact and individual athlete followings | Rules and protective arrangements developed for reduced traction and persistent angular momentum; Earth practices cannot simply be declared safe |
| Aerial figures, precision and timed individual events | Readable feats, repeatable records and individual careers | Judging or timing standards, equipment and a suitable event format |

These are research families, not four adopted sports. Team clubs, martial-arts
organizations and individual competitors can have different actual rosters and
competition calendars. The quantitative circuit uses a common eight-person roster
and eight organizations per competition solely to cost a programme with many
participants. Each organization keeps one athlete resident and brings seven
visitors for two 56-night blocks annually. Eight visiting production/support
workers accompany each competition. Two competitions run concurrently; four
successive paired blocks occupy 224 programme days.

The resulting **32 organizations and 256 registered athletes** require **72 resident
workers**, **512 visitor arrivals** and **28,672 guest-nights annually**. At most
128 visitors are present in a paired block. Two hundred guest beds at a planned
50% annual occupancy provide 36,525 guest-nights, while the route is assumed to
allow 600 programme arrivals. Two arenas provide 4,800 hours annually; competition,
resident and visiting training, maintenance and community use require 4,264 hours.
The busiest competition block uses 330 of its 448 arena hours before allocating
the separately budgeted shared use. These screens fit the annual and block
allowances. A dated schedule, rest requirements and certified route and habitat
capacities remain to be established.

Multiple sports expand the kinds of audience attachment available. Their fans
also overlap. In the worked circuit, mutually exclusive cohorts include 25, 20,
15 and 10 million people following only one of the four competitions, plus ten
million following all four. Individual sport followings sum to 110 million, while
the actual audience is **80 million distinct fans**. A common subscription and
membership offering earns the same assumed 7.5 U per unique fan, giving **600 million
U annually**, rather than charging the shared followers four times. Separately
priced event purchases could add receipts when fans actually buy them.

The circuit adds **200 million U** of dedicated capital for two venues and the guest
beds. At a conditional return fare of **25,000 U per visiting person**, travel costs
**12.8 million U annually**; 5,000 U and 100,000 U fare cases bracket that allowance.
The reference pays **120 million U** of total compensation, with **40 million U to
residents and 80 million U to nonresident participants**, reflecting the 20% share
of remote receipts allocated to employee compensation. Production on the Moon
includes those visiting employees' work, while their pay is recorded as income
leaving the lunar economy. Their homes, club ownership and work on Earth require
separate accounts.

With those assumptions, the circuit generates **317.058 million U of direct gross
value added** and **149.647 million U of cash before debt annually**. Increasing the
return fare to 100,000 U reduces that cash to **118.927 million U**. The lower-fare
case leaves **157.839 million U**. These results show why a large offworld fan base
could pay for visiting athletes as well as resident ones. They do not establish
today's transport prices, physiological readiness, the assumed audience or a
qualified timetable. The circuit replaces the smaller league in this comparison;
its receipts are not added to that league's receipts.

## Building the sports industry

The early creative work can begin with little purchased equipment. An illustrative
small prototype uses **1–4 person-years** of design, programming, production and
community work, plus **50,000–200,000 U** of purchased tools and services. Valuing
that effort at the existing production-worker allowance of 120,000 U per
person-year gives **0.17–0.68 million U** in economic development cost. Deferred
founder pay or volunteer effort can lower immediate cash expenditure, while the
work still consumes time. A persuasive sport and lasting interest also require
judgement, iteration and favourable audience response; spending the budget cannot
ensure them.

Developing a full four-competition programme is a larger undertaking. The quick
[build-out calculation](results/buildout.json) allows **5.5–17 million U** for
discovery, playable products, event formats, production tools, rights arrangements
and community development, followed by **4.0–11.9 million U** for physical trials
and commissioning. These stages contain **43–129 person-years** altogether, valued
at the same compensation proxy. The trial allowance includes sixteen people
making two 28-night visits, priced at the existing scenario's return fares and
host charges. Equipment rentals, consumables and specialist services are included;
permanent assets already in the venue budget are counted there once. The small
prototype above is part of discovery, not an extra charge.

For the **32-organization visiting circuit**, the resulting full programme uses are:

| Development or funding use | Lower illustrative case, million U | Higher illustrative case, million U |
|---|---:|---:|
| Creative development and physical pilots | 9.5 | 28.9 |
| Two venues and 200 guest beds, inherited installed allowance | 200.0 | 200.0 |
| Asset contingency, assumed 20–40% | 40.0 | 80.0 |
| Six to twelve months of operations without receipts, at base compensation | 34.9 | 69.8 |
| Separately funded renewal reserve for the same period | 5.0 | 10.0 |
| Initial paid promotion while building the target audience | 12.8 | 128.0 |
| **Gross uses before financing costs or early receipts** | **302.2** | **516.8** |

These are planning combinations, not confidence limits or a requirement to spend
the whole amount before the first event. The 200 million U remains an unquoted
installed-capital allowance. Suitable shared halls, modular obstacles, mats,
camera and scoring systems can make the incremental equipment relatively modest;
constructing pressurized space, guest accommodation or new host infrastructure is
a different cost. Leasing existing space substitutes rent and availability
obligations for ownership. This screen retains the existing dedicated facilities
and excludes construction of the host settlement. Freight and installation must
fit the installed allowance or be added when priced; contingency cannot establish
the adequacy of an unspecified engineering design.

Audience development is deliberately separated from purchasing promotion. The
circuit's target is sixteen million paying viewers. If paid promotion supplies
10%, 30% or 100% of them at an assumed 8 U per paid-sourced customer, the purchased
promotion costs **12.8, 38.4 or 128 million U**. The remainder arrive through the
programmes, players, recommendations or existing communities, whose creative and
community work is already budgeted. This creates effective initial promotion
costs of 0.8, 2.4 or 8 U per paying viewer; neither the paid share nor successful
organic growth has been measured. The annual accounts retain their original
8 U acquisition assumption until evidence warrants changing it. Large fan support
can therefore arise without buying every relationship, while its development
still has real costs and an uncertain outcome.

The operating allowance funds the full programme's minimum payroll and physical
services for six to twelve months without receipts, before revenue-linked pay
increases. Initial promotion is counted separately, and a renewal reserve funds
the existing replacement allowance once. No second generic working-capital
percentage, the earlier small-venue launch budget, or the value of the audience
is added. With the inherited illustrative 20 million U loan, construction interest,
issuance fees and a debt reserve add about **2.2–3.7 million U** of uses across the
specified draw schedules; debt is a funding source, not a reduction in economic
cost. A dated payment and revenue schedule is needed to determine peak outside
funding. [Project-finance guidance](https://ppp.worldbank.org/financing/issues-in-project-financed-transactions)
likewise treats revenue certainty and construction-cost sensitivity as separate
questions.

This supports gradual commitment: inexpensive prototypes and early programmes
can establish whether people return, followed by physical trials and contracted
seasons as the evidence improves. Shared tools and existing work should reduce
the incremental budget where they actually substitute for new development.
Successful early receipts could fund later expansion; the totals above give no
credit for such receipts and assign no probability to success. The scale of Earth
support remains independent of the size of the initial creative team.

### Spectators, accommodation and the visitor economy

Fans who travel to watch a competition could support hotels, guest apartments,
restaurants, shops, guides and local transport as well as the event itself. The
existing [early-tourism account](../../research/studies/world_economy/early_exports.md)
already budgets visitor accommodation, food and destination activities, their
capital and operations. Its first milestone has 100,000 residents, so its visitor
totals cannot be transferred to this 500-resident host. The
[earlier small-venue account](../../research/studies/world_economy/sports_finance.md)
also sells public visitor packages including accommodation, training and sport.
Neither account estimates the additional spectator demand induced by this larger
professional circuit.

The present circuit pays for its visiting athletes and support staff. Its
200 guest beds and host-service purchases provide that workforce's accommodation;
**paying spectator trips, hospitality sales and admission receipts are absent from
its annual totals**. Any shared beds would need a schedule combining workers,
fans and other guests. Additional hotels or guest apartments would require their
own construction, utilities, staff, supplies, renewal and operating funds beyond
the build-out screen above.

The economic connection should allocate each purchase to its supplier:

| Fan purchase | Account receiving the income |
|---|---|
| Event admission or a separately priced club experience | Event or club receipts, with the cost of serving spectators |
| Accommodation, meals, tours and local journeys | Hospitality and local-service suppliers, including any new capacity they must fund |
| Merchandise | Retail and production accounts, with imported goods and any league royalty separated |
| Earth–Moon travel | The transport operator's account; lunar transport income depends on who supplies the service |

A package containing a hotel stay and admission must allocate its receipts between
those services before combining the accounts. The
[existing tourism accounting method](../../research/studies/world_economy/tourism.py)
likewise treats visitor services as activity within the economy. Hotels' gross
sales, their purchases from suppliers and the suppliers' sales cannot all be
added as new value. Estimating the benefit attributable to a league also requires
distinguishing new trips and longer stays from spending by people who would have
visited anyway, and subtracting activity displaced when beds or routes fill.
Spectator tourism is therefore a plausible additional source of income whose
demand, accommodation budget and contribution remain unestimated here.

## From a venue business to clubs and a season

The unchanged [earlier venue account](../../research/studies/world_economy/sports_finance.md)
assumes 100 guest beds, twenty resident performers and production workers, 1,000
visits, 20 million U of media receipts and 5 million U of sponsorship annually.
Its 39 million U receipts and 7.12 million U cash before debt reproduce from the
pinned product. Its host settlement and transport fares remain outside its capital
account; its preliminary participant-hour capacity is not a qualified sport schedule.

The new model replaces that business boundary with four resident clubs, one shared
arena and an annual competition. It removes tourist receipts and guest-bed capital,
adds explicit payroll, customer acquisition, club transfers and imported inputs,
and substitutes a new 60 million U incremental capital allowance. Both cases retain
U as their real accounting unit and assume an existing host. They are alternatives,
so their revenues and capital should not be summed or interpreted as a measured
cost saving from the redesign.

| Reference operating assumption | Quantity and consequence |
|---|---|
| Clubs and players | Four clubs, eight athletes each; 32 resident athletes |
| Other resident workers | Sixteen production/commercial workers and eight officials, medical, coaching and administrative workers; 56 workers total, 11.2% of the assumed 500 residents |
| Competition | Each pair meets eight times: 48 regular matches plus three playoff matches, 51 broadcasts a year |
| Arena calendar | 204 competition/setup hours, 1,200 training hours, 120 maintenance hours and 400 community hours; 1,924 of 2,400 available hours annually |
| Pay | 150,000 U per athlete, 120,000 U per production worker and 140,000 U per other worker annually; total 7.84 million U |
| Host services | 100,000 U per worker annually for accommodation, utilities and shared services; total 5.6 million U |
| Dedicated capital | 60 million U, with a twenty-year blended renewal life; 3 million U replacement annually |

This is one co-located league: clubs need not each have a separate town or arena.
Earth fan communities could identify with clubs through players, style or history.
A larger league could later develop regional homes, but additional teams create
payroll, training, medical and accommodation obligations before they necessarily
create additional demand. A four-club league also has little competitive depth:
injury, retirement or an unbalanced roster could impair much of the programme.

The calendar reserves real arena time rather than multiplying a speculative number
of simultaneous participants by every hour. Eight-person training groups and the
workforce mix remain design assumptions. Rest schedules, coaches, clinical cover,
rescue, communications redundancy, household needs and the host's spare capacity
still require a qualified operating plan. Their allocated costs cannot prove that
the assumed staffing or services are sufficient. Passenger replacement and freight
routes also remain unpriced beyond the service allowances.

## Audience economics and earned receipts

Let A be annual average paying audience equivalents and y the annual receipts per
equivalent reaching the lunar rights owner. Media receipts are Ay. For a direct
paid product, y = 20 U could arise from 40 U of consumer spending with half retained
by offworld distribution and payment services. A wholesale contract can imply the
same receipts without identifying that many individual subscribers. Distributor
consumer turnover is outside the lunar producer account.

The reference adds 4 million U of separate sponsorship, 1 million U of game and
merchandise royalties, and 0.1 million U of local admissions. Sponsorship must buy
services outside any advertising inventory already monetized through the rights
deal. Royalties count only the lunar license payment; Earth game-store sales and
merchandise manufacturing remain with their producers. Each contract needs a
defined owner, buyer, territory, deliverable, term and cancellation arrangement.

At an assumed 25% engagement rate and 20% paid conversion among engaged people,
one million annual paid equivalents requires twenty million people reached.
Retention of 75% requires replacing 250,000 customers each year; at 8 U per acquired
customer this costs 2 million U annually. Initial acquisition of the entire audience
requires a separate 8 million U commissioning allowance. These rates are explicit
commercial hypotheses; impressions, casual views and paying customer-years are
different observations.

An advertising-funded alternative needs another measurement. Six slots per viewer
hour, 70% fill, 20 U per thousand impressions and a 50% rights-owner share yield
0.042 U per viewer hour. Earning the same 20 million U then requires about 476 million
viewer hours annually. That is an alternative way to monetize the programme, not
an additional receipt to add to the subscription case. Free reach can exceed paid
reach considerably while earning less per person.

## Costs, value added and financing

The reference purchases 13.64 million U of intermediate services: host services
5.6 million U, facility operations 1.5 million U, event services 2.04 million U,
customer acquisition 2 million U and variable commercial services 2.5 million U.
The latter equals 10% of remote receipts. These purchases exclude the 7.84 million U
employee payroll. The [BEA accounting definition](https://www.bea.gov/help/faq/1197)
keeps compensation inside value added and deducts purchased intermediate inputs.

The model uses the following identities, with no inventory change or product taxes:

```text
direct gross value added = earned receipts - intermediate purchases
gross operating surplus = direct gross value added - employee compensation
net operating surplus = gross operating surplus - economic depreciation
cash before debt = net operating surplus - tax allowance
cash after debt = cash before debt - interest - principal repayment
```

Renewal equals depreciation in this steady annual account. The tax allowance is
20% of positive net operating surplus, without interest deductions or loss relief;
it is not a proposed lunar tax regime. A grant of 35% of media and sponsorship
receipts distributes 8.4 million U to clubs. Each club receives 2.1 million U and
pays 2 million U of athlete compensation and host services. The clubs together
retain 0.4 million U before tax, and the central organization retains 0.22 million U.
Their sum is the consolidated 0.62 million U net operating surplus. Altering the
grant redistributes that surplus and can bankrupt the centre even while the
combined account remains positive; it creates no new revenue.

| Annual case | Receipts, million U | Direct gross value added, million U | Cash before debt, million U |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 250,000 paid equivalents; sponsors and royalties one quarter of reference | 6.35 | −3.915 | −14.755 |
| 500,000; sponsors and royalties halved | 12.60 | 1.210 | −9.630 |
| Reference: one million; 75% retention | 25.10 | 11.460 | 0.496 |
| One million; retention falls to 40% | 25.10 | 8.660 | −2.180 |
| Ten million; expanded commercial production, pay and capital | 230.10 | 143.460 | 97.424 |
| All remote receipts disappear; local admissions continue | 0.10 | −9.040 | −19.880 |

The ten-million case holds the same season and 56 workers, doubles pay and installed
capital, and raises variable commercial services from 10% to 25% of remote receipts.
Sponsorship is 20 million U and royalties 10 million U. Its added 60 million U of
capital needs separate funding. It shows how audience reach can increase income
without adding residents in proportion; its high surplus is conditional on these
cost assumptions. Competitive athlete pay, exclusivity fees, audience acquisition
or the production needed to retain that audience could absorb more of the receipts.

Holding sponsors and royalties at reference values, operating break-even needs
961,250 paid equivalents at 20 U owner yield. An illustrative 20 million U debt at
5% real interest over ten years costs 2.590 million U annually: paying it requires
1.164 million equivalents, and covering it 1.5 times requires 1.265 million. The
one-million reference consequently has a 2.094 million U annual gap after debt.
Its operating cash supports only about 2.55 million U of debt at that coverage,
rate and term. The assumed 20 million U debt is a stress comparison, not an
underwritten funding plan.

Capital, working cash, initial audience acquisition, half a year's debt reserve
and 2% issuance fees total 74.695 million U before host construction, transport,
construction interest and commissioning operating losses. Even if the illustrative
20 million U loan were available, 54.695 million U would need other funding.
Advances create future performance obligations; equity creates ownership claims;
debt creates repayment obligations. None is earned export revenue when raised.

Retention and owner yield matter together. At 75% retention, operating break-even
requires 2.197 million equivalents at 10 U yield, 0.961 million at 20 U, or
0.452 million at 40 U. Doubling host charges or surrendering half the contractual
rights receipts produces losses in the reference case. The product also tests half
the payroll with an extra million U of annual technical services; this isolates a
possible automation effect without asserting that its service or staffing quality
can actually be maintained. Automation can reduce production work while athletes,
medical responsibility, artistic judgement and audience acquisition retain costs.

## What reaches the lunar economy

The reference's 25 million U exports are offset by 4.92 million U of direct imported
services, an assumed 2.18 million U of imports embodied in domestic suppliers, and
2.1 million U of imported replacement assets. That leaves 15.8 million U in the
programme's trade account after replacement. Paying its assumed foreign debt leaves
13.210 million U of external cash before household imports and other settlement
requirements. For a 500-person host this is about 26,420 U per resident annually,
as a scale comparison only. It is neither a payment to each resident nor the
operator's disposable profit. Local workers and suppliers receive much of it,
and the operator still has the cash shortfall described above.

Direct value added excludes suppliers' own value added; counting all supplier
sales again would double-count intermediate production. The upstream import
allowance is a sensitivity assumption rather than a complete supply-chain model.
No spending multiplier is applied. Household imports, capacity displaced from
other work, environmental services and construction-period imports remain outside
this partial trade account. A settlement's dependence on one league also creates
concentration risk: losing its audience could simultaneously cut wages, supplier
orders and external receipts.

Contractual rights capture and equity ownership have different effects. A lunar
producer paid only a location fee records less revenue than one licensing its own
programme. Foreign shareholders in a lunar producer do not automatically relocate
its production: their profits affect resident income. The model records foreign
interest and both distributed and retained foreign equity earnings, including the
assumed share of losses, separately from dividends and debt principal. Its income
memo remains a narrow producer account, not a complete lunar national account.

At the wider economy's mature scale, sport belongs within existing leisure,
information, equipment and transport production. The older reference allocates
about 104 trillion U annually to leisure in an 839.2 trillion U lunar economy.
Assigning 1% of that leisure value added to sport gives 1.04 trillion U *within*
the allocation. It establishes neither professional-league turnover nor new
exports. Domestic recreation, facilities and instruction could become large with
billions of residents even if remote league demand remained modest.

## Digital development and a staged route to a league

A playable adaptation can give Earth audiences access to the sport's decisions
before lunar travel is available. Its movement should retain the physical
commitments, landings and interactions that make the lunar activity distinctive,
while controls make them usable. Game purchases, continuing services and licensing
have separate accounts from competitive esports and from the human league.
Players need not become spectators, and spectators need not buy a game.

An Earth studio's pre-settlement earnings are Earth production. A transfer of those
earnings into lunar facilities is financing, while a later payment for lunar-owned
services or rights can be an export. Brands, recordings, software and contracts can
support a business; no exclusive ownership of gravity or an entire sporting idea
is presumed. Shared rules, player representation, club participation and the
allocation of rights require institutional choices, with creator and athlete
compensation inside the business model.

Live transmission need not move spectators, but communication is not instantaneous.
[NASA's stated Earth–Moon light-time](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240007214/downloads/NDE%20Needs%20to%20Enable%20Deep%20Space%20Exploration.pdf)
is about 1.28 seconds one way before network delays. That permits delayed live
viewing; a rapid competitive game split between Earth and lunar players would
need a different arrangement. Regional game servers, replay, asynchronous
challenges and separately located competitions are plausible designs. Broadcast
continuity, local recording and the timing of interactive features require tests.

The proposed development sequence ties each larger commitment to evidence:

| Stage | Work and observation | Result that changes the decision |
|---|---|---|
| Playable sport candidates | Compare repeat sessions, tactical diversity, understandable scoring and spectator return against alternative games; preserve failures | Reject a candidate whose appeal disappears after novelty or depends on physically impossible movement |
| Commercial digital property | Measure paid conversion, cohort retention, acquisition cost, refunds and contribution after platforms and production | Proceed only if repeat cohorts support the actual cost of the product; digital success alone does not authorize league revenue assumptions |
| Physical qualification and broadcast pilot | Qualified motion, venue and health trials; a complete staffing/calendar budget; ordinary broadcast footage tested for comprehension | Revise the sport or production if safe play cannot deliver the appeal found digitally |
| Contracted resident season | Price host services and delivery, negotiate rights and sponsor obligations, test buyer concentration and cancellation terms | Commit facility capital against fundable downside cash, not estimated audience reach |
| Additional clubs or venues | Measure incremental retained viewers and commercial receipts against new residents, training and capital | Expand when the additional competition earns or deliberately subsidizes its full incremental cost |

These are proposed tests, not completed trials. The model supplies an explicit
benchmark for the commercial experiments: at the reference yield, 100 million U
of annual external receipts needs 4.75 million paid equivalents after holding
sponsors and royalties at 5 million U; one billion U needs 49.75 million. These
are revenue requirements, not profit projections. They show why an exceptionally
successful league could matter enormously to a small settlement, and identify
the audience achievement that such a claim requires.

The economic opportunity begins with people doing something worth watching in a
place that changes what their bodies can do. Resident competition can turn that
activity into a recurring export before large-scale settlement or tourism. Its
commercial development therefore deserves its own research programme alongside
materials and manufacturing: one that develops the sport, measures lasting
audience attachment, and preserves enough of the resulting income to sustain the
people and facilities that make it possible.

## Reproduction and evidence

[sports.py](sports.py) calculates the accounts from [sports_inputs.json](sports_inputs.json)
through [accounts.py](accounts.py). [results/sports.json](results/sports.json)
records the scenarios, sensitivity grid, break-even thresholds, club distributions
and funding uses. Inputs bind the earlier finance and current physical products
by schema and SHA-256; a mismatch stops regeneration for review. No domain model
is imported from the research lane. The tests independently solve break-even by
bisection, amortize debt, and check consolidation, import boundaries, ownership,
capacity and product regeneration. [checks.json](checks.json) records actual
verification. Numerical consistency establishes the scenario's arithmetic;
commercial demand, safe play and facility adequacy remain empirical questions.
