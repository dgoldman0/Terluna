# Lunar life as a cultural export: entertainment, performance and creative production

12 November 2026. A small lunar population could support an entertainment industry
whose audience lives mainly on Earth. Sport supplies one recurring attraction;
ordinary life, performances, creative work and the process of building a community
could give other audiences reasons to follow the same place. This study develops
that wider opportunity for Terluna researchers, with a conditional production and
trade account rather than a forecast of tastes or settlement dates.

The central economic possibility is that many people can value access to the lives
and work of a few people far away. A lunar community need not sell a physical visit
to each interested person. It can sell programmes, performances, memberships,
licenses and commissions, while fans can also contribute gifts or investment. A
portfolio reaching 140 million distinct fans generates 1.202 billion U of annual
exports in the worked case below, with 104 resident workers and visiting athletes,
artists and production staff. Its audience, pricing and costs are assumptions;
the calculation makes their implications explicit.

## What people could follow

A viewer might first arrive for an athletic competition, then remain interested in
the athletes' practice, a visiting dancer's residency or a resident making something
for the community. Another viewer could follow a cook, a gardener or a construction
team without ever watching a match. These are different relationships with people
and activities, capable of supporting several programmes from one settlement.

The interest can develop as lunar life develops. Early programmes would show
enclosed homes and workplaces, journeys, preparation, low-gravity movement and
the practical work of maintaining a community. The Open Moon's later gardens,
waters, landscapes and regional cultures could extend that material enormously.
Those later environments depend on the domain research and environmental build;
they supply no early revenue in this model. The [daily-life research](../../research/studies/daily_life/README.md)
is a source of physical conditions and prospective human possibilities, not a
record of an inhabited lunar society.

| Cultural activity | Potential buyers and receipts | What must actually be produced |
|---|---|---|
| Everyday-life series, creator channels and documentaries | Viewers, distributors, sponsors and commissioning editors | People and continuing stories worth following; reliable capture, editing and participant agreements |
| Dance, acrobatics, music and theatrical performance | Remote audiences, patrons, concert distributors and licensed adaptations | Performances whose artistic quality sustains interest; rehearsals, staging, recording and fair performer compensation |
| Several sports and competitions | Fans, clubs, sponsors, broadcasters and game publishers | The human competition and visiting-team programme developed in the [sports study](sports.md) |
| Factual and educational entertainment | Schools, museums, families, publishers and commissioning institutions | Accurate programmes and interactive sessions, with research results distinguished from demonstrations and speculation |
| Films, photography, advertising and visiting creator residencies | Production companies, brands and rights buyers | Location services, people and footage whose value warrants the travel and production cost |
| Games, virtual visits and licensed worlds | Players, developers, museums and experience operators | Playable experiences, licensed assets and useful access; a virtual visitor is distinct from a paying physical traveler |
| Designs, craft, fashion, recordings and publishing | Collectors, publishers, manufacturers and individual buyers | Original cultural work, physical or licensed; royalties count separately from offworld manufacturing and retail sales |

The [dance programme](../dance/README.md) supplies a particularly concrete artistic
possibility: changes in gravity allow long flights containing fast bodily
articulation. Its numerical motions and garments inform prospective choreography,
while a performance still requires dancers' judgement, physical qualification and
an audience. Music and dramatic work could also become distinctly lunar through
people's experiences, choices and collaborations. They need not all depend on a
physical effect unavailable on Earth.

Film and advertising have a different commercial test. Simulated scenery,
computer-generated movement and terrestrial studios compete with location work.
An actual lunar production must offer something its buyer values enough to cover
the additional expense: a performer, an event, documentary authenticity, a
commissioned setting or a recognized cultural property. The model therefore
includes specific production commissions rather than assuming a fixed share of
the whole film industry moves to the Moon.

## Existing evidence and its limits

Digital distribution already permits individual creators and small organizations
to sell cultural work internationally. In its [January 2026 letter](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/the-future-of-youtube-2026/),
YouTube reports paying more than US$100 billion to creators, artists and media
companies over the preceding four years. That is a cumulative platform payout,
covering a wide range of producers. It establishes neither a typical creator's
earnings nor a lunar market share. It does show that substantial financial support
can reach distributed producers through digital media and several payment types.

Space programming already spans live coverage, series and documentaries.
[NASA's distribution description](https://www.nasa.gov/ways-to-watch/) offers those
formats through NASA+, which is free and advertising-free. This supports the range
of possible formats and also identifies competition: lunar producers would need
to earn attention and payment alongside freely available space material. The
reasons to pay could include an artist's work, a continuing story, a competition,
participation in a community or an institution's specific commissioned programme.

The [sports source register](sports_sources.json) documents sports-property and
games-market comparisons. They remain separate markets with overlapping buyers;
their global totals cannot be added into a lunar addressable market. The broader
creative-service figures located in UNCTAD search results are excluded because the
official summary and full report returned access errors. [entertainment_sources.json](entertainment_sources.json)
records the exact access and reading state. No published market size supplies the
audience values in the calculation.

## How cultural income crosses the Earth–Moon boundary

Remote audiences can pay an Earth platform which retains distribution revenue and
pays a lunar producer. The lunar receipt is an export of the delivered service or
licensed work. A game developed and sold on Earth remains Earth production; a
license fee paid to a lunar rights holder enters the lunar account once. Similarly,
an Earth manufacturer can make licensed clothing without the Moon shipping every
item. These arrangements let lunar creators participate in a large downstream
business while the account keeps only the receipts and production attributable
to them.

Rights ownership, production location and employee residence have separate
consequences. A visiting artist can perform on the Moon while keeping a home on
Earth. Under the model's employee arrangement, the artist's work contributes to
lunar production and their compensation is income paid abroad. Hiring an
independent offworld production company instead creates a purchased service.
Foreign owners of a lunar producer receive income, including retained earnings
where the modeled direct-investment treatment applies. These distinctions follow
the targeted [ECB balance-of-payments definitions](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/pubbydate/2025/html/ecb.bopiipbook202507.en.html);
actual contracts and residence would determine the classification.

Support also arrives through payments that buy no current output. Patron gifts
are transfers. Equity and loans finance activity and create claims; a customer
advance creates an obligation to deliver later. A prospective fan-funded production
could combine all of them, but its annual earnings account must distinguish them.
The worked portfolio's optional patron assumption is 1% of fans giving 20 U a
year, or 28 million U at 140 million fans. That support is shown separately from
the 1.202 billion U of earned exports.

## A shared production programme

The calculation starts with the committed [four-competition sports account](results/sports.json),
which has 80 million unique fans, 72 resident workers, visiting teams, two arenas
and 200 guest beds. It then adds 32 resident cultural-production workers and forty
visiting artists or producers, each making two 28-night visits annually. An
additional 30 million U of studio and production capital raises dedicated
investment from 200 million to 230 million U. The host settlement remains outside
that capital account.

| Additional annual programme | Output units | Studio hours | Arena and rehearsal hours |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Everyday-life programmes | 150 | 300 | 0 |
| Documentary productions | 12 | 240 | 0 |
| Performances | 24 | 0 | 288 |
| Interactive classes or sessions | 40 | 120 | 0 |
| Separately commissioned location productions | 8 | 240 | 0 |
| **Total** | **234** | **900** | **288** |

Output units are budgeted production assignments, not equal-length finished
programmes. The input file specifies capture costs, crew sizes and work hours
for each. Together they require 17,568 hours of additional crew work against
48,000 hours available from 32 workers at 1,500 productive hours each annually.
That allowance covers only the specified assignments. Creative development,
participant preparation and specialist requirements need verification against the
chosen programme; offworld editing and distribution are purchased separately.

The combined operation has **104 resident workers** within the assumed 500-person
host, **592 visitor arrivals** against a 600-arrival programme allowance, and
**30,912 guest-nights** against 36,525 available at the planned occupancy. A
conservative simultaneous-visitor screen gives **168 guests** against 200 beds.
Sport and performance use **4,552 of 4,800 annual arena hours**, while the added
studio uses 900 of its 2,400 available hours. These calculations preserve the
sports programme's community and maintenance allocations.

Shared facilities therefore help, but the remaining eight arrivals and 248 arena
hours are a narrow annual margin. A concert tour or film unit could require a
different venue, more freight, longer rehearsals or different dates. The study
does not treat one hall as simultaneously available to every genre, and its annual
and peak screens do not establish a qualified daily timetable. A higher output
commitment must carry the additional residents, visitors, beds and equipment it
needs.

## Audience overlap and the revenue account

The reference portfolio contains 140 million distinct people. Its disjoint
audience groups include forty million following only sport, thirty million only
ordinary-life programming, ten million only performance, and ten million only
factual programming. Three further groups follow sport with ordinary life,
performance or factual programmes respectively: twenty, ten and ten million.
Another ten million follow the three non-sport genres together. The resulting
genre followings are eighty million for sport, sixty million for ordinary life,
and thirty million each for performance and factual work. They sum to 200 million
genre memberships but remain **140 million people**.

Twenty percent buy a viewing product. Its net annual receipt to the lunar producers
is assumed to be 25 U per paid equivalent, up from the sport product's 20 U.
Five percent buy additional membership services yielding 40 U each; those members
are already included among the paid viewers. The reference makes no automatic
retention gain from diversification: it retains the sports model's 75% annual
retention and 8 U acquisition cost for each replacement customer.

| Annual earned export | Million U | Boundary |
|---|---:|---|
| Portfolio viewing | 700 | 28 million paid equivalents at 25 U net yield |
| Additional membership services | 280 | Seven million members at 40 U; distinct benefits beyond viewing |
| Game, merchandise and archive royalties | 140 | Average 1 U per unique fan; excludes licensees' sales |
| Separate sponsor services | 70 | Average 0.5 U per fan; excludes inventory already sold in viewing agreements |
| Production commissions | 12 | Separate contracted work and rights, excluded from the bundle's receipts |
| **Total exports** | **1,202** | Replaces the sports-only bundle |

Local admissions add 0.1 million U. The 600 million U sports export is already
represented within this portfolio, so the **increment is 602 million U**, not
1.202 billion U plus another 600 million U. Part of that increment requires sixty
million additional fans and part requires a higher viewing yield. These are
separate propositions to test; a richer catalogue could succeed at one while
failing at the other.

The royalty allowance includes games and catalogues rather than adding their
entire sales again. A documentary, motion recording or performance could earn
later licenses, but usable rights, creator participation, preservation costs,
demand decay and the commercial value of older material determine that return.
No perpetual income, asset resale multiple or automatic increase in property
values is included.

## Production, income and cash

The model consolidates the lunar cultural producers. Existing sports facility,
travel and host purchases remain; portfolio commercial and audience-acquisition
costs are calculated once. Added capture purchases total 5.5 million U annually,
with 3.2 million U of additional resident host services, 0.672 million U of guest
services, 2 million U of return travel and 1 million U of studio operations.
These fixed capture and site costs are distinct from employee compensation and
the variable postproduction, commercial and delivery services.

Variable services cost 35% of remote receipts. Employee compensation is the
greater of the specified base staffing cost or 20% of remote receipts; the latter
gives **240.4 million U annually** in the reference. It is allocated between
resident and visiting employees in the base compensation proportions. Annual
customer replacement costs **56 million U**, and capital renewal **11.5 million U**.
After all intermediate purchases, the portfolio generates **671.986 million U of
direct gross value added**. Compensation, renewal and the 20% positive-surplus
tax allowance leave **336.069 million U before debt**.

| Conditional annual case | Exports, million U | Direct gross value added, million U | Cash before debt, million U | Cash change from sports alone, million U |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Existing sports control | 600.0 | 317.058 | 149.647 | 0 |
| Expanded portfolio, 140 million fans | 1,202.0 | 671.986 | 336.069 | 186.422 |
| Extra programmes, same 80 million fans and viewing price | 612.0 | 312.486 | 142.869 | −6.778 |
| Expanded reach, paid conversion falls from 20% to 10% | 852.0 | 472.486 | 232.469 | 82.822 |
| Audience one quarter as large; costs renegotiated to the scenario | 309.5 | 133.861 | 48.369 | −101.278 |
| Shared revenue shock: receipts halve, full reference payroll retained | 601.0 | 281.336 | 23.549 | −126.098 |

The same-audience case is a useful control: the new programmes earn 12 million U
of commissions but attract no new fans and secure no viewing-price increase.
They reduce annual cash by 6.778 million U because their costs exceed the added
contribution. This does not negate their artistic or community value. It identifies
the amount that other earnings, patrons or public cultural provision would have
to justify if those benefits were the reason to produce them.

The revenue-shock case preserves the reference payroll and the reference annual
customer-replacement budget, while variable commercial costs fall with receipts.
Several genres can fail together through a distributor problem, damaged audience
trust or a common interruption. Genre diversity alone does not remove dependence
on the same host, communications or buyers. The quarter-audience case instead
allows the stipulated revenue-linked compensation adjustment; it is a different
operating scale, not an instant response to lost revenue. Neither case has an
assigned probability, and no reserves or patron gifts are assumed to cure it.

The reference programme leaves **567.733 million U of external cash annually**
after the specified direct and upstream imports, imported replacement, nonresident
employee pay, the inherited 20 million U debt's first-year service and foreign
dividends. This is a partial economic-flow account, not the producer's profit or
a complete settlement balance. It excludes household imports, other settlement
services, initial construction imports and the income allocation among suppliers.
Reinvested foreign earnings remain distinct from dividends paid in cash. These
qualifications matter because an export industry can earn substantial foreign
receipts while much of the benefit reaches workers, suppliers or offworld owners.

Initial funding also differs from the annual account. Acquiring the full modeled
28 million paid audience at 8 U per customer requires **224 million U** before the
steady replacement assumption is applicable. The studio adds **30 million U** of
capital, and launch losses and working capital require a dated development budget.
The cash tables do not establish that a new company begins with those customers
or can borrow against a hypothetical stabilized year. Digital releases,
commissioning advances, patrons and fan investment could help build toward that
state; each needs its own timing and obligations.

## Institutions that let a community earn from its culture

Many independent creators, clubs and organizations could share cameras, studios,
transmission and visiting accommodation without sharing a single editorial voice.
Cooperatives, private studios, public institutions and mixed arrangements have
different distributions of control and income. A common facility account can make
its services available to them, while contracts identify who owns each recording,
which performances are included in a bundle and who receives later royalties.
This avoids assuming that one company owns the whole culture of a settlement.

Ordinary life creates a particular production constraint: a community's residents
have lives beyond the programme. Voluntary participation, agreed filming places
and hours, payment for creative work, and room to withdraw from future production
support a durable relationship between creators and neighbours. A family's day,
a scientist's work and a public performance need different arrangements. The
model pays a dedicated workforce; it does not assume that every resident provides
unlimited free content or that every workplace is a permanent set.

Authenticity can be commercially valuable when viewers know what they are seeing.
Actual footage, staged performances, documentary interpretation and fictional or
simulated scenes can each have a market. They should retain those identities.
The project's physical products can inform games and future-world imagery while
their research limitations remain visible wherever an empirical claim is made.
That distinction preserves both artistic freedom and the value of access to real
events and people.

Tourism can later complement these relationships. A fan might buy a trip to see
a competition or take a class, but transport and guest capacity then constrain
that sale. This study reserves its guest capacity for production visitors and
includes no general tourist receipts. Licensed Earth exhibitions, virtual access
and remote learning can reach additional buyers without consuming those beds.
Their local production is assigned to the place that supplies it, with lunar
license and service payments recorded separately.

## Development and economic significance

An initial digital programme can begin with terrestrial production: physically
grounded games, speculative art clearly presented as such, interviews and
documented research. Those activities can earn money and establish an audience
before a lunar settlement exists, with income attributed to the producers' actual
locations. Subsequent lunar visits can produce commissioned work and limited
events. A resident community can then sustain recurring programmes and host
visiting artists and teams, while later environmental development permits new
forms of outdoor life and culture.

The immediate research programme should compare distinct audience responses.
Trials of sports, daily-life stories, performances and factual work should measure
who returns, who pays, which memberships overlap, and whether a combined offering
improves retention or price enough to meet its added costs. Paid tests and buyer
contracts provide stronger evidence than launch views. Production trials should
record crew time, resident participation, usable footage, venue hours and rights
costs so that audience success can be matched to a deliverable programme.

The worked case demonstrates why cultural exports deserve attention beside
materials, engineering and other traded services. Millions of supporters can
contribute small individual amounts to work produced by a modest local population;
visiting participants and offworld production partners can widen that work further.
Under the stated assumptions the resulting receipts are large enough to support
significant local compensation, imported services and additional capital. They
do not finance the whole Open Moon, and they are not an increment to add to the
mature economy's existing leisure and information sectors. They identify a path
by which an early community could earn substantial income from the meaning,
pleasure and interest that people elsewhere find in lunar life.

## Reproduction and review

[entertainment.py](entertainment.py) reads [entertainment_inputs.json](entertainment_inputs.json)
and the sports product by versioned schema and exact SHA-256. It writes
[results/entertainment.json](results/entertainment.json) using the same annual
accounting functions as the sports study. The sports-only control reproduces the
prior product's production, compensation, income and cash values. Other checks
test audience overlap, incremental expenditure, capacity failures, patron transfers
and fixed payroll during a common revenue shock. The [check record](checks.json)
states the numerical and editorial activities performed, separately from audience
validation and production qualification.

```sh
python3 -m humanities.economics.entertainment
python3 -m pytest -q humanities/tests/test_entertainment_economics.py
```

The default output goes to ignored `research/runs/cultural_economics/`. Replacing
the reviewed product requires an explicit `--output` path. No full papers or
external commercial datasets are redistributed with this study.
