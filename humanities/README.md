# Humanities

The lane for how people live, make and move on the Open Moon where the work is an art or a practice rather than an
engineering system: dance first, then sport, and later music, the martial arts and acrobatics, and the crafts; cultural economics studies how these practices sustain livelihoods and earn export income. Its models follow
the repository's rules for a domain: physics and data from the other domains arrive as products (the shared gravity,
the design run's air, the habitation note's movement mechanics), and what this lane computes leaves as products of
its own. Rendering of its results lives in [visualization/dance](../visualization/dance/); the explorable world reads
its products through the immersion's bake when they mature.

| Subject | Holds |
|---|---|
| [dance](dance/README.md) | A dancer's body, the physics of dancing at lunar gravity in dense air, a movement vocabulary and grammar, a whole-body motion engine and the garments, toward flowing dances that only the Moon allows |
| [sport](sport/README.md) | What an athlete's body can do at lunar gravity against Earth, from the dance's body model and a larger scaled body; what the dance's solved phrase did in its landings and take-offs; a vocabulary of 24 athletic moves, where players can be and when, and four contests between players across the floor's grips, with candidate forms of play; toward one sport that works on the Moon and as an e-sport |

## Cultural economics

The [sports economics study](economics/sports.md) models large Earth fan followings,
several competitions, resident and visiting clubs, and the resulting lunar income.
A four-competition scenario has 32 organizations and 256 registered athletes, with
72 resident workers and 224 visiting athletes. Its 80-million-fan case earns
600 million U annually in exports, conditional on explicit audience and cost
assumptions. The study separates earned receipts, patron transfers, investment,
value added, resident income and cash; the physical research selects no sport.

The [entertainment study](economics/entertainment.md) extends that account to
everyday life, performance, factual programming, games and commissioned production.
Its shared-audience model compares the added costs with new fans and purchases,
while visiting artists, crew time and shared facilities remain explicit. A worked
140-million-fan case earns 1.202 billion U of annual exports, with 104 resident
workers; all audience and commercial quantities remain scenario assumptions.

Both economic studies now include a quick build-out screen: creative effort,
physical pilots, installed facilities, initial promotion and operating reserves.
The complete sports cases use 302-517 million U and the combined portfolio
359-679 million U before financing and early receipts; these retain the existing
facility allowances and are planning scenarios, not engineering quotations.

The cross-domain [complex returns study](../research/studies/complex_returns/README.md)
uses the current provisioning account to compare whole-programme funding,
incremental industry, capital-provider claims and socioeconomic benefits across
all stakeholders. Residents are one group; Earth customers, workers, funders and
research users also enter the appraisal.

The [staged financing extension](../research/studies/complex_returns/staged.md)
starts with a funded productive module and uses earned cash for later industrial
facilities. Worldwide sports, cultural, licensing and institutional demand develop
independently of resident population. Successful service scenarios can sustain
expansion; capital recovery still depends strongly on prices, margins, shared
infrastructure and commissioning, whose small-system engineering remains open.

The [integrated export-strategy comparison](../research/studies/complex_returns/strategies.md)
compares commodity, specialty and premium materials, propellant/logistics and
computing alongside worldwide services. It accounts for shared customer budgets,
production limits, qualification costs and earned-cash expansion, and distinguishes
combined enterprise returns from each industrial activity's contribution.

The [U price study](../research/studies/complex_returns/prices.md) retains U as
the established economic unit serving the planned off-Earth zone. It tests
domestic spending from actual distributions and expansion commitments, separate
from Earth purchases, against explicit basket and supply assumptions. Its
conditional price-level comparisons are not annual inflation forecasts.
